# Analysis Framework, Pitfalls, and Failure Cases

## Category

production / retro subtree. Node positioning: reducing failure cases back into constraints and signals — an analysis framework for four recurring patterns: premature optimization, unclear authority boundaries, capacity by gut feeling, and absent release rollback.

## Definition


Failure cases are the most expensive teaching material. The easiest mistake when reading someone else's failure is to read it as a story: remembering "they used some technology and it went down" without extracting the constraint. In fact the overwhelming majority of production disasters are not one specific technology chosen wrongly, but a small set of recurring patterns repeating themselves across different projects. Building an analysis framework that reduces a case back into constraints and signals is what makes the time spent on the case worth it.

## Problem

The easiest mistake when reading someone else's failure is to read it as a story: remembering "they used some technology and it went down" without extracting the constraint. Most production disasters are not one technology chosen wrongly but a small set of recurring patterns repeating across projects — build an analysis path, and the case repays the cost it incurred.


### A Failure Case's Value Lies in the Constraint, Not the Plot

The same "the server went down" story, read shallowly, is incident plot; read deeply, what comes out is constraints:

- Under what conditions this kind of crash happens, and whether your own project meets those conditions.
- Which step produced the earliest signal, and why nobody saw it.
- What kind of design would keep this failure from happening, or keep it survivable when it does.

Read this way, other people's cases become check items in your own project. The reverse reading — remembering only the conclusion — also carries a hidden harm: feeling safe because "we do not use that technology", while an isomorphic risk lies in your own project wearing a different technical shell.

### A Reusable Analysis Path

Whether the failure happened in someone else's project or your own, the most effective path usually has four steps:

- Reconstruct the timeline: from the first anomalous signal to the end of stop-bleeding, how long each step took.
- Find the violated constraint: confirm which constraint that should have held was violated by this failure — for example, a single authority, evidenced capacity, or changes being revertible.
- Separate the layers: what the technical root cause is and what the process root cause is; stopping at the technical layer usually fails to fix things completely.
- Convert into check items: how this constraint should be validated in advance, and who is responsible for checking it at which stage.

The step most easily skipped is the second one. Jumping straight from the symptom to "swap the component" or "add a lock" usually lets an isomorphic failure come back six months later in a different place.

### Recurring Pattern: Premature Optimization

The typical script of premature optimization: early in the project, the architecture is built heavy for an imagined scale — multi-layer caching, service decomposition, and complex sharding strategies all in place — while the actual online volume stays for a long time at a fraction of the estimate. A heavy architecture is not heavy for free; its communication cost, troubleshooting cost, and operating cost are paid every day, and team time is heavily consumed maintaining scalability nobody uses.

Signals of this kind of failure are worth watching for:

- The number of modules on the architecture diagram far exceeds what the team's headcount can maintain.
- Code written for a path that "might be useful later" that the current release never uses.
- Diagnosing one ordinary problem crosses four or more processes while the problem itself touches a single feature.

The reverse pattern is just as common: none of the scalability that should have been built got built, and the launch surge punches straight through the single point. The basis for judging heavy versus light is not preference, but the shape of the dominant load in the problem model, and which items on the irreversible-decision list become ten times more expensive if not changed now.

### Recurring Pattern: Unclear Authority Boundaries

Blurry authority boundaries are the most expensive kind of debt in long-cycle projects. Common forms include:

- The same data has two writers, and nobody knows which to trust when they disagree.
- Client-reported numbers are accepted directly by the server, and cheats and anomalous data march straight in.
- Derived data quietly became the source of truth over long evolution, and the source data is now too risky to touch.
- In-match temporary state and long-term assets intrude into each other, and settlement anomalies and asset errors contaminate each other.

The common trait of this pattern is that it is completely invisible day to day: the system runs, features work, and the data looks right. The problem erupts all at once during reconciliation, server merges, compensation, and dispute tracing, and by then a long time has usually passed since the boundary was drawn wrong, with the cost of untangling it multiplied several times over. When analyzing this kind of case, what is worth pressing on is not which line of code broke, but "was the authority-ownership agreement ever written down at the time, and who broke it".

### Recurring Pattern: Capacity by Gut Feeling

Capacity failure scripts are strikingly similar: the launch surge takes down login, or the database falls over on event night. Retrospectives almost always find that the capacity numbers behind the launch decision came either from an estimate of an estimate, or directly from a colleague's experienced gut. Common symptoms include:

- No one can state the conversion chain from DAU to concurrency to requests per second, and the numbers do not line up with each other.
- No measured single-machine numbers; the load test became a performance that produced a pretty peak.
- Only the average was calculated, not the peak shape, when game load peak shapes are usually much steeper than the mean.
- The scale-out plan stops at "add machines", with nobody verifying whether adding machines merely moves the bottleneck elsewhere.

What is special about capacity problems is that they are pure derivation work with no technical difficulty; the entire difficulty is whether the organization demands evidence. Whether a team is prone to capacity incidents can be judged roughly from whether it asks for numbers in reviews.

### Recurring Pattern: Missing Release and Rollback

Another class of repeatedly replayed failures concerns change management:

- Multiple systems must release in sync, with no ordering guarantee and coordination done by hand.
- The rollback plan stays verbal, and only during a real rollback is it discovered that the data structure is already incompatible.
- Configuration and data changes bypass the code release process, and when something breaks nobody can find what changed.
- The gray release scope is a formality, and a full release is the same as no gray release.

The lessons of this pattern are relatively easy to absorb, because its failures sit closest to the people who release. What is genuinely hard is holding the line long term: release discipline tends to loosen one release at a time under version pressure, until some incident pulls everyone back to the table.

### Why These Patterns Keep Recurring

The four patterns share common soil:

- Invisible day to day: their debt never affects current features and only comes due under pressure.
- Misplaced cost: whoever took the shortcut saved effort in the moment, while the cost is paid by future on-call staff and by every player.
- Silent luck: nothing going wrong gets read as "the original judgment was right", which reinforces the risk perception instead of correcting it.

Understanding this helps put effort in the right place: rather than blaming a specific decision, build mechanisms that keep these patterns from hiding — reviews that demand evidence, continuously updated capacity derivations, executable rollback drills.

### Turning a Case into Your Own Checklist

After reading a case, the more effective closing action is three questions:

- Does an isomorphic risk exist in your own project, just in a different shape.
- In which stage's checklist should this risk appear.
- Who is responsible for asking this question out loud at the next review.

Without these three actions, the more cases you read the more it becomes story consumption; with them, the team's risk checklist grows thicker with every other team's incident. Your own project's incident retrospectives work the same way, and the return is more direct: tuition you paid yourself should at least redeem into one line on the checklist.

### Common Pitfalls

- Consuming cases as stories, remembering only which technology was used and never extracting the constraint behind it.
- Stopping the retrospective at the technical root cause without asking why process and mechanism allowed it to happen.
- Concluding you are safe because "we do not use that technology", ignoring that an isomorphic risk has changed shells.
- Blaming individuals, so the first reaction to the next failure becomes concealment instead of reporting.
- Pushing an architecture redo after every failure instead of first restoring the most basic demand for evidence.
- A checklist that only grows without revision or review, accumulating a thick volume nobody opens again.

Read enough failure cases and a plain pattern appears: disasters rarely come from ignorance; they come from knowing the risk exists and having no mechanism to honor that knowledge. The point of an analysis framework is to turn knowledge into mechanism, so the constraints are still enforced when someone forgets.
