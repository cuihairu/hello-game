# Key Questions Checklist

## Category

production / retro subtree. Node positioning: a design review checklist — authority and consistency, capacity and performance, failure degradation and recovery, release rollback and compatibility, four groups, each question demanding evidence rather than a stance.

## Definition


Many people have checklists for reviews; very few of them actually work. The difference is usually not in coverage but in the quality of the questions: a checklist that asks "have you considered performance" will almost always receive the answer "yes, we have", and the review approves a plan nobody validated in a warm haze of agreement. Designing a checklist that will not be waved away comes down to making every question demand evidence, numbers, or an explicit unknown, rather than demanding an attitude.

## Problem

A checklist's value depends on question quality: "have you considered performance" will certainly get "yes, we have", and the review approves a plan nobody validated in a warm haze of agreement. Good questions demand evidence, numbers, or an explicit unknown; a checklist only becomes usable once it is split by stage (kickoff, design, and pre-launch each ask their own questions).


### A Checklist's Value Depends on Question Quality

Questions that can genuinely get through a review usually have three traits:

- Demand evidence rather than a stance: do not ask whether something was considered, ask what the basis is and where the data is.
- Land on a concrete scenario: do not ask whether something could go wrong, ask which part hits its ceiling first at peak.
- Allow an honest unknown: the answer "don't know" is acceptable, but it must come with "who finds out what, and by when".

The opposite way, questions that are easy to wave off tend to be broad and empty, like "how is reliability guaranteed" or "how is scalability handled". Such questions have neither a scenario nor an acceptance criterion, so a passing "no problems so far" ends the matter. Checklist designers must be clear about one thing: how broad a question is directly determines how perfunctory the answer is.

### Good Questions Demand Evidence, Not a Stance

Rewriting the same concern in a form that demands evidence usually changes the review outcome completely:

- Do not ask "is performance enough"; ask "what is the measured single-machine capacity, which load test produced it, and what margin is budgeted".
- Do not ask "could data be lost"; ask "where is the authoritative data stored, what happens when the write path fails, and who compensates".
- Do not ask "what do we do when something breaks"; ask "what is the most likely first failure, how long until it is detected, and what is the stop-bleeding action".
- Do not ask "can it scale"; ask "when online numbers double, which component fails first, and what has to change to scale out".

The cleverness of this phrasing is that it does two things at once: if the other side has prepared, the question draws out a solid derivation; if they have not, the question exposes the blind spot on the spot instead of letting it surface only in production. The most valuable moments in a review are often exactly when some question makes the author realize for the first time that they never thought of something.

### A Checklist Only Becomes Usable Once Split by Stage

One giant checklist spanning every stage is almost certainly destined to fail, because most questions have no meaning at a specific stage. The common split is by milestone volume:

- Kickoff stage: what is the dominant problem domain, what is the most expensive failure, which decisions are irreversible, and what prototype validates them.
- Design review stage: state ownership, authority boundaries, capacity derivation, failure and degradation, release and rollback.
- Pre-launch: load test conclusions, monitoring coverage, emergency plans, rollback drills, gray release and ramp-up cadence.
- Retrospective stage: which questions were asked in advance, which were missed, and which volume the missed ones should be added to.

The benefit of splitting into volumes is that each review faces only a dozen or so related questions, each meaningful right now. Checklist length is itself a cost; a full checklist of two hundred items turns a review into a box-ticking ceremony.

### Authority and Consistency Questions

This group targets state boundaries, which is also where designs most easily get vague:

- Which source is the authority for each category of key state, and whether any dual-write or derived value is being treated as fact.
- Which client-reported data is trusted directly, and where that trust boundary lies.
- When two systems modify the same data at once, what rule adjudicates.
- When scheduled jobs, async messages, and player actions arrive concurrently, what guarantees ordering.

Being waved off on this group is extremely costly, because once authority boundaries ship they are hard to untangle, and backfilling, reconciliation, and accountability keep paying for them long term.

### Capacity and Performance Questions

Capacity questions are the easiest to wave off with "it should be enough" and worth pressing on item by item:

- What is the conversion chain from business metrics to system load, and what is the basis for each step.
- Do single-machine numbers come from measurement or estimation, and does the measured environment resemble production.
- Which component is the first expected bottleneck, and what does it look like when it appears.
- At what scale are each of the three peak scenarios — server launch, events, version updates — prepared.
- What is the emergency plan when reality exceeds expectations: rate limiting, queuing, or degradation.

If capacity questions cannot be answered with numbers, the derivation was usually never done rather than there being no real problem. The value of this group is in forcing "feelings" into "numbers".

### Failure, Degradation, and Recovery Questions

The block most often missing from a design review is defining behavior when the system breaks:

- When a dependent external service times out or becomes unavailable, what state does this system enter.
- Which features can degrade, whether degradation is automatic or a manual switch, and where the switch lives.
- What the player-visible symptoms are, and whether freezes, duplicate charges, or corrupted state can occur.
- After a failure recovers, how is intermediate-state data handled, and is reconciliation and compensation needed.
- What is the target recovery time, and what mechanism guarantees it.

The quality of answers to this group often directly predicts how chaotic the first production failure will be. Plans whose answers contain the phrase "it probably won't go down" deserve to be pressed on especially carefully.

### Release, Rollback, and Compatibility Questions

Change-related risk has its own question set:

- Which coexisting versions does this change involve, and what will old client versions encounter.
- What are the rollback steps when something goes wrong, and does rollback have irreversible consequences at the data layer.
- Do configuration and data changes need to ship in sync with code changes, and who guarantees the order.
- How is the gray release scope decided, and which metrics decide whether to continue or stop.

Many teams' rollback plans stop at the single sentence "we can roll back", only to discover during a real incident that rollback itself triggers a new failure. Driving rollback steps to the point where they are executable is the core purpose of this checklist branch.

### Mechanisms That Keep Reviews from Being Waved Off

A question checklist is only a tool; it needs a matching review mechanism to be honored:

- Record an owner and a deadline on the spot for every unresolved question; a question with no deadline is as good as not asked.
- Allow "don't know" as an answer, but require it to become a validation task, with the result flowing back into the next review.
- Archive review records, including rejected plans and the reasons for rejection.
- During retrospectives, look back at review records and add missed questions to the checklist, so the checklist itself iterates.

The vitality of a checklist comes from this loop: a review finds a blind spot, the blind spot becomes a task, and the task's result revises the checklist again. Teams that only use a checklist as a template to fill in usually abandon it within two releases.

### Common Pitfalls

- Questions so broad and empty that a single "we considered it" waves them off, reducing the review to a confirmation ritual.
- One giant checklist spanning every stage, turning the review into a two-hundred-item box-ticking performance.
- Asking only whether a plan exists, not what the basis and data are, letting estimates masquerade as conclusions.
- Not allowing "don't know", forcing everyone to improvise answers on the spot and hiding blind spots even deeper.
- Questions with no owner and no deadline, so nobody reads the review record after it is written.
- A checklist that never changes, with the same kinds of problems surfacing repeatedly but never revised.

The essence of a question checklist is turning the pitfalls the last batch of people stepped in into questions the next batch must answer. It cannot prevent every incident, but it can make "never thought of it" show its face during the design stage instead of waiting until the failure report.
