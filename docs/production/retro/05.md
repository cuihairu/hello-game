# Personal Gap-Filling Checklist

## Category

production / retro subtree. Node positioning: a personal capability self-audit — graded by "what you can actually deliver" rather than "what you have studied", across four dimensions: primary path, state and data, operations and delivery, and collaboration and expression.

## Definition


A personal capability inventory is harder than a team one, because people naturally overestimate what they are familiar with. Reading an article about gateway design and personally surviving an ingress-layer avalanche may both feel like "I understand networking"; the gap only shows up the night you face a failure alone. A usable self-audit checklist aims not at listing knowledge points but at measuring each dimension by "what you can actually deliver", letting weak spots surface on their own.

## Problem

The difficulty of self-audit is overestimating familiarity: reading about gateway design and surviving an ingress-layer avalanche both feel like "I understand networking", and the gap only shows up the night you face a failure alone. A good self-audit checklist measures dimensions by deliverables, letting weak spots surface on their own rather than listing knowledge points to manufacture a sense of security.


### The Difficulty of Self-Audit Is Overestimating Familiarity

The capability illusion usually appears in two places:

- Read but never did it: you followed someone else's derivation and mistook that for having the same judgment.
- Did it but never thought it through: you implemented a ready-made plan once and cannot derive it under different constraints.

So the standard self-audit action is not "have I studied this" but three harder questions: can you design a solution for this area independently, can you troubleshoot it independently, and can you explain the trade-offs to the team. All three passing counts as grounded; a position where only one passes is often the weak spot itself.

### Grade by What You Can Do, Not What You Have Studied

When scoring a dimension, a common four-level split is harder to fool yourself with than words like "aware", "familiar", and "mastered":

- Can explain the concepts and common trade-offs, but has no complete practice.
- Can complete development and triage inside a framework designed by someone else.
- Can independently design a module or subsystem, handling boundaries and exceptions.
- Can lead a cross-system solution, making trade-offs for capacity, consistency, and team division of labor.

One fact worth facing honestly when scoring: dimensions where the gap between levels is large usually deserve more investment than dimensions scored high, because in projects the part that fails first is often the part nobody truly owns.

### Dimensions on the Primary Path Side

The primary path is the route every player action passes through, and a shortcoming here most easily becomes an incident directly:

- Network ingress: long-connection management, weak-network behavior, heartbeat and reconnection, and the handling approach for message reliability and ordering.
- Synchronization and time: frame advance, input and state convergence, prediction and reconciliation, and the trade-offs in frame catch-up after a disconnect.
- Concurrency and runtime: how logic advance is organized within a process, how threads and tasks are split, and how lock contention and backpressure are recognized.

A self-audit on this side can be very concrete: take any player action path in the current project — can you trace it from the client keypress all the way to the state landing in the database, naming at each step who is authoritative, who is async, and what happens when it breaks. Any segment you cannot walk through end to end usually maps to a weak dimension.

### Dimensions on the State and Data Side

This side determines whether the system can stay alive long term; shortcomings here often come due late but at huge cost:

- State partitioning: whether temporary state, long-term assets, and derived data are kept apart, and what each one's consistency requirements are.
- Authority ownership: for each data type who is the source of truth, and how the risk of dual-write and derived-over-source is prevented.
- Storage and migration: whether data structures hold up during server merges, scale-out, and archival, and how compensation and reconciliation are done.

A reference self-audit question: for any data in the project, can you say where its authority lives, how to rebuild it if it is lost, and how to repair it after an error. The data you cannot answer that for is often also the most expensive to mishandle in a future asset incident.

### Dimensions on the Operations and Delivery Side

Quality of life after launch is largely determined by this side:

- Observability: how logs, metrics, and traces work together to locate problems, and whether key paths have a trace threading them.
- Capacity: can you derive from business metrics to machine count, and do you dare trust load test conclusions directly.
- Release and rollback: how gray release is done, whether rollback steps are executable, and who guarantees the order of configuration versus code.
- Security and risk control: which inputs are inherently untrusted, and how the boundaries of risk control and bans are handled.

The common state here is "I've gone along and done it", and going along does not produce judgment. A self-audit question: if an alert fires on duty, what do you look at in the first minute and what in the second — if you cannot answer, your triage methodology is not built yet.

### Dimensions on the Collaboration and Expression Side

Beyond technology, this side often determines how much of the same capability gets realized:

- Requirement translation: can you translate technical constraints into cost numbers a product colleague can weigh.
- Solution expression: can you explain trade-offs in a review rather than only the conclusion.
- Retrospective contribution: can you extract process improvements from an incident rather than stopping at fixing the code.
- Knowledge retention: have your own decisions and pitfalls been left as records others can read.

This side is easily dismissed as a soft skill, but the two types of people most often complained about on a team — those who cannot explain their own solution and those who cannot explain their own system — both have the problem here.

### Validate Against Project Experience, Not Against Feeling

After scoring dimensions, it is worth validating the scores with a concrete action:

- For each dimension scored high, find an instance you handled completely yourself; it only counts if you can give the details.
- For each dimension scored low, think back over whether there were scenarios you actually encountered but did not recognize at the time.
- A high-scoring dimension with no instance found drops a level automatically, because it is probably an illusion from reading.

This validation occasionally produces counterintuitive results: some people have plenty of project experience but cannot give an independent-design instance for any dimension, which means they have been executing other people's frameworks all along. That is itself an important self-audit finding, pointing to seeking design opportunities rather than reading ten more articles.

### Common Paths for Filling Gaps

Once gaps are found, different kinds of gaps have different ways to fill them:

- Concept gaps: read documentation and others' retrospectives to build a vocabulary and trade-off map quickly; the cheapest option.
- Practice gaps: take a small scenario and do it end to end, such as adding a monitoring path to an existing system or running one capacity derivation.
- Judgment gaps: accumulated only by taking part in real reviews and failure retrospectives, listening mainly to how others ask questions and weigh trade-offs.

A fairly efficient long-term habit is to bind the self-audit to daily events: check the relevant dimensions after every failure retrospective, and compare against the checklist to find blind spots before taking on a new module. A self-audit that follows the project's rhythm is usually more honest than an annual inventory, because the memory is fresh.

### Common Pitfalls

- Scoring yourself with "studied" and "know about", producing uniformly inflated scores and missing every blind spot.
- A checklist that keeps growing until it becomes a knowledge-point recitation table disconnected from actual work.
- Only taking stock before a job change or promotion, and never checking against project events in between.
- Answering practice gaps with heavy reading after finding them, still unable to design after finishing.
- Watching only technical dimensions while collaboration and expression shortcomings go unowned for a long time.
- Self-audit results never turning into concrete action, so the same conclusions surface again next year.

The real use of a gap-filling checklist is not giving yourself a capability score, but turning the vague question "what to study and do next" into specific actions. An honest checklist keeps the direction from going wildly wrong; a self-deceiving checklist can have you spinning in your strongest area no matter how hard you try.
