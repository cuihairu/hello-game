# Architecture Patterns and Selection Comparison

## Category

production / retro subtree. Node positioning: a pattern is an answer to a problem domain — single-server full state, room processes, large-world zoning, modular monolith, service decomposition, and Actor each fit a different problem, and comparison happens along problem dimensions rather than by name.

## Definition


Arguments about architecture patterns often fail to conclude because answers to different problem domains are being compared against each other. A sharded-server monolith is the mature answer for long-cycle growth categories, a room process is the mature answer for session-closed categories, and large-world zoning is the answer for persistently online categories; they are not on the same track to begin with. Before comparing architecture patterns, the more useful step is to confirm what your own project's dominant problem domain is; otherwise a comparison table can be as detailed as you like and still just mismap someone else's answer onto your own problem.

## Problem

Comparing patterns by name ("we use Actor, they use microservices") neither converges nor holds up: a pattern has no right or wrong once detached from its problem domain — single-server full state is a disaster at tens of millions of DAU and the efficiency king at a hundred thousand DAU. The value of comparison lies in stating each pattern's applicable boundary and its cost clearly; combination is the norm.


### A Pattern Is Not a Style, It Is an Answer to a Problem Domain

Every common architecture pattern is answering a particular set of questions:

- Single-server full state: all logic in one process, fitting stages with simple state, controllable scale, and a very small team.
- Sharded-server structure: state and players are split by server shard, fitting long-cycle categories where assets are hard-bound and social interaction closes within a server.
- Room process: one match is the birth-and-death unit of state, fitting categories whose sessions close and leave only the result outside the match.
- Large-world zoning: a persistently online world is cut into manageable regions, fitting categories where world state exists long term and players move freely.
- Stateless ingress plus stateful logic: the connection layer and the logic layer are separated, fitting scenarios with a large connection volume but concentrated logic state.

These patterns have no ranking between them, only whether they match the current problem domain. The first sentence of most discussions — "what architecture should we use" — is actually asking the question backwards. A more effective question is: "what is the birth-and-death unit of our state, where do we put authority, and which load hits its ceiling first."

### Compare Along Problem Dimensions, Not Names

When patterns are put side by side, what is actually comparable is not the name but a few stable dimensions:

- State ownership: in which unit does state live, and who carries authority.
- Lifecycle: what creates and destroys the state — one match, one server shard, or the whole world.
- Scale unit: when traffic rises, do you add machines, add processes, or migrate regions.
- Failure radius: when one unit goes wrong, how many people are affected and how long until recovery.
- Operational complexity: how much staffing and process the everyday release, scaling, and troubleshooting require.
- Release cadence: can a module release independently, and how large is the blast radius of one change.
- Team cognitive load: how long before a newcomer understands the whole picture, and how many people can locate a fault.

It is normal for two patterns to trade wins across dimensions. The goal of comparison is not to elect an all-round champion, but to find the one that matches your highest-priority dimensions while its worst dimension remains tolerable.

### Comparing Single-Server Full State with the Sharded-Server Structure

The substance of this comparison is whether state gets cut horizontally. Sharding locks player assets and social relationships inside one server; the benefit is simple state, a small failure radius, and server merge available as an operating lever; the cost is that every cross-server feature needs an extra link, and leaderboard, matchmaking, and cross-server combat all become separate problems. Single-server full state removes those cross-server links, but state scale, release risk, and single points of failure keep degrading as online numbers grow.

The usual basis for judgment is how closed assets and social interaction are: if player investment settles mainly into in-server relationships and assets, sharding is usually less effort; if players care more about real-time competition and the whole-server ecosystem, partitioning must be done carefully, because the cost of merging back after splitting is extremely high. Another often-neglected variable is operating strategy: if opening new servers and merging servers are part of the monetization rhythm, the sharded structure is itself an operating tool, a use that does not exist in a full-state structure.

### Comparing Room Processes with Large-World Zoning

The substance of this comparison is the birth-and-death granularity of state. A room process treats one match as the unit — created at match start, destroyed at settlement — so state is naturally bounded, a failure affects one match, and scaling out just means opening more processes. Large-world zoning state lives long term; migrating one region requires moving the entire player context, and both scaling and failure handling are much more complex.

The easy place to misjudge is hybrid categories: part of a persistently online world's gameplay is carried by room-based instances, or a room-based product's matchmaking, social, and asset systems are long-cycle systems. In those cases both patterns usually coexist, and what actually needs designing is the boundary between them, not which pattern is more advanced.

### Comparing the Modular Monolith with Service Decomposition

Service decomposition is introduced too early in game backends very often. Independent release, independent scaling, and failure isolation brought by splitting are valuable, but the costs are equally real: inter-process communication, deployment orchestration, cross-link troubleshooting, and data consistency each consume team effort for the long term.

A more common healthy path is to start from a modular monolith, keep module boundaries clear, and wait until some module's load curve, release cadence, or team ownership genuinely diverges before splitting that one module out. Pushing it as "split where it hurts" often beats spreading it out at once because "the industry all split". Conversely, merging systems back together after they have been split is just as hard, so a split decision deserves to be argued to the standard of an irreversible decision.

### Comparing Actor with Shared Memory Plus Locking

This comparison happens inside a process. Actor-style models fence state into independent units driven serially by messages; the benefit is no lock contention and relatively clear fault localization; the cost is that cross-unit operations become asynchronous messages and transactional operations are awkward to write. Shared memory plus locking is direct and intuitive to express, but lock granularity design and deadlock investigation test the team continuously.

A common way to converge is to layer by state heat and interaction pattern: strongly interacting, high-frequency core state is driven serially on a single thread, while weakly interacting peripheral systems use a concurrency framework. The root cause of most production incidents is not which side was chosen, but that the two paths were mixed on the same state with no boundary.

A practical clue when judging: what kind of code the team has mainly written before. Teams coming from business backends tend to adapt more easily to the shared-memory way of thinking, while teams coming from network frameworks adapt more easily to message driving. The payoff from continuing a familiar model usually outweighs chasing theoretical elegance.

### Combination Is the Norm

Most projects of any size are combinations of patterns: stateless ingress, logic in room processes, assets sharded or globally unique, and the operating side as services. Combination itself is not the problem; the problem is usually a blurry boundary:

- A room process rewrites long-term assets directly, and in-match state leaks outside the match.
- A global service and shard logic wait on each other, and the failure crosses layers.
- The same data has separate authorities in two patterns, and nobody knows which to trust when they disagree.
- Boundary interfaces have no version awareness, so one side upgrading drags the other into its release cycle.

The principle worth holding in a combined architecture is that each pattern owns authority only within its own problem domain, and cross-domain interaction goes through explicit contracts and interfaces rather than shared memory and direct calls.

### When a Re-Comparison Is Needed

An architecture comparison is not something finished at kickoff and filed away. When certain signals appear, it often means it is worth running the comparison once more:

- Load curves of different modules have clearly diverged, and uniform scaling has started to waste or fall short.
- Cross-module failure contagion is frequent, and the need for isolation has exceeded the original estimate.
- Team size has changed and the cognitive load model no longer matches reality.
- The category direction has shifted and the dominant problem domain has switched from one to another.
- Assumptions about load shape made at kickoff were overturned by a product change, and the original architecture's premises no longer hold.

A common mistake during a re-comparison is patching only on top of the existing architecture while refusing to re-examine the original dimension assumptions. That is exactly the point of comparison: dimensions are relatively stable, and a pattern is just a solution to them.

### Common Pitfalls

- Treating patterns as schools to take sides in, rather than as answers to different problem domains.
- Comparing only names and slogans, not the substantive dimensions like state ownership, scale unit, and failure radius.
- Reverse-engineering your own architecture from someone else's successful project without seeing that the other party's problem domain and team conditions differ.
- Splitting into services because it is fashionable rather than because it hurts, and paying back the benefit with communication and troubleshooting cost after the split.
- Bringing in both patterns in a hybrid category without designing the boundary and authority ownership clearly.
- Never comparing again after the architecture is fixed, running on old assumptions even though load and team have both changed.
- Leaving no record of the comparison conclusion, so the next discussion starts from zero.

The value of an architecture comparison lies not in giving a standard answer, but in letting the team speak in the same set of dimensions. Clear dimensions shrink disagreement; vague dimensions mean whichever pattern you choose gets argued over again after launch.
