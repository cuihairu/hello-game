# Engine, Language, and Technology Selection

## Category

production / retro subtree. Node positioning: the correct order for a selection discussion — fix the constraints first (team, category, platform, cycle), then look at candidate fit; use irreversibility to decide how much argument to invest, and use decision records to settle it.

## Definition


Technology selection is the topic teams argue about the most and settle the least. Most of the time it is not because the options genuinely cannot be ranked, but because the order of the discussion is reversed: people argue about "which one to use" without first fixing "who we are, what category we are making, what platforms we are shipping to, how many people, and how long the cycle is". Once the constraints are absent, the discussion degenerates into each person listing the strengths of the tools they already know, and in the end it often becomes whoever has the higher rank gets listened to. A mature selection discussion usually goes the other way: write the constraints down clearly first, then look at which constraints each candidate fits and which ones it sacrifices.

## Problem

Selection is hardest to settle because the order of discussion is reversed: argue "which one" first, without fixing "who we are, what we are making, how many people, how long the cycle". With constraints absent, the discussion degenerates into each person listing the strengths of familiar tools, and in the end whoever has the higher rank gets listened to. Irreversible decisions get under-argued while reversible ones get over-argued, and the investment ends up misallocated.


### Separating Hard Constraints from Soft Constraints

A more reliable approach is to list two kinds of constraints before arguing about options:

- Hard constraints: target platform, the real-time requirements of the category, the team's existing skill structure, the release cycle, commercial and compliance terms — these usually leave little room for discussion.
- Soft constraints: team preference, technological sophistication, community momentum, personal experience — these can be weighed, and are often overestimated.

Hard constraints eliminate a whole batch of candidates outright. If the target platform is a mini-game platform, many native-first options no longer hold; if the whole team has a background in one language but you force in a new language nobody has used, the learning cost will eat up most of a release. Many seemingly technical disagreements in a selection meeting are essentially one side holding a hard constraint and the other holding a soft one, each trying to persuade the other. Separate these two layers first and the discussion often becomes effective much faster.

### What Engine Selection Is Really Weighing

The rendering capabilities and physics features on an engine's spec page are usually not the deciding factors. In real projects, engine selection is more often driven by the following:

- Category and platform fit: 2D or 3D, mini-game or native, single-platform or multi-platform.
- Existing team experience: shipping a solid 70-point product with a familiar engine is often better than chasing a 90-point one with an unfamiliar engine.
- Content production pipeline: how smooth the editor is, the art asset workflow, how designers configure content — this pipeline determines mass-production throughput.
- Commercial terms: revenue share, source-code availability, the pace of version upgrades — the vendor's roadmap affects the project long term.
- Ecosystem and hiring: the size of the user base determines what kind of people you can hire when the team expands.
- Multi-platform planning: multi-platform needs underestimated at kickoff turn out to be unsupported by the foundation when you add a platform midway.

In most engine-selection failures, the loss is not on benchmarks; it is losing to a content pipeline that bottlenecks throughput, to a key role that stays unfilled for a long time, or to commercial terms that change midway. You cannot feel these problems at the prototype stage; they erupt all at once in the mass-production phase, and by then the cost of switching is entirely different.

### The Weighing Logic for Server-Side Languages Is Different

A client engine makes a large part of the decision for the team; a server-side language means the team bears all the consequences itself. Common considerations include:

- Whether the runtime's characteristics fit the category: long-connection capacity, concurrency model, GC pause behavior, and the maturity of debugging and profiling tools.
- Ecosystem completeness: whether protocol libraries, storage drivers, and observability components are all present; everything you have to build yourself because it is missing is a long-term cost.
- Hiring difficulty: an obscure language often means the team size has an invisible ceiling.
- Existing code and team skills: in most projects the server-side language is determined by the team's existing capabilities, not the other way around.
- Long-term maintenance supply: the maturity of operational discipline and troubleshooting ability often affects long-term cost more than the language's own sophistication.

Switching a server-side language is extremely expensive, so choices at this position tend to be conservative. A common combination: pick one unified language across the whole stack while the team is small to lower collaboration cost; once the team grows, introduce a language better suited to the load characteristics module by module, rather than rewriting in one shot. Language wars on the server side are often not that meaningful; what really opens the gap is usually operational discipline and troubleshooting ability.

### Using Irreversibility to Decide How Much Argument to Invest

Not every selection is worth three rounds of review meetings. A common yardstick for how much argument to invest is how irreversible the decision is:

- Engine, core language, core storage: once rolled out, going back is extremely hard; these usually warrant prototype validation and a formal review.
- Protocol format, module boundaries, core middleware: adjustment cost is moderate; worth writing the reasoning clearly, but not worth prolonged back-and-forth.
- Utility libraries, helper scripts: highly reversible; small-scope trial and error is enough.

For irreversible decisions, a common practice is to validate the biggest risk point with a vertical slice: run the core path through with a real category and a near-real scale, rather than studying the feature list on the vendor's website. The conclusions gained from validation are worth archiving, even if the prototype code itself is eventually discarded.

### How to Run a Selection Meeting Without Spinning

The most common failure mode of a selection discussion is not a wrong conclusion, but that the meeting itself has no boundaries. A few proven meeting disciplines are worth following:

- Send out the constraint list and candidate materials in advance; do not speak in the meeting without having read the material.
- Assign one person familiar with each candidate to present, and require them to state clearly what it is bad at.
- Bring disagreements down to concrete dimensions for comparison, rather than staying at vague good and bad.
- When no conclusion is reached, settle on the spot what gets validated next, who is responsible, and when to report back.

A common closing action is to confirm three things on the spot: what was decided, what was not, and who validates what by when. Without this step, the next meeting often restarts from the beginning of the argument.

### Decision Records Outlive Conclusions

The context behind a selection conclusion is forgotten within six months. A more durable practice is to leave a short record, containing:

- What the hard constraints were at the time, and which candidates were considered.
- Why each candidate was rejected, and what benefits were given up.
- What problems were expected to appear, and how you planned to watch for them.

The value of this record shows up at two moments. First, when the risk actually materializes six months later, the team can quickly tell whether "the original judgment was wrong" or "the premises changed", and the two have entirely different retrospective directions. Second, when a new member takes over, they do not have to do code archaeology and rely on word of mouth to understand "why this stack". Projects that write poor decision records often re-run the same selection argument every year or two.

### When Is It Worth Switching Foundations

Switching engines or languages is rarely a sudden decision; it is usually triggered by an accumulation of steadily worsening signals. There is also a passive form worth guarding against separately: the engine vendor stops maintaining it, key middleware stops updating, or platform rule changes make the old solution no longer supported. Passive switching is far more dangerous than voluntary switching, because the timetable is not in your hands and it often lands exactly on the period of highest release pressure. Voluntary trigger signals include:

- The core experience still cannot be delivered on the current foundation despite repeated attempts.
- The content pipeline keeps bottlenecking throughput, and the team spends large amounts of time fighting things unrelated to the goal.
- A key role stays unfilled for a long time, or the system grinds to a halt the moment one of a few depended-on people leaves.
- Commercial terms change substantially and the cost structure of continuing to use it no longer holds.

Even when the signals hold, gradual replacement is often more realistic than tearing down and rewriting: the new foundation takes on incremental modules first, existing modules migrate at the maintenance pace, and a rollback position is kept available at all times. The most underestimated cost of a one-shot rewrite is losing all the already-fixed hidden pitfalls of the old system at the same time.

### Common Pitfalls

- Picking whichever looks pleasing from a list of options, rather than first writing down the project's own constraints.
- Deciding from spec sheets and benchmarks while ignoring long-term variables like the content pipeline, hiring, and commercial terms.
- Using soft constraints to argue down hard ones, e.g. sacrificing platform fit for technological sophistication.
- Investing less argument time in irreversible decisions than in debating reversible ones.
- Holding selection meetings over many rounds with neither a conclusion nor a validation task, starting from zero next time.
- Not leaving decision records after a selection, so the same argument plays out again years later.

The output of a selection is never the three words "picked right"; it is a decision process that can be revisited, questioned, and understood by those who come after. If the process is preserved, a wrong choice can still be corrected; if it is not preserved, even a right choice cannot explain why it was right.

### Case Study: A Selection Retrospective Investigation After "A New Engine Decided in Two Meetings, Throughput Down 60% in Mass Production"

> The case is a demonstration example (not drawn from a real project), with numbers rounded for simplicity. It takes this page's "Separating Hard Constraints from Soft Constraints", "What Engine Selection Is Really Weighing", "Using Irreversibility to Decide How Much Argument to Invest", "How to Run a Selection Meeting Without Spinning", "Decision Records Outlive Conclusions", and "When Is It Worth Switching Foundations" to the deep end: argument skipped at kickoff comes due in the mass-production phase as three bills — throughput, hiring, and repeated arguments.

**Background and Symptoms**: A **12**-person team (all clients with an engine A background) kicks off a multi-platform project and decides on engine B — high community momentum, pretty benchmarks — after just **2** selection meetings: **0** pages of hard-constraint list, **0** candidate comparison documents, **0** vertical slices, and **0** decision records. The bill comes due in month **4** of mass production: importing art assets into engine B takes **9** manual steps, assembling one scene takes **2** days, weekly throughput falls from **5** scenes to **2** (down **60**%); a key role stays open for **3** months, of **40** resumes only **3** can pick up engine B, and **1** person is hired; in month **5** the team decides to add a mini-game platform, and engine B's export chain does not support it. At the retrospective nobody can clearly say "why did we pick it in the first place", and the same argument restarts **3** times within six months, each time from zero.

### Step One: Symptoms and Baselines — Separate "What Was Owed at the Time" from "What Comes Due Now"

Align the baseline: split "the argument skipped at kickoff" and "the cost coming due in mass production" into two ledgers, and first record two entries:

- **Owed**: **0** pages of hard-constraint list — all **5** items (target platform, category real-time requirements, skill structure, release cycle, commercial terms) never put on paper; **2** selection meetings to settle it, **0** validation tasks
- **Due**: weekly throughput **5** → **2** (down **60**%); **1** person hired after **3** months of recruiting; adding a platform reveals the foundation does not support it, and the client faces a wholesale migration

What dropped is not engine performance, it is the content pipeline; what cannot be hired is not engineers, it is engineers who know engine B — neither ledger is explained by "benchmarks".

### Step Two: Layered Attribution — From Throughput Loss Back to the Selection-Process Ledger

```text
约束账   硬约束清单 0 页、候选对比 0 份，2 轮会凭社区热度定案——软约束说服了硬约束
产能账   美术入库 9 步手工、单场景装配 2 天，周产能 5 → 2 个场景（掉 60%）
人员账   关键岗位开放 3 个月：简历 40 份、能上手 3 份、到岗 1 人
记录账   决策记录 0 份，同一争论半年重开 3 次、场场从零
```

The ledgers converge: the **60%** throughput loss is a pipeline ledger, the **1** person in **3** months is an ecosystem ledger, the **3** restarts in six months is a record ledger — all three hang off the same constraint ledger: on the day of kickoff, the constraint list was **0** pages.

### Step Three: Root Cause — Picked the Option First, Filled in the Reasoning After

Checked against this page's "Separating Hard Constraints from Soft Constraints": at kickoff, "picking whichever looks pleasing from a list of options, rather than first writing down the project's own constraints" — all **5** "hard constraints: target platform, the category's real-time requirements, the team's existing skill structure, the release cycle, commercial and compliance terms" were never put on paper; in the meetings, "many seemingly technical disagreements are essentially one side holding a hard constraint and the other holding a soft one, each trying to persuade the other", and in the end "using soft constraints to argue down hard ones, e.g. sacrificing platform fit for technological sophistication" happened verbatim. Checked against "What Engine Selection Is Really Weighing": none of the six dimensions — category and platform fit, existing team experience, content production pipeline, commercial terms, ecosystem and hiring, multi-platform planning — entered the comparison argument; looking back, throughput, hiring, and multi-platform all went wrong — "most engine-selection failures are not lost on benchmarks, but lost to a content pipeline bottlenecking throughput, a key role unfilled for a long time, or commercial terms changing midway" hits the first two of its three points, and "you cannot feel these problems at the prototype stage; they erupt all at once in mass production, and by then the cost of switching is entirely different" came true word for word. Checked against "Using Irreversibility to Decide How Much Argument to Invest": the engine belongs to the tier "once rolled out, going back is extremely hard; these usually warrant prototype validation and a formal review", yet the argument was only **2** meetings and **0** slices; in the same quarter the team argued for **3** weeks over a reversible state-management library — "investing less argument time in irreversible decisions than in debating reversible ones" happened verbatim; "deciding from spec sheets and benchmarks while ignoring long-term variables like the content pipeline, hiring, and commercial terms" was exactly the approach taken at the time, and "not leaving decision records after a selection, so the same argument plays out again years later" came due early at a frequency of **3** times in six months.

### Step Four: Remediation and Backfill — Put the Constraints on Paper First, Then Migrate Back at a Pace

The fix has two parts. Emergency: at **T+0**, freeze the "switch or not" argument, write out all **5** hard constraints item by item and score engine B against them, with platform fit and ecosystem/hiring both flagged red; at **T+1**, stop the throughput bleeding — **2** people in **5** days converge the **9** manual import steps into a **1**-click import script, single-scene assembly drops from **2** days to **0.5** days, and weekly throughput goes from **2** scenes back to **5**. Mid-term migration: engine B is still maintained, so this is not the worst case of "passive switching is far more dangerous than voluntary switching, because the timetable is not in your hands"; migrate back to engine A by gradual replacement — all incremental modules land on A first, B's existing modules migrate back over **2** release cycles at the maintenance pace, with a rollback position kept at every step; do not tear down and rewrite, because "the most underestimated cost of a one-shot rewrite is losing all the already-fixed hidden pitfalls of the old system at the same time". Three process steps: step one, constraints first — add **5** mandatory hard-constraint fields to the kickoff review form, and no selection meeting is held if any is missing; step two, meeting discipline — "send out the constraint list and candidate materials in advance; do not speak in the meeting without having read the material" and "assign one person familiar with each candidate to present, and require them to state clearly what it is bad at", bring disagreements down to concrete dimensions, and when undecided, leave a validation task on the spot (who is responsible, when to report back); step three, decision records — a three-part template ("what the hard constraints were and which candidates were considered", "why each candidate was rejected and what benefits were given up", "what problems were expected and how to watch for them") goes into the repository, and irreversible decisions in the three categories of engine, core language, and core storage are required to have a vertical slice plus a formal review. Recheck: six months later a new project kicks off, entering the meeting only with **5**/**5** hard constraints complete, **1** selection meeting settles it, and the **2** post-meeting validation tasks are reported back **2**/**2** on schedule; **1** decision record is in the repository, and a new member reads "why this stack" in **30** minutes, with the same argument restarting **0** times.

### The Three Lessons of the Case

1. **The first paper of a selection is the constraint list, not the candidate list** — "shipping a solid 70-point product with a familiar engine is often better than chasing a 90-point one with an unfamiliar engine" was dismissed as conservative humor at the time, and six months later became the only stop-loss line.
2. **Argument skipped on an irreversible decision is repaid with principal and interest in mass production** — the argument saved by deciding in **2** meetings came back as **60%** lost throughput, **1** person in **3** months, and **2** release cycles of migration.
3. **Decision records are written for yourself six months later** — **0** records let the same argument restart **3** times in six months; "if the process is preserved, a wrong choice can still be corrected; if it is not preserved, even a right choice cannot explain why it was right".
