# Studio Division of Labor and Quality Gates

## Category

A newly added node in the production tree. Node positioning: distills "how a solo developer or a small team can borrow a mature studio's organizational structure" into a trimmable reference — how roles are layered, who adjudicates at each handoff and against which vocabulary, and what evidence defines "done." The material is a structural survey of the open-source engineering template Claude-Code-Game-Studios (49 role definitions, 74 workflow skills, 28 review gates); this page extracts the transferable division-of-labor and gate skeleton from it. Upstream: R&D Collaboration and the Release Workflow (06) frames the workflow as responsibility-interface design; this page supplies a ready-made sample of that interface.

## Definition

A studio method is made of three things: **role layering** — directors set direction, leads hold their departments, and specialists do the execution, with each role being an explicit contract: responsibilities, output format, a "what not to do" list, and an escalation path; **phase gates** — at a handoff a designated role adjudicates against a fixed vocabulary, and if it does not pass, it does not enter the next phase; **quality evidence** — "done" is not "I say I finished it," it is a verifiable artifact: tests that passed, retained screenshots, real sign-offs. A solo developer plus AI can play multiple roles in one person, but cannot skip the interfaces between roles: the person who writes cannot also be the person who accepts.

## Problem

Most quality problems on small teams are not technical problems, they are that nobody is gatekeeping: design goes unchallenged, code goes unreviewed, visuals go unaccepted, and releases go unblocked. Copying a big studio's org chart wholesale is too heavy, and setting up no interfaces at all is equivalent to running naked — the problem is not a lack of roles, it is a lack of the minimal structure of "who blocks what, at which point."

### Role layering: boundaries first, then talk collaboration

The template organizes 49 roles in three layers: the director layer (the highest adjudication for the five lines of creative, technical, art, audio, and narrative), the lead layer (department-level definitions for game design, lead programmer, QA, localization, and so on), and the specialist layer (concrete execution for gameplay, engine, networking, tools, levels, economy, playtest balance, and so on). The layers run on five coordination rules:

- **Vertical delegation**: directors delegate to leads, leads delegate to specialists; complex decisions do not skip layers.
- **Horizontal consultation**: peers may ask each other, but must not make a binding decision in the other party's domain.
- **Conflict escalation**: disagreements go up to a common superior; when there is no common superior, design conflicts go to the creative director and technical conflicts go to the technical director.
- **Change propagation**: cross-department design changes are coordinated and fanned out uniformly by the producer, not relayed piecemeal by the parties involved.
- **No cross-domain edits**: do not change directories and files that someone else owns without being delegated to.

The standard move when crossing a boundary is "state your own rules, acknowledge the other party's goal is legitimate, and escalate the trade-off," rather than adjudicating it yourself. The value of these boundaries shows up more clearly in reverse: nearly every role carries a "what not to do" list — the producer does not make creative and technical decisions, the lead programmer does not unilaterally veto design, QA does not skip testing under schedule pressure (only escalate to the producer) — responsibility boundaries are held by prohibitions, not by good will.

### Phase gates: the vocabulary is the discipline

Production is split into seven phases: concept → system design → technical setup → pre-production → mass production → polish → release, with six phase gates set between adjacent phases; within phases there are another 28 review gates, divided among seven gatekeepers: the creative director holds 6 (pillar stress test, GDD alignment, narrative consistency, playtest validation, and so on), the technical director holds 8 (system boundaries, feasibility, architecture sign-off, ADR review, change impact, and so on), the producer holds 5 (scope, sprint, milestone, Epic, phase readiness), the art director holds 4, the lead programmer holds 2, the QA lead holds 2, and the narrative director holds 1. A gate's operability lives entirely in the vocabulary:

- each gate has its own adjudication vocabulary: the scope gate answers REALISTIC / OPTIMISTIC / UNREALISTIC, the milestone gate answers ON TRACK / AT RISK / OFF TRACK, and the story-readiness gate answers ADEQUATE / GAPS / INADEQUATE. Answering with another gate's vocabulary is a wrong answer — the caller branches on the word.
- **NOT ASSESSED is the fourth verdict**, with a fixed ordering: FAIL / CONCERNS > NOT ASSESSED > PASS. "Cannot check" must have its own name, otherwise missing data gets rendered as clean — a report with no "could not assess" state manufactures a false green light by nature.
- the verdict word always goes on the first line and must not be buried in a paragraph; a missing required artifact is a **finding** (FAIL or CONCERNS), not "missing input."
- document depth is controlled by three tiers: the full tier requires the GDD's eight sections (overview, player fantasy, detailed rules, formulas, edge cases, dependencies, tuning knobs, acceptance criteria) and all ADRs; the standard tier requires only five sections (the formulas section is mandatory when the system defines numerical rules) and the key ADRs; the minimum tier does not require a GDD, and a one-page brief substitutes — the tier decides "what must exist," not "done well or poorly."

### Quality evidence: done is defined as a verifiable artifact

The template binds "story type" and "the evidence that must be delivered before closing" into a single matrix: logic-type (formulas, AI, state machines) must pass automated unit tests, blocking; integration-type must pass integration tests or have a retained playtest, blocking; visual and feel-type must deliver **retained screenshots** plus a lead sign-off, blocking; UI-type must retain a screenshot for every changed screen, blocking; config and numbers-type only needs a smoke check, advisory only. Three inferences are worth noting separately:

- **"Retained" is the keyword**. A screenshot that was taken and then deleted is an assertion, not evidence — evidence must be a file still on disk when the ticket is closed; a sign-off sheet that was never actually signed stays blank, and a pre-ticked approval is manufacturing a missing signature as if it were signed.
- **Parsing passing is not having run it**. Player-visible changes must have actually been run on real hardware, observed, and screenshots retained; the minimum tier may waive automated tests, but it does not waive "looking at it with your own eyes."
- untestable acceptance criteria are a blacklist: "the feel is balanced" and "it runs normally" are not acceptance criteria and must be rewritten as binary testable conditions (such as "any screen can complete navigation within two key presses" or "input response ≤ 50ms at the target frame rate"). At the same time, four kinds of things are explicitly **not automated**: visual fidelity, feel, platform rendering, and full matches — they rely on evidence-retaining human acceptance, not on assertions pretending to be automatable.

### Prototypes and slices: falsify the riskiest assumption first

The template distinguishes three verification builds, each with a hard time limit: a concept prototype answers "is this core interaction fun," with a 1-day cap, testing only one mechanic and doing no polish at all; a technical probe answers "is X technically doable," about 4 hours, producing YES / NO / PARTIAL; a vertical slice answers "can we build a complete loop at mass-production quality and on schedule," targeting 3–5 minutes of continuous gameplay, with a 1–3 week schedule, and going over means the scope was sliced wrong, not that capability is the problem. Supporting discipline:

- **The isolation law**: prototype code never enters the production repo and is never promoted to official status by refactoring; after a slice passes, the production implementation is written from zero. The prototype's output is a REPORT (hypothesis, riskiest hypothesis, observed result, recommendation PROCEED / PIVOT / KILL), not code.
- the same concept iterated three times to PIVOT forces consideration of KILL — "choosing naturally between two or three prototypes beats iterating one concept to death."
- a slice can only cut scope, never quality: a slice that does not look like the target game cannot validate mass-production feasibility.
- network-game warning: verifying locally at 0ms cannot validate feel under 80ms of latency, and a local prototype can only prove "interesting," not network feel.

### Numbers you can copy

Most of the thresholds in the template can be adopted as-is: cut tasks into 1–3 days each; leave a fixed 20% sprint buffer for unplanned work; escalate milestone risk two sprints early; scope creep ≤10% is treated as normal, over 25% requires cutting scope or a formal deferral, over 50% stops work and replans; always give a three-point estimate of optimistic / expected / pessimistic, round to half-day units, and split anything over 10 days; a sprint burn-down 10–25 percentage points behind counts as at risk, over 25 counts as behind, and a story with no update for 4 days is considered stalled; each retrospective is limited to 3–5 action items, each with an owner and a deadline; a fixed bug only counts as closed when there is a regression test that would have caught the original bug.
