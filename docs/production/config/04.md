# Workflow, Incident Fail-Safes, and Collaboration

## Category

production / config subtree. Node positioning: translate "this action is high-risk" into "this action cannot be done casually on the side"—role interfaces, change tiering, permission trace-keeping, fail-safes, and rollback drills.

## Definition


Reviewing config incidents reveals a recurring pattern: the action that went wrong is usually one the process allows anyone to execute at any time. Changing a value that affects the whole server economy requires no approval, deleting a row has no second confirmation, and the test environment entrance is open to everyone. The root of this class of incident is not capability but process—the system failed to translate "this action is high-risk" into "this action is hard to do casually." The entire point of fail-safe design is to turn risk levels into operational friction.

The collaboration surface of a config pipeline is much wider than that of code: designers, programmers, operations, and QA all work on the same chain, at a faster cadence than code. What workflow has to solve is not "keeping people in line", but keeping high-frequency changes safe even when sufficient communication is missing.

## Problem

The root of config incidents lies in process, not capability: the mistaken action is usually one the process allows anyone to execute at any time—changing a server-wide economic value without approval, deleting rows without second confirmation, test entrances open to everyone. The whole point of fail-safes is turning risk levels into operational friction; rollback needs drills, not standby status.


### Roles and Responsibility Interfaces

The common division of roles on the config chain is roughly a combination of three responsibilities: content editing, structure maintenance, and release operations. The division itself has no standard answer; what tends to go wrong are the fuzzy zones at responsibility seams:

- Who explains field meaning: designers mis-fill fields often because nobody can state the field's exact semantics.
- Who approves structure changes: adding a field affects downstream code, yet it is often treated as a purely designer-side matter.
- Who authorizes releases: whether those who can edit can also release directly is where the risk exposure is largest when the two are not separated.

A more robust practice is to make the seams explicit: structure changes go through code-side review, release permission is separated from edit permission, and field semantics are answered by the definition file rather than by people. A clear responsibility interface does not mean a heavier process: most day-to-day changes still take the light path, and only the part that crosses the interface enters the heavy path.

### Change Tiering: Not Every Change Deserves the Same Process

The point of approval is to keep risk and friction commensurate, not to put up checkpoints everywhere. The usual outcome of blanket checkpoints is that the process gets bypassed and loses its binding force. A common tiering approach combines impact scope with reversibility:

- Low risk: pure display copy, arrangement that does not affect numbers—publish straight after automatic validation post-edit.
- Medium risk: ordinary numerical adjustments, needing one lightweight confirmation and gray-release observation.
- High risk: changes involving economic output, payment probability, or server-wide switches, needing explicit approval and a release window.
- Irreversible operations: deleting data, bulk corrections—usually requiring backups first and two-person review.

The value of tiering is to keep high-frequency low-risk changes flowing smoothly while concentrating review resources on what is genuinely dangerous. Which tier a change belongs to can be declared in the field definition rather than guessed by people every time.

### Permissions: Who Can Touch Which Table

The plain principle of permission design is minimal sufficiency, which in the config context usually splits into several dimensions:

- Authorization by table or by module: the designer in charge of events does not directly edit economy tables.
- Authorization by environment: the write permission for production is far smaller than for test, and usually granted only to release roles.
- Authorization by operation: edit, approve, and release permissions are separated, at least at the high-risk tier.
- Temporary authorization has a term: temporary expanded permissions during an event are automatically revoked at expiry.

The immediate consequence of permissions being too loose is a large incident exposure; the consequence of being too tight is that permission borrowing becomes routine—once "let me just borrow a colleague's account and change this" gets accepted, the permission system exists in name only. So permission granularity needs periodic calibration, so that normal work basically never requires borrowing.

### Trace-Keeping: First-Hand Evidence During Troubleshooting

When config goes wrong, the four most-asked questions are: who changed it, when, what changed, and why. If those four questions cannot be answered, troubleshooting degrades from engineering activity into archaeology. A trace-keeping system usually needs to cover:

- The author, time, and field-level diff of every edit.
- The batch content, approver, and target environment of every release.
- The load record of every activation: which processes loaded which version at what time.
- The explanatory text at approval: why the change, and what the judgment basis was at the time.
- Records of intercepts and rollbacks: failed releases are just as valuable to review as successful ones.

The hard part of trace-keeping has never been technology, but persistence. The explanatory text is the easiest thing to be omitted first, and it is exactly the most valuable information when reviewing months later. Many teams make the explanation a required field at commit—better to write it short than to leave it blank.

### Common Fail-Safe Designs

Fail-safe measures are mostly plain; what matters is whether they are actually deployed on the operational path:

- A second confirmation for dangerous operations, with confirmation text spelling out the impact scope instead of a single "Are you sure?".
- A diff preview before release, letting the operator see what exactly they are about to ship.
- Mandatory justification for out-of-range numbers: values outside the normal range require a written explanation.
- A dry run before bulk changes: first produce the list of rows that will be affected, then execute after confirmation.
- Default-value fallbacks: missing fields take a safe default and raise an alert, instead of failing to load or reading as zero.
- Tightened time windows: high-risk operations are open only during staffed hours, and the threshold is automatically raised late at night.

The criterion for a fail-safe is simple: if the victim of one mistaken operation is not the operator themselves, that operation deserves friction. The farther and less visible the victim is from the operator, the greater the friction should be.

### Rollback Needs Drills, Not Standby Status

Nearly every system claims rollback capability, but whether it actually works when an incident hits depends on three things: whether the rollback target is clear (which batch to return to), whether the rollback path is clean (whether old data is compatible with new code), and whether the rollback has been drilled. Common failure modes include:

- Only the rollback button was kept, not the complete data that old batches depend on.
- Nobody walks the rollback path in normal times, so when it is really needed, permissions, process, and dependencies jam up everywhere.
- After rollback, the config conflicts with player data that has already happened, so even going back does not run level.

Regularly drilling one full rollback in the test environment costs little and buys real confidence in the sentence "we can go back." Drills also surface a batch of problems invisible in normal times, such as the export artifacts of old batches having long since been cleaned up.

### Config Freeze and Release Rhythm

The rhythm of the config pipeline usually interlocks with the version rhythm: a freeze period before a major version, and a high-frequency channel during day-to-day operations. The conflict between the two rhythms is a common source of collaboration friction:

- During a freeze, operations still has to tune live activities, which requires distinguishing "changes affecting the new version" from "changes affecting production only".
- The day a major version goes live usually comes with dense config releases, and release windows and observation duty have to be scheduled in advance.
- During operationally dense holiday seasons, approvers being off duty causes release backlogs, so proxy approval has to be arranged in advance.

The solution to rhythm problems is usually not canceling the freeze, but spelling out the freeze granularity clearly: which tables are frozen, which channels are open, how exceptions are handled. An unexplained freeze will always end up broken by reality.

### From Incident Back to Process

Every config incident deserves one question: how could the same mistaken operation be disallowed next time, or be easier to spot. The answer is usually not "be more careful next time", but adding one concrete layer to the process:

- Add a validation rule so this class of error gets reported at edit time.
- Add an approval tier so this class of change can no longer be released silently.
- Add a trace record so this class of problem can be pinned to a batch within five minutes of occurring.

A fail-safe checklist grows one item at a time like this. If a project that has been running for two years still has an empty fail-safe checklist, it is usually not good luck—it means the incidents were never seriously converted.

### Common Pitfalls

- Cramming all changes into one approval flow, so high-frequency low-risk changes get dragged down and everyone starts bypassing the process.
- Edit, approve, and release permissions held by the same group of people, so risk exposure loses its checks and balances.
- Trace-keeping records operations but not reasons, so months later you know what changed but not why.
- The rollback plan stays in a document and is never drilled, and only when an incident hits do you find you cannot go back.
- A one-size-fits-all freeze repeatedly broken by operational needs, so the freeze exists in name only.

The essence of workflow and fail-safes is admitting that under high-frequency change, people's attention and communication bandwidth are limited, and then having the system hold the bottom line in people's place. A system that holds the bottom line looks like constraints everywhere, but once it actually runs, what it buys is the freedom for everyone to move fast.
