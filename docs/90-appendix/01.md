# Reviewing Problems by Category

Card games, MOBAs, SLGs, or idle games are often used directly to derive an architecture, but the category label alone is not enough to give the answer. What truly affects the solution is where the system pressure concentrates. This page cross-references the knowledge base's related problem facets by common category.

## The basic method of review

A more effective review path starts by asking two questions more fundamental than category:

- In what unit do a player's key states come into and out of existence: a match, an account, a world, or a session.
- Where does the system's most expensive failure land: dispute over rulings, asset errors, being unable to start a match, or an event incident.

These two answers largely decide which directories are worth reviewing in depth. Category nouns only serve to summon intuition; the real distribution of technical pressure has to be pinned down through these two questions. Swap the same category for a different platform or a different monetization model and the answers move noticeably, so each direction below is worth checking against your own project's answers to these two questions rather than following the map. Below each direction is expanded in turn; what each direction gives is the category's principal facets across the knowledge base's directories, not a complete list.

## Facets of room-based categories

Products like mahjong, dou dizhu, billiards, and party games close their main gameplay within a single match; outside the match they retain only results and assets. They look simple, but there are not few problem facets:

- Sync and combat: settlement accuracy, disconnect and reconnect, and proxy play on timeouts are the fairness concerns most watched in this category.
- Data modeling: authoritative ownership of out-of-match assets, records, and ranks; the boundary between temporary state on the settlement path and long-lived assets.
- Common services: matchmaking quality, friend invites, spectating, and replays often determine a product's lifespan.
- Platform SDK: login, sharing, and payment integration for mini-game channels often feed back to influence the client's engine choice.
- Observability: locating match anomalies and gathering evidence for disputes directly affects customer-support cost.

The engineering incidents most likely to occur in room-based categories are often not "we can't build the gameplay," but unstable settlement and reconnect paths that look like edge cases.

## Facets of hard real-time competitive categories

For MOBAs, FPS games, fighters, and racers, the first-order problem is that latency, jitter, and cheating directly eat into fairness:

- Sync and combat: frame sync or state sync, prediction and reconciliation, and server-side arbitration timing almost determine the entire architecture.
- Execution model: advancing on a fixed tick, frame catch-up and replay budgets, single-frame cost control.
- Network access: transport-layer choice, weak-network optimization, packet rate and packet-batching strategy.
- Observability: quantifying frame-time distribution, frame deltas, and input latency — without these there is no basis for tuning.

The sync and execution models involved in this category are interrelated and together form the engineering foundation of fairness.

## Facets of persistently online categories

For MMOs, open worlds, and large-world social products, state lives for the long term and complexity keeps accumulating with content:

- Service splitting: cross-server gameplay, scene migration, and hotspot governance are unavoidable distributed-system topics for these projects.
- Data modeling: world state, player context, and data-structure elasticity for sharding and server merge.
- Execution model: AOI (area of interest) and broadcast cost, scheduling large numbers of entities.
- Capacity scaling topic: launch ramp-up, cross-server traffic, scaling paths under long-term growth.
- Version release: downtime-free updates, multi-endpoint coexistence, and release discipline for long-cycle content replacement.

A notable feature of technical debt in these projects: no single point looks serious, but layered onto the world model over years they begin to amplify each other. When reviewing, it's worth ranking by "what degrades over time."

## Facets of long-term growth categories

For card games, SLGs, idle and simulation-management products, the center of gravity is not in-match competition but cycles, assets, and pacing:

- Operations and monetization: event frameworks, payment paths, and lifecycle management are the body of the product, not an accessory.
- Config pipeline topic: numerical tables, event configuration, validation, and release flow directly determine iteration speed.
- Data modeling: growth assets, gacha records, and auditability of orders and compensations.
- Version release: coexistence of changes at different cadences, hotfix windows, rollback safety.
- Security and risk control: arbitrage, order farming, and studios eroding the economic system.

A common misjudgment in this category is betting all technical investment on gameplay implementation, only to find that during long-term operations the event path and configuration governance are what really drain the team.

## Facets of platform-ecosystem categories

Mini games, hyper-casual, and any product heavily dependent on channel distribution — platform constraints reshape the technical form:

- Platform SDK: the integration shape and review adaptation for login, payment, ads, and social sharing.
- Version release: package-size control, multi-channel packaging, and how platform review rhythm squeezes the release window.
- Security and risk control: handoff between platform-side rules and your own risk control.
- Operations and monetization: ad-monetization path, cross-platform data definitions.

A point often underweighted in these products: platform constraints often feed back to determine the client engine and package structure, so the review order at the selection stage should start with the platform.

## Differences within a single category are often larger

A reminder easily missed when reviewing by category: engineering differences inside a category are often larger than differences between categories. Also card games — real-time competitive card games and purely turn-based gacha card games are almost two different technical species; also casual games — those wired into heavy ad monetization versus pure in-app-purchase ones differ enormously in monetization-path complexity. The main variables causing the difference are usually:

- Platform and channels: mini-game platforms, native multi-endpoint, overseas multi-channel — each has different integration and release pressure.
- Monetization model: the degree of mixing in-app purchases, ads, and subscriptions directly changes the weighting of operations and risk control.
- Operations cadence: seasonal, versioned, or long-tail idle-phase operations determines the shape of the config pipeline and release discipline.

So category review only gives the first layer of index. When applying it to your own project, you still have to return to the problem model and recalibrate once with shared state and asset complexity.

## Common pitfalls

- Treating the category page as an architecture template, feeling that picking a category equals picking a technical solution.
- Only reading content related to your own category and ignoring isomorphic problem facets in adjacent categories.
- Substituting category nouns for problem analysis, skipping the two fundamental questions of state life-and-death and most expensive failure.
- Ignoring differences within a category and directly applying another product's practices to your own operations model.
- Turning review into a one-off browse, without checking against the specific problems of your current project.

The value of category review lies in making a familiar product context the door into technical problems. Once the door is open you should walk through: what truly determines the architecture is never the category label, but the verifiable problems behind it.
