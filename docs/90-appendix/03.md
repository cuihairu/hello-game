# Doing Cross-Cutting Selection and Quick Comparison

The trade-offs in the knowledge base's main line are scattered across directories: the sync model lives in sync and combat, transport choice in network access, storage partitioning in data modeling, and capacity derivation in the capacity scaling topic. Read one chapter at a time and the trade-offs are all clear, but when you actually reach a selection meeting and need to compare several options in one breath, you find each lives in a different chapter. Cross-cutting comparison compresses these scattered trade-offs onto the same set of dimensions, letting different solutions speak in the same coordinate system.

## What comparison solves is dimension amnesia

The most common failure mode of a selection discussion isn't comparing wrong, it's comparing on incomplete dimensions: when discussing engines you only compare features and forget commercial terms; when discussing storage you only compare performance and forget migration cost; when discussing a sync solution you only compare development difficulty and forget replay and anti-cheat. In a retrospective you can almost always find that the dimension missed back then was exactly the decisive one.

The value of comparison lies not in supplying new knowledge but in preventing dimension amnesia. It concentrates the trade-off dimensions that recur throughout the book onto one page, so that before comparing, the discussants run through the dimension checklist first and then decide which dimensions carry weight for your project.

## Frequently used comparison dimensions

The dimensions the main-line content points to repeatedly can be roughly grouped as:

- Real-time dimension: how high is the interaction latency requirement, can prediction and reconciliation be tolerated, where is the floor of behavior under a weak network.
- Authoritative dimension: which side the ruling lands on, how far fairness and anti-cheat requirements go.
- State dimension: in what unit state comes into and out of existence, how long the lifetime, how strong the consistency requirement.
- Version and multi-endpoint dimension: how heavy the multi-version coexistence pressure, how platform review and channel rhythm constrain release.
- Capacity dimension: what the load curve looks like, in what scenario the peak appears, what the scaling unit is.
- Team dimension: existing skill structure, hiring pool, and long-term supply of maintenance capacity.
- Cost dimension: not just machine cost but also hidden daily spending on communication, troubleshooting, and operations.

To test whether a set of dimensions is enough, apply two very different solutions to it and compare: if some dimensions can't be filled in, the dimension table still needs to be supplemented per project rather than copied from a generic checklist.

## Write the priorities first, then look at solutions

The easiest mistake with comparison is looking at solutions first and looking for reasons after. The healthy order is the reverse:

- First write down the project's priorities on each dimension — which dimensions are hard, which can concede.
- Then place the candidate solutions onto the dimensions one by one, allowing some dimensions to read "unknown."
- Finally see which solution holds the hard dimensions and takes the overall advantage on the soft ones.

Writing priorities first has a second effect: it moves the discussion from "which solution is better" to "which dimension matters." Disagreement on dimension priorities often converges more easily than preference among solutions, because disputes over dimensions can be arbitrated by problems and data, while disputes over solutions easily become disputes over taste.

## Use at a selection meeting

The typical use of cross-cutting comparison at a selection meeting is as the meeting skeleton:

- Before the meeting send the dimension checklist and candidate solutions to attendees, each pre-placing their position.
- In the meeting advance by dimension rather than by solution, discussing only one dimension per round.
- Record each dimension's conclusion on the spot, including the explicit conclusion "this dimension doesn't matter for this project."
- By the end of the meeting a filled-in comparison table naturally settles out — the body of the decision record.

A meeting advanced by dimension has a bonus benefit: objections get structured onto a specific dimension rather than a vague "I feel it's not right," and later verification knows what to verify.

## Use in review and retrospective

The comparison table is useful not only at selection time but also serves as an index in reviews and retrospectives:

- In a solution review, use the dimension checklist to check whether a new solution omitted an explanation for some dimension.
- In an incident retrospective, look back at the original comparison table to confirm how the dimension corresponding to the incident was assessed.
- At an architecture-evolution decision point, place the old and new architectures on the same dimension table and run the comparison again, rather than relying only on the felt delta.

The retrospective scenario is especially worth insisting on: putting the incident and the original assessment record side by side, the team quickly distinguishes which were judgment errors and which were premise changes. These two cases call for entirely different remediation actions, and mixing them in one retrospective often prescribes the wrong cure.

## Use to help newcomers build a map

For a newcomer getting familiar with a technical field, the hard part isn't learning concepts but building a sense of the space of trade-offs: under what conditions people pick A, under what conditions they pick B, and why no all-purpose solution exists. Cross-cutting comparison happens to supply this map:

- Have the newcomer read the dimension checklist first, then cross-reference the specific chapters in the main line to understand where each dimension comes from.
- Show the newcomer a real historical selection comparison table from the project — more intuitive than any abstract explanation.
- Have the newcomer try to independently fill in a dimension-priority table for the current project; where they fill it wrong is the understanding gap.

This use also tests the quality of the comparison table: if the table a newcomer fills differs greatly from a senior's, some dimensions' weight rationale hasn't been written down explicitly and is worth completing.

## Moments when comparison easily distorts

The comparison tool also has moments when it fails; a few common cases are worth watching:

- Team difference: another team's solution holds in another team; when placing dimensions you've mistaken their team capability for your own.
- Stage difference: a reasonable choice in the validation phase gets carried into mass production, or vice versa.
- Sample bias: reverse-engineering from a successful project's public architecture without seeing the options they eliminated or the detours they took.
- Static comparison: the table is archived after being done and never updated after load and team changes.

The common root of distortion is treating comparison as a conclusion, when comparison is only a structured way of asking. Hold that point and errors in a comparison table are easy to spot; fail to hold it and even the most precise table will skew the decision.

## Getting comparison conclusions to land

Conclusions drawn from comparison need a few companion actions to avoid evaporating:

- Write the decision record on the spot: the conclusion, the assessment of key dimensions, the rejected solutions, and the reasons for rejection.
- Assign verification tasks to uncertain dimensions, with an owner and deadline.
- Archive the comparison table in a findable location, linked to later reviews and retrospectives.
- Agree on review checkpoints to re-compare when load, team, or platform constraints change.

It's these steps that turn a comparison table from a meeting artifact into a team asset. Without these actions, every selection meeting redraws the table and every table expires with the meeting.

## Common pitfalls

- Treating the comparison table as a scorecard, assuming the highest scorer wins and ignoring that dimensions have weights and trade-offs between them.
- Reaching a conclusion first, then finding the dimensions, so comparison degenerates into retrofitting legitimacy onto a settled preference.
- Copying the dimension checklist from a generic template without supplementing or trimming it for your own project.
- Omitting the team dimension when comparing, defaulting another team's conditions for holding to be your own.
- Running comparison only once so that when premises change the table isn't updated, and stale conclusions keep supporting decisions.

The purpose of cross-cutting comparison is to make comparison honest: every solution is examined under the same set of dimensions, and every dimension is explicitly discussed about whether it matters. It doesn't decide for people, but it can ensure that when a decision is made, no one can pretend a dimension doesn't exist.
