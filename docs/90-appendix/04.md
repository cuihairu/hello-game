# How to Use the Appendix

The two fates most often befalling an appendix are being treated as the body text and read straight through again, or being treated with the idiom "keeping the box but returning the pearl" — flipping only the appendix and never touching the main line. Neither use understands the division of labor between appendix and main line. This page explains the role the appendix plays in the whole knowledge base, the suitable moments to use it, and when you should retreat from the appendix back to the main line.

## Division of labor between appendix and main line

The knowledge base's main line begins with a general theory and the problem model, then expands in turn through network access, sync and combat, execution model, service splitting, messaging and IPC, client architecture, scripting and hot update, version release, data modeling, caching and middleware, common services, operations and monetization, platform and SDK, observability, and security and risk control, finally landing on practice and retrospective. The main line's job is to build causation: each chapter explains why a class of problem takes its shape, where the constraints come from, and how the trade-offs are derived.

The appendix, by contrast, consists of game type topics, problem-domain consolidation, config pipeline, capacity scaling, and engine language tools, with a completely different way of working:

- It doesn't re-derive, only compresses: rearranging content scattered across multiple chapters under the same viewpoint.
- The appendix is mainly for review and verification; the relevant judgments still need the background and reasoning in the main line.
- It provides no answers, only comparison: every conclusion carries its own premises.

A simple metaphor: the main line is the road, the appendix is the map. You can look at the map repeatedly, but the map can't replace walking.

## When to open the appendix

The moments for using the appendix have fairly clear signals, usually when you already hold a specific problem:

- You're about to do a selection or solution comparison and need a stable set of dimensions to organize the discussion.
- You're assessing a project or competitor and want to quickly confirm the technical pressure distribution of this class of product.
- You're preparing a review, sharing, or training session and need to organize scattered content into material.
- In a retrospective you want to confirm how a similar problem happened and was handled in other projects.
- You're halfway through reading the main line and want to see the overall map first before deciding the order to read closely.

Conversely, if you have no specific problem and just want to "learn a bit," the appendix is usually not the right starting point. Compressed facts lack causal support, they're easy to read, and what remains is an illusion.

## Reviewing with a question

Reviewing with a question is the most common use; operationally a few points are worth noting:

- Write the question as one sentence, as specific as possible: "how is authority determined on the settlement path" is far better than "look at room-based games."
- When reviewing, first scan the page's structure and find the section corresponding to your question, rather than reading from top to bottom.
- When a conclusion in a section is incomprehensible, mark it — that's a place needing a return to the main line to fill in the causation.
- The result of the review should ideally land as a few lines of notes, stating the answer chain to this question inside the book.

The deliverable of review is notes, not the feeling of "having read it." The appendix page itself is someone else's compression; only when you add your own project's context does it become something reusable.

## Doing horizontal comparison before acting

Comparison is the appendix's second main use, common before selection meetings and solution reviews:

- Locate by category or problem domain and pull the relevant pages for background reading.
- Fill your project's situation on each dimension into the comparison framework; leaving it unknown is acceptable.
- Bring the comparison result into the meeting directly, as the starting point of discussion rather than its endpoint.

The difference between pre-acting comparison and improvisation on the spot is obvious: the former focuses the meeting on disagreements about dimensions, while the latter has the meeting spinning inside everyone's own memories. The appendix's value here is replacing "memory" with "paper."

## Organizing sharing and training material

The appendix's natural horizontal viewpoint makes it well suited to secondary use:

- For internal sharing, take a topic page as a skeleton and fill in instances and data from your own project.
- When onboarding newcomers, give them a topic page first to build a map, then point them to the corresponding main-line chapters for close reading.
- When building a review template, pick the constraints from problem-domain consolidation relevant to the project and organize them into a review question list.
- When archiving a retrospective, use a topic page to check the common pitfalls of similar projects and confirm the improvement items haven't missed a direction.

What these uses share is that the appendix supplies structure and the user supplies flesh. Using only the appendix without filling makes material feel hollow; using only instances without structure makes it scattered and hard to convey.

## Judging which you should read

When choosing between the main line and the appendix, you can rely on a few simple self-check questions:

- You can state the problem but not the causation: go back to the main line and read the corresponding chapter's derivation.
- You can state the causation but need a horizontal view: consult the appendix and organize material with the compressed viewpoint.
- You don't even have the outline of the problem yet: start from the problem model, first determine the dominant problem domain, then decide the direction.
- You find an appendix conclusion conflicts with your project's actual observations: return to the main line to check the premises; the difference is usually in the premises.

The underlying principle of this self-check has only one line: understanding lives in the main line, indexing in the appendix, both ends are needed, and you go toward whichever end is missing.

## Team-level usage conventions

For the appendix to keep playing a role inside a team, it usually takes a few conventions:

- Unified vocabulary: speak in the knowledge base's problem-domain vocabulary during discussion to reduce everyone talking past each other.
- Material linkage: the review template and retrospective template directly reference the corresponding topic viewpoint, so the appendix enters the workflow.
- Fixed newcomer path: write the main-line-then-topic order into onboarding material to avoid newcomers orbiting only inside the appendix.
- Periodic backfill: backfill the project's own experience and data into the team's comparison tables and checklists, growing project assets alongside the appendix.

The appendix is universal; team assets are your own. Connect the two and the knowledge base turns from reading material into a working foundation.

## What the appendix cannot replace

Finally, a few things the appendix explicitly cannot do:

- Replace understanding of the main line: labels don't hold constraints, and it's not holding constraints that causes you to miss questions at review time.
- Replace a project's specific derivation: judgments about capacity, authority, and release ultimately have to land on your own project's numbers and scenarios.
- Replace real experience: having read a hundred cases and having shouldered one failure are still two different abilities.

The appendix's honest position is that of an accelerator: accelerating review, accelerating comparison, accelerating the organization of material. Its acceleration presupposes the trunk already exists; when the trunk doesn't exist, an accelerator only makes people drift off faster.

## Common pitfalls

- Only reading the appendix, not the main line, accumulating a pile of label-shaped impressions that can't support actual decisions.
- Treating the appendix as an introductory textbook and forcibly building understanding on top of compressed facts.
- Reviewing without a specific question, leaving only vague impressions after reading with no reusable output.
- Using appendix conclusions to skip your own project's derivation, treating a generic comparison as a project answer.
- The team has no usage conventions, so the appendix stays personal browsing and never enters the workflow.

Whether the appendix is useful can be seen from whether it gets opened and cited during reviews, retrospectives, and selection. Repeated consultation usually means the checklists or comparisons inside can support actual work.
