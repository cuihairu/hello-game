# Ingress Layer

## Category

Glossary entry. Node role: the ingress layer is the first stop of traffic, responsible for connections and sessions rather than business — both extremes (a thin forwarding shell vs. one stuffed with business) make problems appear in the wrong place.

## Definition


The ingress layer is the first stop where player traffic enters the system, responsible for connections and sessions rather than business logic. In practice it is often placed at one of two extremes: either built as a thin forwarding shell, or stuffed with authentication, events, and even settlement. Both extremes make problems appear in the wrong place.

## Problem

Building the ingress layer as a thin shell leaves session management and connection governance nowhere to live; stuffing it with authentication, events, and settlement produces a single point of growth and breaks horizontal scaling. The cost of an unclear boundary is guessing the wrong layer first when troubleshooting.


### What the ingress layer usually owns

- Connection establishment, teardown, heartbeat, and timeout management.
- Login-state validation and player session binding.
- Routing messages by room, scene, or business node.
- Basic rate limiting, blacklists, and abnormal packet handling.
- Write buffering, backpressure, and link-quality observability.

These responsibilities share a common trait: they sit on the common path for all players, and they carry state. This is also the starting point for understanding every design trade-off of the ingress layer.

### Its boundary with the business layer

The ingress layer should understand connections and sessions; it should not carry heavy business logic. The reason is direct: the problems of business logic are local, and put in the wrong place they become a global failure source. Once the ingress layer slows down because of some piece of business logic, what slows down is the full volume of traffic. A common practice is to let the ingress layer do only three things — recognize connections, recognize sessions, recognize routing — and leave business judgment to the nodes behind it.

### Why it is inherently stateful

Once it maintains long-lived connections, the ingress layer has state: which connection belongs to which player, which node it is bound to, and what weak-network condition it is currently in. This is an essential difference from an ordinary stateless API gateway. Being stateful means that restart, scale-in/out, and failure migration all have to answer "what happens to existing sessions", and the common answers are session migration, reconnection compensation, or letting the client fall back to a re-entrant point.

### Why the ingress layer is easily underestimated

Because in normal times it just looks like forwarding. What really tests it are the abnormal moments: traffic surges, network jitter, batch node failures, gray release. In those moments, the ingress layer's session persistence, backpressure, and routing updates determine whether players see a brief fluctuation or a server-wide incident. No problems in normal times does not mean its design is right; it only means it has not been tested yet.

### A few common design questions around sessions

- Are session and login state the same thing, and how are expiry and renewal handled.
- On reconnection, how is the original session found back, and which step is it fall back to if it cannot be found.
- How are multi-device login and displacement adjudicated, and how is the old device notified.
- How is heartbeat timeout set, and how is mistaken kick-out under weak networks mitigated.

These questions have no single answer, but each must have a definite one. Vague session semantics eventually turns into hard-to-reproduce, hard-to-attribute problems of the "occasional disconnect" variety.

### Common variants of this term

Gateway, front server, gate, ingress server — the things they refer to are close across projects, but the boundaries may differ: some put login authentication into the ingress layer, some put push batching and packet compression in, and some also carry part of the matchmaking logic. When reading someone else's design, confirming which jobs its ingress layer takes on is far more precise than arguing over names.

### How capacity and distribution are considered

A single ingress layer has a carrying limit, usually the combined ceiling of connection count, broadcast fan-out, and packet forwarding. Beyond that, a common approach is to front multiple ingress layers, then add a routing-and-scheduling layer to spread sessions evenly. The scale-out plan is best worked out at design time, because the ingress layer is stateful, and migrating sessions afterwards costs far more than migrating business nodes.

### Typical symptoms when the ingress layer breaks

- Large numbers of players disconnect at once while the business nodes are all fine.
- Broadcast latency rises noticeably with online count, with no obvious business cause to be found.
- Restarting the ingress layer once amounts to a full server-wide disconnect, turning the release window into an incident window.
- Weak-network players are kicked out early, while genuinely abnormal traffic is forwarded normally.

These symptoms share one common direction: the problem is not in business logic but in connection and session management on the common path.

### Whether authentication belongs in the ingress layer

Putting authentication in the ingress layer versus in a separate service are both common. Putting it in the ingress layer saves one hop, but certificate and key management then gets bound to connection duties; separating it out is cleaner, at the cost of one extra hop in the login path. A common compromise is to make login authentication a separate service and have the ingress layer do only session validation and renewal.

### Common pitfalls

- Building it as a pure forwarding shell, leaving session information scattered across business nodes so that reconnection and migration have no starting point.
- Stuffing authentication, event logic, and even settlement into the ingress layer, making the common path carry local risk.
- Testing only normal connections and not disconnect/reconnect, half-open connections, or backpressure, only to discover after launch that session recovery was never designed.
- Equating the ingress layer with a stateless gateway and rolling-restarting it on a stateless-service assumption, manufacturing a full server-wide disconnect.
