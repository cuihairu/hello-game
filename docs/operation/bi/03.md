# Economy, Balance, and Risk Control Analysis

## Category

operation / bi subtree. Node positioning: economy, balance, and risk control — three kinds of analysis tracking three sides of the same system (are resources and behaviors controllable) — flow, range, anomaly — cannot be split apart.

## Definition


Economy analysis, numerical balance, and risk control analysis are often assigned to different roles: designers look at balance, operations look at revenue, the security team looks at anomalies. Yet in long-running projects these three are actually all tracking different sides of the same system — whether resources and behaviors still remain within a controllable range.

## Problem

When the three kinds of analysis sit in three different roles, economy looks at totals but not flows, balance relies on subjective feel, and risk control only watches cheat rates — the conclusions contradict each other and pass blame to each other. The resource faucet and sink ledger, numerical ranges, and anomalous behavior are three readings of the same ledger; viewed separately, each misses half.


### Why these three kinds of analysis cannot be split apart

As long as player behavior affects resource distribution, growth speed, and social competition, these three kinds of analysis are naturally coupled:

- An economy anomaly may come from runaway numerical faucet, or from cheating or a black market
- Paying structure volatility may come from campaign design, or from account washing, refund arbitrage, or proxy top-up
- A drop in participation for a given mechanic may be a balance problem, or a deviation in the reward structure

If the team completely separates these analyses, the situation "everyone sees a local anomaly but no one can explain the overall change" keeps recurring.

### Economy analysis looks not at totals, but at flow

Looking only at total revenue, total consumption, and total holdings is usually not enough. More valuable questions in economy analysis include:

- Which kind of resource is continuously inflating or deflating
- Is the resource structure gap between high-value users and ordinary users widening
- Has a given campaign or gift pack changed the faucet and sink balance of a key currency
- Is a scarce item starting to concentrate abnormally in a few accounts

In other words, economy analysis cares not about whether the surface numbers look good, but about whether the resource distribution has deviated from product expectations.

### Balance analysis cannot rely only on subjective feel

Many projects doing numerical balance easily leave the discussion at "feels too grindy", "feels too hard", "feels like the numbers collapsed". These judgments are certainly important, but without behavioral data to back them up, adjustments easily turn into a pendulum:

- Seeing churn and blindly adding benefits, hollowing out the core challenge
- Seeing paying drop and adding promotions, breaking the price anchor
- Seeing high-end players complain and raising the ceiling, further leaving the mid and low tiers behind

More effective balance analysis usually looks at both:

- Tiered pass rates and failure rates
- Completion times for key progression lines
- Participation depth and abandonment points of core mechanics
- Growth efficiency differences across paying tiers

The point of doing this is to ground "experience judgments" in observable facts rather than letting emotions override each other.

### Why risk control analysis cannot only watch cheat rates

Risk control analysis is not just about catching cheats; it also looks at how anomalous behavior changes economy and operating data. For example:

- Resource farming scripts amplify currency inflation, dragging down monetization and leaderboard credibility
- Studios doing mass account-raising pollute new-user, retention, and recall data
- Refund arbitrage and proxy top-up create fake revenue peaks
- Group-control scripts make some campaigns look highly participated while actually delivering no real value

So risk control analysis should share a data foundation with economy analysis, such as asset transaction records, order transaction records, login behavior, device fingerprints, and social propagation chains. Otherwise risk control can only see "suspicious", not "what was affected".

### A more practical analysis structure

A more effective approach is to put all three kinds of analysis into a "facts -> deviation -> cause -> action" framework:

- Facts: which key metrics underwent anomalous change
- Deviation: where it deviates relative to history, same-tier users, experiment groups, or a target range
- Cause: is it a version adjustment, campaign spend, economy structure change, or anomalous behavior
- Action: should you adjust numbers, adjust spend, adjust campaigns, add restrictions, or go straight into risk control handling

The benefit is that analysis results connect naturally to operating actions rather than stopping at describing phenomena.

### Common Mistakes

- Economy analysis looks only at totals, not tiers, regions, or flows
- Balance adjustments look only at top-end feedback, not real behavioral data
- Risk control analysis runs in isolation, without cross-validating against paying, economy, and campaign results
- After finding an anomaly, only a conclusion with no follow-up action chain and validation mechanism

The truly mature state of economy, balance, and risk control analysis is not having more reports, but the team being able to distinguish "normal fluctuation" from "structural shift" and knowing who should use what means to pull the system back into the controllable range.
