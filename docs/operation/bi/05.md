# AI in Operations, Analysis, and Risk Control

## Category

operation / bi subtree. Node positioning: the realistic landing spots and boundaries of AI on the operations side — aiding explanation rather than replacing judgment, with particular caution in risk control scenarios involving assets and punishment.

## Definition


AI in operations is most easily overestimated and most easily misused. The reason is simple: operations and analysis contain many text, classification, prediction, and decision-support scenarios that look well suited to handing to a model; but at the same time these scenarios often involve assets, user experience, bans and punishments, and operating judgment, and cannot accept "close enough".

## Problem

Operations scenarios largely involve assets, user experience, bans and punishments, and operating judgment, and cannot accept "close enough"; overestimating AI wires unexplainable outputs straight into the decision chain. Fill in definitions and closed loops first, and AI outputs have an anchor to check against — reverse the order and misuse arrives before value does.


### First distinguish what AI is suitable for and what it is not

In operations, analysis, and risk control, AI is better suited to these positions:

- Aiding classification: organizing customer support tickets, player feedback, campaign reviews, and report content
- Aiding summarization: automatically generating daily report drafts, campaign retrospective first drafts, and candidate explanations for anomalous fluctuations
- Aiding retrieval: helping operations and analysts quickly locate relevant information among large volumes of reports, documents, and rules
- Aiding prediction: churn risk, recall opportunity, paying tendency, and anomalous behavior scoring
- Aiding review: first-pass screening of text, chat, announcements, and report content

It is not suitable to directly and independently take on these high-risk actions:

- Directly deciding bans or punishments
- Directly changing prices, issuing rewards, or executing batched reach
- Generating external announcements and customer support conclusions without human confirmation
- Replacing accounting rules and compliance rules for the final call

In other words, AI is better as an "assistant" and "candidate generator" than as an unconstrained final arbiter.

### The most realistic landing spots on the operations side

For most teams, what produces value first is not complex agents, but placing AI in high-repetition, text-heavy, low-final-responsibility steps. For example:

- Automatically summarizing the themes of player negative reviews to help operations quickly locate version pain points
- Summarizing anomalous metric changes during a campaign and generating a retrospective draft for an analyst to check
- Automatically tagging customer support tickets to first route "payment issues", "asset anomalies", and "cheat reports"
- Drafting multiple versions of recall copy, campaign titles, and announcement summaries

The shared characteristic of these scenarios is that the cost of AI getting it wrong is controllable, and there is a human review closed loop.

### What is truly valuable on the analysis side is "aiding explanation", not "replacing judgment"

Many people expect AI to automatically read all the reports and then tell the team "why the data dropped". What is more feasible in reality is having it take part in a few narrow tasks:

- Given a metric anomaly, automatically listing the possibly related versions, campaigns, and configuration changes
- Retrieving similar cases from historical retrospectives and the knowledge base
- Helping analysts generate preliminary SQL, chart descriptions, or retrospective outlines

The value here lies in speed, not in replacing analysts. Because operating judgment ultimately still depends on context — channel changes, version issues, external public opinion, temporary operational actions — content like this is hard for a model to derive correct conclusions from on its own.

### Why risk control scenarios need even more caution

When AI does risk control, the most common temptation is "let the model automatically detect anomalies and automatically punish". This step is high risk, because risk control must not only catch accurately but also explain clearly, allow appeals, and be re-reviewable. A more reasonable approach is usually:

- Use the model for risk scoring and suspect ranking
- Use explicit rules for hard constraints, such as amount thresholds, frequency limits, and blacklist hits
- Use humans or a controlled process for final punishment confirmation

Especially when it comes to account bans, asset recovery, and compliance review, explainability and auditability usually matter more than raw hit rate.

### What should be filled in first before bringing in AI

Many teams rush to bring in AI while foundational data, knowledge management, and process governance are all underdeveloped, and the result merely automates the chaos. More realistic prerequisites usually include:

- Foundational data definitions relatively stable
- Operations rules, ticket types, and punishment flows have structured definitions
- Key actions have human review, leave a trail, and have feedback loops
- There is a way to evaluate what the AI output actually helped with and where it went wrong

Without this foundation, AI easily becomes "generating a lot of plausible-sounding words" but cannot truly be embedded into production flows.

### Common Mistakes

- Treating AI as an all-purpose operations middle platform, hoping it replaces reports, analysis, customer support, and risk control in one step
- Skipping human review in high-risk scenarios and wiring model output directly into business actions
- No evaluation standard, judging whether AI is effective by subjective feel alone
- Ignoring data governance and knowledge governance, so the model's input itself is unreliable

The true value of AI in operations, analysis, and risk control is not automatically making decisions for the team, but front-loading the large volume of repetitive, low-structure, time-consuming auxiliary work so people can put their energy into the more critical judgment, governance, and accountability.
