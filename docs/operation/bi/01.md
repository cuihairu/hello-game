# Operations and Monetization Fundamentals

## Category

operation / bi subtree. Node positioning: operations is not a shop page but a lifecycle management system — look at campaigns, gift packs, seasons, and recall within the same resource flow and pricing strategy. Overview of the bi subtree.

## Definition


The operating capability of a long-running game is fundamentally answering one question: how does a project repeatedly create content consumption, paying conversion, and user return without continuously damaging user experience and system stability. Campaigns, gift packs, seasons, recall, pricing strategy, spend linkage, and version cadence — these actions that look independent actually all act on the same lifecycle management system.

## Problem

Understanding operations as "configuring campaigns, listing gift packs" misses its engineering essence: all operational actions act on the same lifecycle system, and when definitions are unclear or the loop is missing, three revenue reports can differ by 90,000. Operations becoming a technical problem is not a metaphor — when definitions, processes, or the data pipeline fail anywhere, the operating judgment distorts as a whole.


### Why operations becomes a technical problem

When a product first launches, the team usually focuses more on day-one retention, server stability, and core gameplay feedback. When the project enters stable operations, the situation changes rapidly:

- Retention is no longer determined by gameplay alone, but is also affected by campaign cadence, content supply, and reach efficiency
- Paying is no longer just a shop feature, but is bound to pricing tiers, resource scarcity, and user segmentation
- Version rollout is no longer just an R&D cadence, but part of the operating cycle, channel windows, and campaign scheduling

At this point, if operational actions still rely on manual spreadsheets, ad-hoc SQL, and multiple inconsistent backends, the team quickly hits two bottlenecks:

- Operational actions are slow to execute and cannot keep up with the cadence
- Operational actions are uncontrollable; even when done, you cannot retrospectively verify the true effect

So operational capability must be systematized, not "have a backend and call it done".

### Monetization is not the shop page, but resource flow and pricing strategy

Many teams' understanding of monetization stops at the top-up entry, monthly cards, and gift pack configuration. More accurately, monetization is an amplifier of the entire resource cycle. It affects:

- The speed at which players acquire resources
- The perceived value of scarce assets
- The appeal of campaign rewards
- The cadence of version goals and pay points

Without a stable resource faucet and sink model, monetization actions quickly go wrong. For example:

- A gift pack's pricing looks like decent conversion, but actually overdrafts the consumption of later versions
- A campaign rebate lifts short-term revenue but destroys currency scarcity
- New user packs and returning user recall packs have muddled definitions, interrupting the long-term nurture of high-value users

So monetization design cannot look only at "what to sell", but also "why sell it now", "what impact it has on the economy loop", and "how to reclaim or correct it after failure".

### What capabilities an operations system usually includes at minimum

A mature operational control plane usually includes the following categories of systems:

- Campaign configuration and scheduling: controls opening, closing, grouping, rules, and rewards
- Monetization backend: manages the shop, gift packs, payment campaigns, first top-up, rebates, and pricing strategy
- User segmentation and audience management: segments by paying, activity, churn risk, and lifecycle stage
- Version and content scheduling: turns R&D output content into an external cadence
- Retrospective system: forms a unified retrospective view of campaigns, versions, and monetization actions

These systems do not have to be fully covered by one platform, but they must share as consistent a data definition and approval process as possible, otherwise operational efficiency gets repeatedly offset.

### Design the closed loop first, then talk about flexibility

The most common construction pitfall of an operations backend is interpreting "flexible" as "configurable for everything". The result is a backend with more and more fields but worse and worse actual controllability. The more reasonable order is:

1. First define the action types, e.g. opening a campaign, changing a price, issuing rewards, doing recall
2. Then define each action type's inputs, scope of impact, approval, and rollback method
3. Only then consider how much configuration freedom is needed

The benefit of designing this way is that the operations system naturally carries governance capability. For example, a gift pack repricing action is not just changing a number; it should be accompanied by:

- Effective time
- Target audience
- Price version
- Approval record
- Observation metrics
- Rollback plan

If none of these exist, the so-called "flexible configuration" just transfers risk to production.

### Common organizational forms and their costs

### Business teams each maintain their own backend

The advantage is fast response to requirements and features close to the project. The disadvantage is inconsistent definitions, mutually incompatible data, and experience that cannot be reused. Once there are many projects, campaigns, gift packs, reach, and retrospectives all get rebuilt repeatedly.

### Platform team builds a unified operations middle platform

The advantage is good unification, strong governance capability, and reusable reports and reach. The disadvantage is that if the platform's expressiveness is insufficient, business teams quickly bypass the platform and go back to side-channel scripts and temporary configuration.

### Platform foundational capabilities plus a project extension layer

This is the more realistic balance point for most teams. Build campaign scheduling, audience segmentation, unified reports, permission auditing, push channels, and the like as shared capabilities, and leave special rules, special gift packs, and project-specific metrics at the business layer. This approach requires very clear interface boundaries, otherwise the platform and the project will keep wrangling.

### Common Mistakes

- Only campaign and shop backends, with no unified retrospective or metric observation capability
- Highly flexible monetization configuration, but no approval, experiment design, or rollback mechanism
- Completely separating the operations cadence from the R&D version, so campaign capability forever lags behind the content plan
- Only chasing short-term revenue, without evaluating the resource cycle, user experience, and long-term paying structure

The truly mature state of operations and monetization is not how many backend pages there are, but whether the team can steadily take action, look at results, and correct course, and knows where each growth came from and what it cost.

### Practical case: an operations definition investigation where "three revenue reports differ by 90,000"

> This case is a demo example (not drawn from a real project), with numbers rounded and simplified. It takes this page's "Why operations becomes a technical problem", "Monetization is not the shop page, but resource flow and pricing strategy", "What capabilities an operations system usually includes at minimum", "Design the closed loop first, then talk about flexibility", and "Common organizational forms and their costs" into deep water: a revenue mismatch during an anniversary preheat week lights up four gaps at once — fragmented definitions, absent approval, missing rollback, and no way to retrospect.

**Background and symptoms**: A long-running project enters stable operations; the anniversary preheat week pushes **6** operational actions in a row — **3** gift pack repricings, **1** login campaign, **2** recall pushes — all going live directly by operations in their respective backends, with **0** approval records and **0** rollback plans. At the day 3 close, the three revenue reports do not match: the operations backend reports **1.28M**, the BI dashboard reports **1.21M**, finance settlement reports **1.19M**, a max gap of **90,000**. Operations says revenue grew, data says it did not grow that much, finance says less arrived; the preheat week's **5** days of transaction records were logged once per definition across three definitions — **15** versions of numbers in total — and the retrospective meeting ran **2** hours with no conclusion. On day 4 it was further discovered that the third repricing changed only the listed price and not the settlement price — listed price **248** yuan, settlement still **198** yuan, a gap of **50** yuan/order — the manual fix dragged on for **4** hours, and **1,300** extra orders were sold inside the window.

### Step 1: Symptoms and definitions — first separate "how much was collected" from "how much was logged"

Unified definitions: take finance arrival as the baseline, reconcile transaction by transaction across the three definitions of "payment time, refund attribution, cutoff point", first logging two items:

- **Three reports**: operations backend **1.28M**, BI dashboard **1.21M**, finance settlement **1.19M** — max gap **90,000**
- **Bridge**: finance 1.19M + refunds in transit **20,000** = BI 1.21M; BI 1.21M + cross-day cutoff **70,000** = operations 1.28M — **20,000** + **70,000** = **90,000**, the gap closes

The three reports are not three embezzlements, they are three sets of definitions: finance deducts refunds on arrival, BI logs refunds but has not deducted them yet, operations counts by order placement and never asks about refunds — the same business was logged three times.

### Step 2: Layered attribution — from revenue reconciliation to an operations chain ledger

```text
动作账   预热周 6 个动作（改价 3、活动 1、推送 2）全部直发生效，审批 0 条，回退方案 0 份
口径账   三表差 9 万 = 退款在途 2 万 + 跨天截单 7 万（119 + 2 = 121，121 + 7 = 128）
回滚账   改价只落一半：标价 248、结算 198，差 50 元/单；回改拖 4 小时，多卖 1300 单、少收 6.5 万
复盘账   5 天 × 3 口径 = 15 版流水，复盘会 2 小时无结论——没有统一复盘系统
```

Ledger convergence: the **90,000** gap is the definition ledger, the **65,000** loss is the rollback ledger — both entries hang on the same action ledger: the "flexible configuration" with **0** approvals and **0** rollback plans is exactly what this page calls "the so-called 'flexible configuration' just transfers risk to production".

### Step 3: Root cause — definitions, approval, rollback, and retrospective, four gaps exposed at once

Contrast with this page's "Design the closed loop first, then talk about flexibility": of the three action steps only the last was taken — configuration freedom was maxed out, while the two steps "first define the action types" and "then define each action type's inputs, scope of impact, approval, and rollback method" were missing; of the six-piece set that accompanies a gift pack repricing only the price version landed, with effective time, target audience, approval record, observation metrics, and rollback plan — five items — all empty. Contrast with "What capabilities an operations system usually includes at minimum": of the five categories of systems on the page, campaign configuration, monetization backend, user segmentation, and version scheduling are all present, and only the "retrospective system" is missing — **15** versions of transaction records with nowhere to converge, and "operational actions are uncontrollable; even when done, you cannot retrospectively verify the true effect" comes true word for word; the page requires them to "share as consistent a data definition and approval process as possible", yet here there are three reports with three definitions and six actions with zero approvals — every team stopped at "have a backend and call it done". Contrast with "Common organizational forms and their costs": it hits "business teams each maintain their own backend" with its "inconsistent definitions, mutually incompatible data", and it also hits the middle platform's warning — under platform constraints business "quickly bypasses the platform and goes back to side-channel scripts and temporary configuration". The first two items of "Common Mistakes" both hit: only campaign and shop backends with no unified retrospective or metric observation capability; highly flexible monetization configuration, but no approval, experiment design, or rollback mechanism.

### Step 4: Handling and backfill — first unify the definitions, then bring the actions into the closed loop

The fix has two parts. Emergency: at **T+0** midday the settlement price was corrected (**248** yuan effective), **1,300** orders × **50** yuan = **65,000** was charged to the campaign loss ledger, closing the loop on the configuration error's accounting; at **T+1** the three-report definitions were unified — refunds counted into the payment day's ledger, cutoff unified to **24**:**00**, all three reports drawing from one source — the **90,000** gap was split open and flattened on the spot (**20,000** + **70,000**), and **15** versions of transaction records were merged into **1** version for the retrospective. Three institutional steps: rearrange the backend in this page's order — first register the action types (opening a campaign, changing a price, issuing rewards, doing recall), then lock in each action type's inputs, scope of impact, approval, and rollback method, and only then release configuration freedom; gift pack repricing must have the six-piece set (effective time, target audience, price version, approval record, observation metrics, rollback plan) complete before it can be submitted; shared capabilities such as campaign scheduling, audience segmentation, unified reports, permission auditing, and push channels are pulled into the platform layer, while project-specific packs and metrics stay in the extension layer. Review: the next month all **6** repricings left an approval trail (**6**/**6**), rollback plans were **6**/**6** complete, and one price increase lacking a rollback plan was rejected at approval (**1** time); the max daily gap across the three reports converged to within **10,000**.

**Backfill checklist**:

| Landing point | Action | Backfill location |
|-----|------|--------|
| Definition unification | Refunds into the payment-day ledger, cutoff unified to 24:00, three reports from one source | What capabilities an operations system usually includes at minimum |
| Action closed loop | Action three-step first, gift pack repricing six-piece set complete before submission | Design the closed loop first, then talk about flexibility |
| Retrospective system | Unified retrospective view of campaigns, versions, and monetization actions, 15 versions merged into 1 | Why operations becomes a technical problem |
| Organizational convergence | Scheduling, audience, reports, and permission auditing pulled into the platform layer, specific metrics left in the extension layer | Common organizational forms and their costs |

### Three lessons from the case

1. **Definition unification comes before definition disputes** — the three reports' gap does not live in the data department; it lives in nobody ever having settled a single unification across the three definitions of payment, refund, and cutoff.
2. **Flexibility must ship with a closed loop** — "flexible configuration" without approval records or rollback plans put a **50** yuan/order configuration error directly on sale in production for **4** hours.
3. **Retrospective capability must exist before the actions do** — a retrospective meeting that cannot draw a conclusion from **15** versions of transaction records is missing not time, but the one "retrospective system" that stands alone among the page's five capabilities.
