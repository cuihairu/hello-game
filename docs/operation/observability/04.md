# Debug, Fault Localization and Postmortem

## Category

operation / observability subtree. Node positioning: the bottleneck of localization efficiency is in the process, not the tools — stop-the-bleeding versus localization order, preserving the evidence chain, ownership of postmortem improvements and a blameless atmosphere.

## Definition


After debug tools, log platforms and distributed tracing are all in place, fault localization can still be slow. Because the bottleneck of localization efficiency is usually not the tools but the process: in what order to rule things out, who is investigating, where the evidence is, and when to stop the bleeding. The essence of localization capability is narrowing the unknown quickly to a range small enough, and depositing each investigation into experience the team can reuse later.

## Problem

Debug tools, log platforms and distributed tracing are all in place, yet localization is still slow: no agreement on what order to rule things out, emergency actions destroying the scene, and postmortems turning into timeline recitation with no ownership for the improvements. Localization is narrowing the unknown quickly into a controllable range and depositing each investigation into reusable team experience — neither of these happens automatically.


### Stop the bleeding first or localize first

This is the first and most important decision in every fault. Three common bases for judging:

- How large is the blast radius: a whole-server outage and a localized feature failure are completely different in urgency.
- Is the recovery move clear: actions such as rolling back, restarting or switching traffic are usually executed first if they are likely to work.
- Is the scene evidence perishable: in-memory state, connection state, queue contents — a restart erases them.

By experience, when the blast radius is large and the recovery move is clear, stop the bleeding first — in the first ten-plus minutes of a production incident the goal is usually not to understand why, but to let players play again. Rolling back a version that turns out afterwards not to be the root cause usually costs far less than a continuing fault; conversely, when the blast radius is small, the recovery action itself is risky, or the scene is unique, gathering evidence before acting pays off more.

Both directions have a cost; the difficulty is not the principle but the judgment in the moment. What improves judgment in the moment is the runbook prepared in peacetime: which symptoms map to which stop-the-bleeding actions, worked through in advance so that on the day there is no need to keep oscillating between stopping the bleeding and gathering evidence.

### How to narrow the problem surface

The essence of localization is making hypotheses falsifiable: each investigation step either rules out a segment of the chain or confirms one. Effective practices usually include:

- Layered bisection: client, access layer, logic layer, storage layer — first establish which segment the problem falls in, then subdivide downward.
- Comparison: differences between a normal instance and an abnormal one, before a change and after it, between an abnormal region and a normal one.
- Timeline: what releases, configuration changes, scale-ups and data corrections happened around the fault's starting point.
- Reversing from symptoms: mapping the phenomena players saw, along the main link, onto the possible services and dependencies.

Investigation direction jumping back and forth is usually because hypotheses were never recorded and never formally ruled out — one layer gets checked by three different groups while another gets nobody at all. Writing "what has been ruled out, what is still to be confirmed" into the incident channel is a localization habit that costs almost nothing, yet it very often turns multi-person investigation from mutual interference into parallel progress.

### Version and configuration are high-frequency suspects

In timeline investigation, the two most common trigger sources are versions and configuration. When an anomaly appears right after a release, suspicion naturally concentrates on the change; more easily overlooked are configuration and operations actions — an event configured with the wrong value, a scheduled task firing early, a switch left on, an operator changing a parameter in the admin console with no trace. A whole-server anomaly chased through code usually turns up nothing, and it ends with the discovery of one configuration value — nearly everyone on a long-running project has this experience.

So a fixed action when narrowing the problem surface usually includes reconciling every change source in the last twenty-four hours: code releases, configuration pushes, event starts, data corrections, scale-up operations — not one can be missed. On platforms where configuration changes are not recorded, this step degrades into asking people one by one, which is also why configuration needs an audit trail.

### Emergency actions destroy the scene

Stopping the bleeding and gathering evidence often conflict: a restart recovers the service and also destroys the in-memory state; kicking players to reconnect stops the bleeding and also clears the session context from when the error occurred. A common compromise is to gather evidence before performing a destructive action — capture thread stacks, memory snapshots, key counters and queue contents, spending a few extra minutes to preserve a scene that can still be seen during the later postmortem.

Another record-keeping duty that is easily overlooked is the operation stream during the emergency: who performed what action at what time, and with what result. When piecing together the timeline afterwards, if this information exists only in the memory of the people involved, the postmortem degrades into mutual accusation, and the timeline is exactly the most basic material in a postmortem.

### The communication structure during localization

Once a fault exceeds the scope one person can handle, the communication structure starts to affect speed. A common arrangement is: one commander responsible for assignment and decisions, handlers focused on their own chains, and one person dedicated to outward information sync. Without that structure, the common symptoms are dozens of people in the group channel all investigating at once, key information drowned in small talk, and conclusions from two directions fighting back and forth.

Outward sync deserves special emphasis. The player community and the customer support channel will not wait for internal localization to finish, and the information vacuum of "players are already fighting in the forums while the company is still silent" damages reputation as much as the fault itself. Outward notification does not need to wait for the root cause: scope of impact, what is being done, and when the next conclusion is expected — that content can and should be updated continuously.

### A postmortem is not timeline recitation

The value of a postmortem lies in answering three progressively deeper questions:

- What is the root cause: not the immediate trigger, but the condition that allowed that trigger to become an incident.
- Why was it not caught earlier: why the alert did not ring, why the test did not cover it, why the review did not stop it.
- How to prevent it from happening again: what the corresponding concrete change is, and who completes it by when.

A postmortem that only writes "what happened at what time, recovered at what time" records history but produces no improvement. The distinction between immediate trigger and root cause especially deserves emphasis: the trigger was a release that set off a slow query, while the root cause may be that the release process has no slow-query review step. Handling only the trigger means the same kind of fault will come back wearing a different trigger.

### Improvements need ownership and deadlines

If a postmortem's output has no owner and deadline, it usually stops at the meeting minutes. Wording such as "strengthen monitoring" or "raise the level of attention" cannot be accepted; the common way to write remediation is: who, by when, completes what verifiable change. Looking at trends across faults matters just as much — if the same kind of fault appears a second time, the previous improvement never landed, which is already a process problem and not a technical one.

A plain standard for whether a postmortem worked is to look back half a year later at the completion rate of improvement items and the repeat rate of the same kind of fault, rather than how thoroughly the postmortem meeting went.

### What if it cannot be reproduced

A large share of production faults cannot be stably reproduced: they depend on a specific timing, a specific data distribution, a specific sequence of operations. The common choices then come in tiers — reconstruct enough information afterwards from logs and metrics; parameterize the suspected conditions and try them in batches in a test environment; or accept that it cannot be reproduced for now and first narrow the trigger conditions with defensive fixes. Insisting on reproduce-first-then-fix keeps the fault hanging for a long time; acting without any reproduction easily fixes in the wrong direction. The usual approach is to push in parallel: keep collecting production evidence on one side, and implement low-risk defensive measures based on existing information on the other, then do the root-cause-level fix once the reproduction conditions are in place.

### Completeness of the fault discovery channel

There is a question in postmortems that is easily skipped: who found this fault first? If the answer is a player or customer support, monitoring coverage has a gap, and that gap itself is an improvement item. A healthy signal is that the vast majority of faults are found by monitoring before players do, with the gap between discovery time and the fault's starting point continuously shrinking. Recording the discovery channel in every postmortem is what lets the blind spots of the monitoring system keep converging.

### How to run a postmortem meeting effectively

Postmortem meeting formats vary widely, but effective ones usually share a few things: the timeline is assembled before the meeting, so the meeting spends no time reconstructing facts but goes straight into analysis; the people involved give a complete account of the information they saw at the time and the basis of their decisions, because questions such as "why did you not roll back then" only make sense against the information that person had at the time; and the meeting is chaired by someone with no direct stake in the incident, which makes it easier to pull the discussion back to the system rather than to individuals.

The meeting's output has to be confirmed during the meeting: improvement items, owners, deadlines, verification method. Postmortems assembled after the meeting disbands tend to have their measures softened. Another piece of experience is controlling the size — with too many participants the discussion easily turns into performance, and the people who actually know the details end up without the chance to speak in full.

### Why a blameless atmosphere affects information quality

The cultural tone of a postmortem directly affects the quality of the evidence. If the postmortem points at "who did it", at the next fault people will instinctively hide information, downplay the impact, and leave details unfavourable to themselves out of the report — and the organization loses exactly the learning material it needs most. Aiming the question at the system and the process, so the people involved are willing to tell the whole story clearly, is what lets a postmortem stand on real evidence.

This is not to say that responsibility does not exist, but that accountability and learning from the fault are best run separately. Mixing them usually means neither gets done well: learning is disturbed by defensive posture, and accountability is inaccurate because the information is distorted.

### Letting localization experience settle into assets

Every fault should deposit two kinds of reusable assets. The first is the localization path of this fault, organized into a checklist or runbook so that the next time a similar symptom appears you can follow it directly instead of guessing from zero. The second is new monitoring points: anything found this time only by a human digging through logs is usually worth turning into a formal metric or a ready-made dashboard, so the same kind of problem is seen directly next time.

Depositing also needs somewhere to land: checklists and runbooks that never enter the on-call process and newcomer training are archived once written, which amounts to no depositing at all. Periodically verifying in drills or later faults whether these documents still match the current system is also part of the depositing work.

Without depositing, localization capability forever depends on a few people familiar with the system. Those people will eventually take leave, transfer and leave the company; team-level localization capability can only be carried by process and documents, not by memory.

### Common pitfalls

- Insisting on "localize first, then act", letting a fault that could be stopped quickly keep getting worse.
- Not recording hypotheses and ruled-out items during investigation, so the direction jumps back and forth and several people duplicate work.
- Not gathering evidence before destructive emergency actions, so there is no scene left to examine afterwards.
- Writing the postmortem as an incident running account, never answering why it was not caught earlier.
- Improvements with no owner and deadline, so the postmortem loop stops on paper.
- A postmortem atmosphere that points at individuals, so next time nobody is willing to provide complete information.
- Leaving an unreproducible fault hanging indefinitely, doing neither low-risk defensive narrowing nor the remediation of after-the-fact evidence gathering.

Fault localization capability is a combination of process, culture and evidence system, with tools being only one link. A team's real progress does not show in how brilliantly one fault was handled, but in the repeat rate of the same kind of fault falling, localization time shortening, and relying less and less on some individual stepping forward.
