# Specialized Observability and Tick Metrics

## Category

operation / observability subtree. Node positioning: the game main link's own vital signs — Tick time, frame-level metrics, matchmaking / room metrics, the cardinality trap and drill-down paths.

## Definition


General monitoring answers "are the machines and services healthy", while the core question of game observability is "is this player's match experience healthy". These two layers are often out of step: the monitoring panel is all green, and the player community is already complaining about stutter and disconnects. When that mismatch appears, what is missing is usually not more machine metrics but observation signals designed specifically around the game's main link.

## Problem

Monitoring panels all green while the player community is already cursing the stutter — this is the typical shape of general metrics and player experience drifting apart: with normal CPU, players still stutter. What is missing is signals designed specifically around the main link (Tick / frame / matchmaking / disconnect-reconnect), plus governance of metric cardinality and drill-down paths.


### The worldview of general metrics and the mismatch with games

The general metric system is built on a worldview of resources and requests: CPU, memory, disk and network describe resources; QPS, latency and error rate describe requests. That worldview works well enough for web services whose unit is the request, but the core of the game main link is neither resource nor request — it is rhythm: logic frames advance in fixed steps, broadcasts dispatch per frame, matchmaking evolves through a queue. Rhythm problems are invisible to both resource metrics and request metrics.

### Why players still stutter when CPU is normal

In scenes where monitoring is normal but players are affected, the common causes fall into a few categories:

- Spikes are flattened by averages: average CPU is only 40%, but a few frames spike into the hundreds of milliseconds, and what the player perceives comes exactly from the worst of those frames.
- Waiting is counted as idle: the logic thread spends most of its time waiting on locks, waiting on disk writes, waiting for the broadcast queue to drain — resource usage is not high, but progress is very slow.
- The problem is downstream on the link: the server is entirely fine, the client has render backlog or frame-sync lag, and server monitoring can never see it.
- Perception comes from the distribution, not the mean: one room stuttering and the server-wide average stuttering are two different things, and metrics aggregated to the whole server dilute the local problem away.

A player's judgment of smoothness is close to "is the worst moment acceptable", while general monitoring is naturally good at describing average state. That perceptual gap is the reason specialized observability exists.

### The main link's own vital signs

The coverage of dedicated metrics around the main link commonly includes:

- Rhythm: distribution of logic frame time, the share of frames over budget, frame-compensation count, maximum catch-up steps in a single frame, and the count of times default input was used because of timeout.
- Sync: distribution of the frame difference between client and server, broadcast queue backlog depth, message end-to-end latency.
- Rooms: number of active rooms, distribution of players per room, room creation and destruction rate, average room lifetime.
- Matchmaking: distribution of matchmaking wait time, matchmaking success rate, the share of players cancelling matchmaking.
- Recovery: disconnect rate, reconnect success rate, catch-up time after reconnect, and the time and success rate of snapshot recovery.

The shared feature of these metrics is that each maps directly onto a player-describable experience problem. Frame time distribution corresponds to "stutter", frame difference distribution to "out of sync", and catch-up time after reconnect to "spinning for a long time". Building specialized observability is essentially translating player language into metric language, and building the translation table in advance rather than inventing it one entry at a time after complaints arrive.

### Why frame-level metrics deserve separate emphasis

Metrics of the Tick system have one special property: they are the common evidence source for almost every sync problem. Slow reconnects, skills arriving half a beat late, replays that do not line up, dispute reports on reports — pushed to the end, they often land on frame-level facts such as "one frame went over budget", "catch-up jumped too many steps", or "the frame difference widens in this range". So mature teams usually treat frame-level metrics as the foundation of main-link observation rather than a nice-to-have: logic frame time is read by distribution and quantiles, over-budget by share and by concentrated time windows, and frame compensation and catch-up by count and steps. Without this layer, every sync problem eventually degrades into guessing from a recording by eye.

### Design points for matchmaking and room metrics

Matchmaking and rooms are two observation points that are easy to instrument wrong. The difficulty with matchmaking is that wait time is a "truncated distribution": players who give up matchmaking have no wait-end time, so counting only the duration of successful matches deletes the least satisfied group of players from the data. So matchmaking-wait observation usually has to look at three faces at once — the distribution of successful durations, the cancel rate and the timeout rate — to reconstruct the real experience.

The difficulty with room metrics is that "average" is almost meaningless. The distributions of players per room, room duration and broadcast volume are often highly skewed, and the mean gets pulled away by a few extreme rooms. The more common approach is to read quantiles and bucketed counts: what share of rooms exceeds the designed capacity, how the share of longer rooms is rising, which gameplay range has abnormal broadcast volume. The trend of room count is itself a capacity signal, and its turning point often appears before resource alerts do.

### Using comparison windows to validate metrics

Another common use of dedicated metrics is ruling out noise. Players complain "it stutters in the evening" — is it that way all day, or only in certain time windows? Disconnect rate rises during an event — is the event's gameplay itself heavy, or did it happen to stack with the evening peak? Looking only at the curve of the abnormal window is hard to answer; putting the abnormal window side by side with the same period historically and with normal windows usually makes the problem reveal itself.

This is also why dedicated metrics should cover everyday operation: without a daily baseline, data from an abnormal window has nothing to compare against, and you can only judge by feel whether "this counts as abnormal".

### Cross-validation between gameplay-layer metrics and business data

Gameplay-layer metrics and the business data on the operations side often validate each other: rooms with severe frame difference usually see in-match churn and reports rise in step; worsening matchmaking wait is usually followed by a swing in retention after matchmaking completes. Reading technical-side dedicated metrics together with business-side behaviour data lets the impact of technical problems be quantified in business language, and lets business anomalies be reverse-inferred into what kind of observation the technical side should add. The two data chains are collected independently and their conclusions match, which raises the credibility of both.

Dedicated metrics usually split into three layers: the system layer describes processes and resources, the service layer describes interfaces and queues, and the gameplay layer describes game semantics such as frames, rooms and matchmaking. Where to hang alerts is worth deliberation — alerts only on the system layer create a blind spot where the machines are all green while players are already rioting, while alerts only on the gameplay layer leave no clues for attribution, so the alert rings and nobody knows where to look.

A common compromise is to make the "player-perceivable" signals first-tier alerts, for example a spike in disconnect rate, a marked worsening of matchmaking wait, or a rising share of rooms over the frame-difference threshold; system-layer and service-layer metrics are kept on the dashboard as drill-down entry points. That way when an alert rings it represents real player impact and also has a ready-made investigation path.

### The cardinality trap

Dedicated metrics carry one general technical risk: cardinality explosion. Aggregating by room, by player, by skill — the dimension combinations quickly exceed what a metric system can bear. Common ways to handle it include:

- Dimension convergence: metrics keep only low-cardinality dimensions such as gameplay type and region group, while individual detail goes into logs.
- Sampled reporting: high-frequency signals such as frame time are sampled by ratio, relying on sample volume to keep distribution estimates stable.
- TopN records: for abnormal individuals keep only the worst few, for example the room with the deepest backlog, the frame with the longest time.

The cardinality problem is best accounted for when the metric system is chosen and the instrumentation is designed; the cost of governing it afterwards is far higher than converging it in advance.

### The governance cost of dedicated instrumentation

Dedicated metrics need lifecycle management just like business code. Every metric has a caliber definition, a collection point and an owner; as versions iterate, instrumentation quietly goes wrong along with code refactoring — a field nobody fills anymore, a sampling point deleted, a semantic changed while the name stays the same. Teams without a metric dictionary and a release acceptance step often find, a year later, a pile of metrics "nobody can explain how they were computed", and the only ending is discarding the whole batch and rebuilding it.

So the cost of specialized observability is not in the first instrumentation but in continuous governance. A common practice is to templatize the golden-path metric checklist, fill it in from the template when a new gameplay launches, and clean up metrics when an old gameplay goes offline, so that the set of metrics always matches the actual gameplays.

### When to start building

By experience, the earlier dedicated metrics are built, the cheaper they are. Adding them after an experience problem appears in production usually leaves only time to add logs, and logs are poor at answering distribution questions; standing up the golden-path metrics early means each later new gameplay only needs to be extended by convention. The longer-running a project, the more it depends on this layer: as gameplays keep stacking, the blind spots of general monitoring only grow, while player tolerance for experience problems only drops.

The timing of bringing a new gameplay onto dedicated observation also matters: instrument before launch and the peak data of the first few days is the most valuable batch of samples; add it after launch and either the peak is missed or the work is rushed under incident pressure and goes wrong. Putting it on the new-gameplay launch checklist is the lowest-cost way to enforce it.

### Finer-grained observation of disconnects and reconnects

Disconnect rate and reconnect are a hotspot for player complaints, but a blanket disconnect rate can hardly guide investigation, so it usually needs a few levels of breakdown:

- By caliber: voluntary quit, network drop, kicked offline — a number mixing these has no meaning.
- By dimension: region, gateway instance, carrier, client version — anomalies are often concentrated on one dimension.
- Reconnect has to be watched as a complete chain: reconnect request success rate, authentication time, state recovery time, catch-up time — what the player perceives is the total duration, not any single segment.

Only after breaking it down does disconnect data allow localization; a coarse disconnect rate can only confirm that something happened, not where. The reconnect chain is especially worth splitting into stages and timing, because the causes of "spinning for a long time" may land on four completely different segments.

### Every metric needs a drill-down path behind it

A metric alert is only the starting point; every core metric should have a drill-down path preset behind it: disconnect rate rising, look first at the region and gateway dimensions; matchmaking wait worsening, look first at queue length and how the online structure changed; frame time over budget, look first at whether it is concentrated in a particular gameplay or time window. Without a preset path, a stretch of free improvisation appears between the alert and the investigation, and time is exactly what is most scarce at incident time. Writing the drill-down entry points into the handling hints attached to the alert or into the on-call runbook is what closes the loop of the dedicated metric system.

### Common pitfalls

- Treating dedicated metrics as supplementary decoration, and only adding logs in a hurry after an experience problem appears, missing the first batch of the scene.
- Looking only at means and not at distributions, so spike problems stay invisible on the panel for a long time.
- Metrics pile up without layering, so after the alerts flood nobody knows which ones actually affect players.
- High-cardinality dimensions go straight into the metric system, so the observation system collapses before the business does.
- Instrumentation without a dictionary and acceptance, quietly going wrong across version iterations, and the data turns out untrustworthy when it is needed.

General monitoring solves whether the service is alive; specialized observability solves whether the game plays right. The two differ in metric source, aggregation and alert strategy; covering both with one approach usually does neither well.
