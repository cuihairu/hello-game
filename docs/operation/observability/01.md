# Logs, Metrics, Tracing and OTel

## Category

operation / observability subtree. Node positioning: observability is an evidence-organization problem — logs / metrics / Tracing as three irreplaceable kinds of evidence, sampling cost and the boundary of OTel. Master outline of the observability subtree.

## Definition


When observability comes up, teams usually get stuck first on tool selection: which log system to use, whether to bring in distributed tracing, how far to take OTel. But tools are only the carrying form; what observability really has to solve is the evidence-organization problem — when something breaks in production, can the team piece scattered runtime traces into one credible causal chain within limited time. Logs, metrics and Tracing are called the three pillars not because they often end up installed in the same platform, but because they are three kinds of evidence with different natures, none substitutable for another.

## Problem

All three tools bought and localization still stalls for 6 hours — what is missing is the evidence chain, not the tools: metrics answer "where is the anomaly", logs answer "what happened", Tracing answers "which hop of the path". The sampling cost of all three is amplified in game scenarios of high concurrency and low latency — without designing the evidence organization, stacking tools only stacks cost.


### What each of the three pillars answers

The division of labour among the three kinds of evidence can usually be summarized as:

- Metrics answer "how is the overall state": trends, water levels, abnormal fluctuations — suited to continuous watching and triggering alerts.
- Logs answer "what exactly happened at the time": details of discrete events, context fields, error stacks — suited to digging in afterwards.
- Tracing answers "where did this one piece of processing go": which services a call passed through, how long each segment took, at which hop it broke or slowed down.

Behind this division of labour lie differences in data shape. Metrics are pre-aggregated time series: extremely cheap per data point but retaining almost no detail. Logs are discrete events carrying context: rich in detail but expensive to store and to search. Tracing strings the multiple fragments of one piece of processing into a structure by causal relationship, sitting between the two, yet requiring every hop along the path to cooperate with instrumentation. Different shapes decide which questions they suit, and they also decide that the cost curves are completely different.

### Why metrics suit standing watch while logs suit dissection

The greatest value of metrics is that they are cheap and continuous. One point per second, kept for months, and the cost is still controllable, which is why they suit carrying the duty of standing watch: anchoring the baseline, discovering deviations, triggering alerts. Logs are the opposite — high cost per entry and search billed by volume, and no one can stand watch by continuously reading logs; their value is in post-incident dissection — you already know roughly where the problem is, then pull out details by condition.

This brings one common build decision: hang alerts on metrics as much as possible, not on "a certain error log appeared". Log-driven alerts tend to be noisy and drift in caliber, while metric alerts have thresholds, durations and recovery conditions that are all easier to manage. Log alerts are not impossible, but they usually have to be narrowed down to the very few error patterns that are genuinely representative.

### A few baseline judgments for building logs

Logs are the largest in volume among the three kinds of evidence and the easiest to abuse; a few common judgments are worth establishing in advance:

- Structured first: unify field naming, and only then do search and cross-service correlation have a foundation.
- trace id and player ID are the two fields most worth making standard equipment — the former strings the chain together, the latter strings the player's perspective together.
- Level semantics must stay stable: warnings are reserved for anomalies that recover automatically, errors for problems that need a human to look at; level inflation makes filtering fail completely.
- Error logs need deduplication and rate limiting: record the same error only once within a short window and accumulate the count, to keep an anomaly loop from growing into a log storm.
- Log volume itself should also be monitored: an abnormal jump in growth rate is often the first signal of abnormal behaviour such as cheat-tool scanning or loop defects.

Each of these judgments looks plain on its own; the difficulty is holding to it across services. Once the logging conventions loosen, search quality and cost degrade together, and the degradation is gradual — it usually surfaces in a lump only at incident time.

### Why Tracing is harder to land in game backends

In web backends the "request" is the natural unit of tracing: one call comes in, one response goes out, and the call tree in between is clear. Most game backend traffic has no such boundary — player sessions are long-lived connections, state sync is a continuous stream, one match can run for a dozen-plus minutes, and the internal calls of frame-driven and broadcast-driven logic have almost no concept of "one request".

So when a game backend does Tracing, it usually has to redefine "what counts as one trace" first. The common choice is to land the tracing unit on smaller, more explicit actions:

- The complete handling path triggered by one player action.
- One cross-service call, for example a matchmaking request, a settlement request, a payment callback.
- The lifecycle of one asynchronous message from dispatch to consumption complete.

Choose the unit wrong and traces end up either so large they have no analytical value, or so fragmented they cannot be assembled into a whole picture. This is the question most worth thinking through before bringing tracing into a game backend; its importance usually outweighs the choice of concrete implementation.

### Sampling cost is the core constraint in game scenarios

The cost of tracing comes from both ends: runtime overhead and storage cost. Full collection may be viable in a low-traffic validation environment, but at production scale sampling is almost always required. The common sampling strategies involve these trade-offs:

- Head sampling: decide at the start of the chain, by ratio, whether to record. Simple to implement, deterministic overhead, but it may drop exactly the trace that has the problem.
- Tail sampling: buffer everything first, then keep the errored and the slow ones afterwards. High informational value, but the buffering itself costs, and the longer the chain the longer the wait.
- Feature-based sampling: raise the sampling rate for particular players, particular gameplays, particular error types. Often the best value for money, but it requires these features to be carried onto the chain at instrumentation time.

For games there is one further special constraint: the main link is high-frequency small operations. A single trace is cheap, but multiplied by hundreds of thousands of operations per second it becomes a substantial cost. So the mature approach usually does not chase full collection but holds two bottom lines — keep errored and slow paths as complete as possible, and sample normal traffic by ratio to retain the ability to compare against baselines.

### Only when the three are correlated does localization become possible

Seen separately, the three pillars each give one fuzzy puzzle piece: metrics say some service's latency rose, logs say there were many timeouts in that time window, tracing says some class of call slowed down. Only when they can be correlated with each other — metrics drilling down into the logs of the corresponding time window, logs carrying a trace id that pulls out the complete chain — does localization change from piecing together guesses into walking along the evidence.

The foundation of correlation is a few habits that look plain: the trace id is generated at the entry and carried through the whole chain, logs are structured with unified field naming, timestamps are aligned to the same clock. None of this work has any technical difficulty; the difficulty is holding to it across teams. Many teams buy all three tools and never build the correlation, and at incident time everyone still reads their own data.

### The real value and boundary of OTel

The value of a unified collection standard like OTel lies mainly in reducing fragmentation: one client library covering metrics, logs and tracing, one unified export protocol, and backend storage that can be replaced without rewriting the instrumentation. For game projects with long lifecycles whose backends have very likely gone through several rounds of storage replacement, the value of this instrumentation asset being portable is often more tangible than the features themselves.

But there are two common misunderstandings. One is treating the integration standard as the solution itself: once the SDK is wired in, what counts as a span on the chain, what the key fields are called, which events are worth recording — these semantic conventions still have to be defined by the team, and the standard will not answer for you. The other is believing unified collection automatically covers the game's main link: tick time, room state, the matchmaking queue — these gameplay-layer signals are not in the default semantics of a general-purpose protocol to begin with, and still depend on dedicated building.

### Common judgments about build order

Observability capability does not suit being built in one big, all-encompassing push; the more common path is staged:

- First stand up the core metrics and the key business logs, so that when something happens you can know about it and take a rough look.
- Then connect main-link tracing, focusing on cross-service synchronous calls and high-value asynchronous flows.
- Finally fill in dedicated observation by pain point, for example slow-query attribution and broadcast backlog attribution.

The logic behind this order is return on investment: metrics and logs are the base of localization, and without them tracing cannot be interpreted at all; while tracing has the highest build cost, so investing it after the main-link evidence is complete is what keeps it from turning into a demo project.

### Cost governance of the observation system itself

Observability capability is itself a subsystem that continuously consumes cost. Log volume swells with business growth and anomaly loops, metrics explode as dimension combinations grow in cardinality, and storage quietly doubles as retention policy changes. Common governance measures include tiered retention (short cycles for hot data, long cycles for error data), dimension convergence (high-cardinality labels go into logs rather than metrics), and a ceiling on log write acceleration, to keep an anomaly loop from knocking over the observation system itself.

More worth watching is the reverse case: observation code dragging the business down. Synchronous log writes blocking the logic thread, full reporting on every request, a sampler taking a lock on the hot path — all of these have really happened. Observation is a side path, and any cost of the side path is transferred onto the main link, so on integration it usually has to be reviewed to the standard of main-link code.

### Metric caliber and baselines need governance too

Metrics are not set-and-forget once collected. Caliber drifts — for the same "latency", the gateway may be measuring ingress time while the logic server measures processing time, and drawing both on the same chart pollutes each other. Baselines drift too — load changes with versions and the operations rhythm, and a threshold set half a year ago may false-alarm every day today, or never fire at all.

The common governance approach is to keep a simple file for each core metric: caliber definition, measurement location, owner, and the basis of the threshold. Thresholds are not guessed by gut feel but derived from historical distributions and reviewed periodically. These actions cost little; without them, the more metrics there are, the less trustworthy they become.

### When it is worth investing more

Scale and architecture are the two main trigger conditions. In the era of small monolithic services, logs plus metrics are often enough; once services are split into more pieces and faults start propagating between subsystems, the payoff of tracing rises noticeably. Another judgment signal is time to recovery: if localization time is far longer than fix time on every fault, the evidence chain is insufficient, and more observation investment is usually more effective than more on-call headcount.

A plain way to judge the direction of investment is to look back at the last few faults: each step where localization got stuck usually corresponds to a missing kind of evidence. Listing those sticking points out is the checklist for the next phase of observation building.

### Common pitfalls

- All three tools were built, but they are not correlated with each other, so at fault time everyone reads their own data and the conclusions contradict one another.
- Chasing full collection while observability cost and noise spiral out of control together, and the key signals get drowned out instead.
- Treating OTel integration as the observability solution itself: the instrumentation channel is unified, but nobody defines the semantic conventions.
- Alerts hung on log matching rules — noisy, drifting in caliber, and on-call fatigues quickly.
- Observation code not reviewed to main-link standards, so sampling and reporting themselves become new performance problems.

The goal of an observation system has never been to record more and more; it is that in the few minutes when something really breaks, the evidence happens to be just enough and happens to string together. Making trade-offs around that goal avoids rework better than making selections around a feature list.

### Case study: an evidence-chain investigation of one "all three tools bought, localization still stalled for 6 hours"

> The case is an illustrative example (not taken from a real project); numbers are rounded and simplified. It puts this page's "What each of the three pillars answers", "Why metrics suit standing watch while logs suit dissection", "A few baseline judgments for building logs", "Only when the three are correlated does localization become possible", "Metric caliber and baselines need governance too", "Common judgments about build order" and "When it is worth investing more" into deep water: a latency fault on a season-settlement night, with all three observation tools present, yet no credible causal chain could be assembled.

**Background and symptoms**: on one long-running project's season-settlement night, from **20**:**10** onward players reported in concentration that in-match actions and settlement had slowed. The on-call opened three panels and each read their own: on the metrics panel the gateway P99 rose from **45**ms to **420**ms and the logic server read **380**ms at the same moment — two "latency" curves drawn on one screen that do not match up: one records ingress time (including queueing), the other only processing time, a gap of **40**ms; the alert panel had **0** fired — the **500**ms threshold had been set half a year earlier and never reviewed since, while the log alerts did fire **3** rules, all noise flushed out by the same error loop; the log platform's **30**-minute window pulled out **1.2 million** timeout logs, with no deduplication and no rate limit; in the tracing platform only **2** of **8** services had a trace id instrumented at the entry, the chain broke at hop **3**, and a later reconciliation also found the logic server and the data server **40** seconds apart on system clock. Localization stalled for **6** hours, while handling the root cause took only **5** minutes — an index-missed full query inside one settlement task had saturated the connection pool.

### Step one: symptoms and caliber — first separate "which segment the latency is in" from "who the evidence belongs to"

Caliber unified: take "the complete handling path triggered by one player action" as the tracing unit, first list clearly what each of the three kinds of evidence can answer, and make two notes:

- **Caliber**: at the same moment the gateway reads **420**ms and the logic server **380**ms; the **40**ms gap is not measurement error, it is two calibers — one includes ingress queueing, the other counts processing only
- **Alerts**: metric alerts fired **0** (the **500**ms threshold was not reached), the **3** log alerts were all noise from the same error loop — the one standing watch did not stand watch, and everything dissected was noise

The two curves not matching up is not broken data, it is caliber drift: the threshold set half a year ago never had a chance to fire on a drifting baseline, while the alerts that were actually ringing hung on log matching rules.

### Step two: layered attribution — from a latency fault to an evidence-chain ledger

```text
Metric ledger    threshold 500ms unreviewed for half a year; gateway 420 and logic server 380 share the name "latency" under two calibers, 40ms apart
Log ledger       30 minutes, 1.2 million timeout logs (about 40,000 per minute), the same error loop flooding the screen, deduplication and rate limit at 0
Trace ledger     only 2 of 8 services instrument a trace id (25% coverage), the chain breaks at hop 3; the logic server and data server clocks are 40 seconds apart
Localization ledger    localization 6 hours (360 minutes) against fix 5 minutes = 72x; the evidence chain is insufficient
```

Ledger wrapped up: the **72**x of **6** hours of localization against **5** minutes of fix is exactly the judgment signal this page names — "if localization time on every fault is far longer than fix time, the evidence chain is insufficient"; the **40**ms caliber gap and the **40**-second clock gap each broke one link of the causal chain.

### Step three: root cause — all three pillars were present, but correlation was never built

Checked against this page's "Only when the three are correlated does localization become possible": of the three plain habits of correlation — the trace id generated at the entry and carried through the whole chain, logs structured with unified field naming, timestamps aligned to the same clock — not one was done. Only **2** of **8** services were instrumented, and "many teams buy all three tools and never build the correlation, and at incident time everyone still reads their own data" came true word for word. Checked against "Why metrics suit standing watch while logs suit dissection": the alerts hung on log matching rules, the **3** that fired were all noise, and the threshold was fixed once by gut feel — "log-driven alerts tend to be noisy and drift in caliber" was hit on both counts. Checked against "A few baseline judgments for building logs": of the five judgments — structured, trace id and player ID as standard equipment, level semantics, deduplication and rate limiting, log-volume monitoring — the first four were all missing. The same error flooding endlessly through **1.2 million** logs is exactly the scene that "record the same error only once within a short window and accumulate the count, to keep an anomaly loop from growing into a log storm" exists to prevent. Checked against "Metric caliber and baselines need governance too": of the four items of a caliber file — caliber definition, measurement location, owner, the basis of the threshold — not one existed, and "the gateway may be measuring ingress time, the logic server may be measuring processing time, and drawing both on the same chart pollutes each other" happened exactly as written. Of the five items in this page's "Common pitfalls", the first and the fourth hit exactly.

### Step four: handling and backfill — fix evidence correlation first, then fill in the base in order

The fix comes in two parts. Emergency: at **T+0**, add an index to the settlement task's query and rate-limit it, the connection pool recovers, and bleeding stops in **5** minutes; at **T+1**, clocks are aligned across all services, the **40**-second gap between the logic server and the data server goes to zero, and replaying that night's logs strings out the complete chain by unified trace id, with the same fault localized in **25** minutes — down from **360** minutes to **25**. Three institutional steps: re-ordered per this page's "Common judgments about build order" — step one stands up the metric and log base, first standing up the core metrics and key business logs so that when something happens you can know about it and take a rough look: move all alerts onto metrics (all **3** log alerts moved off), deduplicate and rate-limit error logs (record the same error only **1** entry per **1** minute and accumulate the count), and put trace id and player ID into required structured fields; step two connects main-link tracing: land the tracing unit on the three classes of action "one player action", "one cross-service call" and "the lifecycle of one asynchronous message from dispatch to consumption complete", instrument all **8** services in full, and hold sampling to the two bottom lines — keep errored and slow paths as complete as possible (**100**%), and sample normal traffic at **1**% to retain the ability to compare against baselines; step three builds the metric file: register caliber definition, measurement location, owner and the basis of the threshold for each of the **6** core metrics, and re-derive thresholds from the historical distribution of the last **30** days — P99 baseline **210**ms, alert line at **1.5**x that = **315**ms, so this case's **420**ms would fire within **2** minutes of the climb instead of staying silent. Review: a drill replays the fault, localizing in **25** minutes on the new evidence chain and fixing in **5**, bringing the localization multiple down from **72** to **5**.

**Backfill checklist**:

| Landing point | Action | Backfill target |
|-----|------|--------|
| Evidence correlation | trace id generated at the entry and carried through the whole chain, structured unified fields, clock alignment | Only when the three are correlated does localization become possible |
| Alert and log discipline | move alerts onto metrics, deduplicate and rate-limit error logs, fix the level semantics | A few baseline judgments for building logs |
| Tracing unit and sampling | land the unit on three classes of action, keep errored and slow paths complete, sample normal traffic by ratio | Sampling cost is the core constraint in game scenarios |
| Metric file | register caliber, location, owner and threshold for six metrics, with periodic review | Metric caliber and baselines need governance too |

### Three lessons from the case

1. **Evidence that is not correlated is the same as no evidence** — all three tools were present, the trace id broke at hop **3** and the clocks were **40** seconds apart; each one looked true on its own, but they could not be assembled into one causal chain.
2. **Localization far outlasting the fix is the bill the evidence chain sends you** — the **72**x of **6** hours against **5** minutes says more about where to invest than any project review.
3. **Both caliber and baseline expire** — the **500**ms threshold from half a year ago and the two "latency" curves **40**ms apart on one screen; without a file and review, "the goal of an observation system has never been to record more and more, but that in the few minutes when something really breaks, the evidence happens to be just enough and happens to string together" never lands.
