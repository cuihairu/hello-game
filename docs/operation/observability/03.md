# Profiling, Flame Graphs and Hotspot Analysis

## Category

operation / observability subtree. Node positioning: moving performance discussion from guesswork to evidence — symptom definition, how to read a flame graph, the sampling versus instrumentation trade-off, hotspot attribution and budget governance.

## Definition


The biggest waste in performance optimization is spending effort on bottlenecks that do not exist or do not matter. Between "it feels slow here" and "the data shows this is the most expensive" lies a complete forensic chain. The value of Profiling and flame graphs is not in drawing an impressive chart, but in moving performance discussion from guesswork to evidence: which path really is the most expensive under which load, whether being expensive there is justified, and how much player-perceivable gain optimizing it would buy.

## Problem

The biggest waste in performance optimization is optimizing bottlenecks that do not exist or do not matter: between "it feels slow here" and "the data shows this is the most expensive" lies a complete forensic chain. Without performance budget constraints, optimization gains leak away continuously across version iterations, and performance regression is the norm rather than the exception.


### Start from defining the symptom, not from the tool

Before sampling, answer four questions: under what load, in what scenario, how slow, and who is affected. Once the symptom is defined clearly, profile results have a coordinate system to read against; when the definition is vague, the same flame graph can be read countless ways, each reading sounding plausible. Common symptom definitions include "frame time doubles during team fights in a certain gameplay", "logic thread CPU rises 30% after an event starts", and "load time exceeds budget on a certain device tier".

Defining the symptom also has a side effect: it determines the timing and scope of sampling. Intermittent jitter has to be sampled inside the time window in which the jitter occurs, load-related hotspots have to be sampled under the corresponding load, and device-related client problems have to be sampled on the target device. Symptom first, then sampling, and only then do the tools point at the answer.

### What a flame graph is actually expressing

A few basic rules for reading flame graphs:

- Horizontal width represents share of time, not call count.
- Vertical is the call stack, and the further down, the closer to the start of the call chain.
- A wide block's "flat top" often means a hotspot: the function itself is consuming time rather than merely calling through to others.
- The total width of the whole chart corresponds to total CPU time during the sampling period.

The common judgments when reading a chart are: wide and flat-topped blocks that belong to business code deserve a first look; wide blocks belonging to runtime foundations, for example memory allocation, serialization primitives and lock operations — first ask "who is calling this so much", and the answer is usually a few levels up. A flame graph points out "where the time went", and "why it went there" often has to be found by following the stack upward to the business cause.

### Sampling versus instrumentation

There are two basic approaches to performance collection, each with clear boundaries of application:

- Sampling: capture call stacks periodically. Low overhead and it can stay resident in production, but very short functions may be underestimated, and calls near the sampling boundary carry attribution error.
- Instrumentation: plant counters and timers at function boundaries. Precise boundaries and it can quantify call counts, but the overhead is high and it changes the program's timing, so it suits only offline or reproduction environments.

Normal production can rely only on sampling; instrumentation is for precise measurement of paths already pinned down in a reproduction environment. The two are often used together: sampling in production points the direction, instrumentation verifies the detail. Laying instrumentation straight into production means the observation itself distorts the system being observed.

### The cost and benefit of continuous production profiling

A low-overhead sampler can stay resident in the production environment and buys two capabilities that would otherwise be unavailable: first, comparison against historical baselines — where this version's hotspot distribution differs from the last, and regression shows up directly in the data; second, preserving the scene at abnormal moments — when jitter occurs there happens to be sampling data to check, instead of reconstructing empty-handed afterwards.

The cost is equally concrete: continuous CPU usage, storage of the sampling data, and the cost of symbol resolution. Release builds usually strip symbol information, so if symbol tables and version numbers are not archived at the same time, captured stacks cannot be mapped back to source code and the chart is as good as never taken. A common configuration for continuous profiling is low-frequency residence plus raising the sampling rate on anomalies, trading a small stable overhead for a baseline that is always comparable.

### Common paths for hotspot attribution

After a flame graph points at a specific function, attribution usually lands on a few common causes, and each one needs a completely different direction of handling:

- Frequent allocation: large numbers of short-lived objects are created. The cost is not in the allocation itself but in reclaim pressure and cache invalidation; the direction is reuse and object pools.
- Serialization and copying: the same data is encoded and copied repeatedly on the broadcast path; the direction is sharing and serialize-on-demand.
- Lock contention: the stack shows waiting on a lock, but the real hotspot is the code holding it; the direction is shrinking the critical section or switching to a lock-free structure.
- Timers and traversal: object sets traversed in full every frame grow with scale; the direction is spreading across frames, incrementals and dirty flags.
- Unexpected paths: a query that should hit the cache lands on the slow path, or writes that should be batched are committed one at a time; the direction is correcting the calling pattern.

Attribute wrong and optimization goes in the reverse direction — for example making lock granularity finer but introducing more overhead, or building an object pool for a function that is called very rarely. This is also why instrumentation data (call counts) has independent value: it answers "how many times was this function called", which is the key input to judging the direction of optimization.

### Sampling window and statistical stability

A flame graph from a single short sample may be accidental: it may happen to catch one event starting or one peak of disk writes, and the hotspot distribution ends up far from normal. When comparing two samples, if the window length and load conditions differ, the conclusions are not comparable. Common practice is to fix the sampling duration, avoid unusual time windows, and sample the same symptom several times to confirm the shape is stable before drawing optimization conclusions. When the data volume is insufficient, sample a few more rounds rather than fixing an optimization list from one isolated chart.

The cost of the same function can differ by an order of magnitude across gameplays, maps and object scales. So profile data is best linkable to business dimensions: record the current gameplay type, online scale, object count and event state during sampling. Without that linkage, optimization conclusions can stop only at the function level, unable to answer "when and in what scenario will players be affected", and so the actual gain of the optimization cannot be estimated.

Context linkage matters especially for the client: the same piece of logic may behave completely differently in the main city than in the battlefield, on a low-end machine than on a high-end one, and discussing hotspots while detached from load easily produces sweeping conclusions drawn from a corner case.

### Special properties of the client and the render path

Client performance problems are often not in the pure logic code: the CPU / GPU boundary, batching and draw calls, asset loading and instantiation, GC pauses, hot updates and decompression — each class has its own observation means. A logic-layer flame graph cannot show GPU bottlenecks; frame rate problems first have to be split into CPU not preparing in time versus GPU not rendering in time, since the two correspond to completely different directions of optimization.

Device tiers are a dimension client profiling cannot avoid. A hotspot on a low-end machine may be entirely invisible on a high-end one, so client sampling is usually done per device tier, and must be done on real target devices — conclusions extrapolated from a dev machine have limited value.

### Server and client each have their priority observation points

Server profiling targets are relatively concentrated: the logic frame main loop, broadcast and serialization, database access paths, scheduled tasks. These paths' load correlates directly with online scale, and they are the most likely to surface first as capacity grows. Client targets are spread across the various parts of the frame budget: logic update, render submission, asset loading, UI rebuild and GC. The two sides often need to localize together — when server frame time is normal but the client plays stuttering, the problem is usually at the junction of sync volume, interpolation and render pressure, and either side looked at alone is easily misread.

### Optimization has to be verified in a loop

Establishing a baseline before optimization and retesting against the same baseline afterwards sounds like common sense but is very often skipped — in a rush, "it feels faster after the change" gets committed. An optimization without a retest has two common endings: the effect is overestimated, or the optimized point simply is not on the main path under real load and players notice nothing.

Baselines should be as close to the shape of real load as possible. Testing only single functions with micro-benchmarks easily steers optimization onto a path that never occurs in a real scenario; the numbers look good but mean nothing to players. A complete loop is usually: baseline under real load, optimize, retest, then observe production metrics to confirm the player side actually changed.

### When specialized optimization is worth doing

A rough priority judgment is cost per call multiplied by trigger frequency: one very expensive path triggered once a year is usually less worth optimizing than one cheap path executed thousands of times per frame. By experience, most player-perceivable performance gains come from high-frequency paths such as the main loop, broadcast, serialization and visibility computation, rather than from the most eye-catching complex algorithms.

This judgment also explains a common frustration in reverse: a team does a highly technical optimization and players feel nothing — most likely the optimized point is not frequent enough. Look at frequency first, then at cost per call; reversing the order easily produces work whose gain is close to zero.

### Using budgets to constrain performance regression

A mechanism further forward than post-hoc profiling is the performance budget: logic frame stages, per-frame broadcast volume and key load times each have explicit budget values, checked against when a new feature is wired in. Budget values do not need to be precise; their role is to make "will this feature slow down the main loop" a discussable and measurable quantity rather than something discovered only after launch. What to do when a budget is exceeded can be optimizing the implementation, reducing the presentation, or consciously raising the budget — what matters is that the decision is made, not that it happens by default.

### Recording and comparing performance data

The long-term value of hotspot analysis depends on historical retention: sampling results archived per version, flame graphs and baseline numbers for key scenarios kept on file, so cross-version comparison has something to stand on. Many teams do profiling but keep no data, starting from zero every time, so performance regression stays invisible for the long term. Archiving costs very little — usually just the sampling file with its version number and scenario description, plus a few lines of baseline numbers.

### Continuous governance beats one-off campaigns

Performance rots with versions: new features introduce new per-frame costs, old hotspots get forgotten, and regression is gradual. Bringing profiling into the routine flow — one hotspot comparison before and after each version, a low-overhead sampler resident on the main branch, a performance baseline check added to the release checklist — usually prevents regression better than a once-a-year performance campaign. Specialized campaigns are good for known major hotspots but cannot stop gradual degradation, and players perceive gradual degradation just as clearly.

### Common pitfalls

- Drawing conclusions straight from one flame graph, without looking at the sampling environment, the load shape or the business context.
- Optimizing the eye-catching but non-critical, investing a lot while players notice nothing.
- Profiling only during performance campaigns, with no baseline normally, so there is nothing to compare against when anomalies hit.
- Release builds not archiving symbol information, so captured call stacks cannot be resolved back to source.
- Using dev-machine or micro-benchmark conclusions to stand in for production load and real devices.
- Not retesting against the baseline after optimizing, judging the effect purely by feel, and even introducing new regressions without knowing.

The value of hotspot analysis lies not in how advanced the tool is, but in whether the three links of symptom definition, context linkage and closed-loop verification are complete. With the links in place, an ordinary sampling tool can support reliable performance decisions; with the links missing, even the best flame graph is only decoration.
