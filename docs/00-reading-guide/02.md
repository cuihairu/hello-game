# Entering by Problem

Specific problems in a project usually come from live symptoms, review disagreements, or incident retrospectives. This index is organized as "symptom → problem domain → related articles," so that what actually happened can be traced back to the premises that need to be checked.

## First Translate the Symptom into a Problem Domain

Symptoms live at the surface layer: "disconnections," "lag," "data doesn't add up," "config doesn't take effect." The knowledge base is organized at the problem-domain layer: access, sync, state boundaries, the release chain. Searching with the symptom as a keyword usually hits a pile of passages that merely talk about the same noun, none of them answering the premise of your question. This step of translation cannot be skipped — the game types and problem model chapter exists precisely to do that translation.

## Three Questions to Ask When Translating

- Which chain does this phenomenon occur on, and what systems sit before and after that chain?
- Which state is involved, and where does its authority lie?
- Where does the cost of failure land — experience, assets, or compliance?

Once those three questions are answered, the entry chapter usually surfaces on its own. If they cannot be answered fully, it often means the phenomenon itself is not yet clearly located, and narrowing the scope first matters more than picking a chapter first.

## A Rough Map from Problems to Entry Points

- Slow connection establishment, frequent disconnections, poor weak-network recovery: enter through network access; this usually drags out access-layer responsibilities and session-binding problems.
- Wrong game feel, position pulled back, replays don't line up: enter through sync and combat; the root usually turns out to be the time model and authority ownership.
- Lag at peak load, process crashes, lock contention: enter through the execution model; look at the scheduling unit and how state is organized.
- Wanting to split services, cross-server gameplay, server-merge migration: enter through service splitting and control plane; look at state boundaries before deployment form.
- Message backlog, duplicate consumption, out-of-order delivery: enter through messages and IPC; distinguish main-chain calls from peripheral events.
- Large package size, slow startup, chaotic resource management: enter through client architecture.
- Problem is known but cannot be fixed, cannot be released, cannot stop the bleeding: enter through scripts and hot update.
- Version compatibility, gray-release windows, channel-review conflicts: enter through version release.
- Data doesn't add up, hard to recover, server-merge errors: enter through data modeling; first distinguish authoritative state from derived copies.
- Cache invalidation, middleware failures amplified by the business: enter through cache and middleware.
- Accounts, payments, mail, leaderboards constantly reworked: enter through shared services.
- Event-config incidents, conflicting report criteria: enter through operations and monetization; the config pipeline appendix provides cross-cutting supplementation.
- Cannot find the cause in production, localization relies on guessing: enter through observability and stability.
- Cheats, farms, arbitrage, ban disputes: enter through security and risk control.
- Channel access, login and payment, platform rules: enter through platforms and SDKs.

## How to Find an Entry Point for Problems Outside the Map

For problems the map does not cover, there are usually two paths. One is to push forward along the player's path, starting at login and asking, for each segment, what problems have occurred there and what the cost of failure is. The other is to work backwards from a state list, listing the states that change most frequently and are weakest under audit, then tracing their chains in reverse. Both paths eventually land in a few main-line chapters, which is far more reliable than keyword search.

## One Problem Often Spans Several Entry Points

"After reconnection, state is wrong" crosses at least three layers: session recovery in network access, frame catch-up and snapshots in sync and combat, and state partitioning in data modeling. Reading through a single entry point easily produces a local patch, which then blows up again in another layer next time. The map gives only an entry point, not the whole answer.

## Topic Relationships for Cross-Entry-Point Problems

Taking "after reconnection, state is wrong" as an example, an effective reading order is:

- First read the session and reconnection parts of network access, to determine what the connection layer recovered.
- Then read the frame catch-up, snapshots, and prediction-correction parts of sync and combat, to determine how the state layer reconciles.
- Finally read the state-partitioning part of data modeling, to confirm which state is worth snapshotting and which can be recomputed.

Reading in this order gives each of the three layers of the problem a corresponding judgment, so nothing gets attributed wholesale to "the network was bad."

## After Entering, Go Back to the Prerequisites

The chapter you land in often uses vocabulary established in earlier chapters. Reading service splitting without having read the problem model leaves state boundaries as just a noun; reading observability without having read the main-chain partitioning leaves the metric list as just a monitoring-item list. Before entering any chapter, first confirm whether you have read its prerequisite chapters — that saves more time than reading one more new chapter.

## Reviews and Retrospectives Are Two Kinds of Entry

During a design review, you can search for counterexamples and failure costs along the judgment chain of related articles; during an incident retrospective, you can work backwards from symptoms to the missing capability layer. The former tends to find only supporting evidence, the latter tends to chase only the direct cause.

## Symptoms Disappearing Does Not Mean the Problem Is Closed

Making symptoms disappear with a patch is the most common outcome when entering by problem. Judging whether a problem is truly closed means being able to state the failure costs of this class of problem and the layers that intercept it, not whether the phenomenon reproduces. In a retrospective, only when you can answer "which chapter's capability should have intercepted this problem" is the loop closed.

## Using the Appendices for Cross-Comparison

After initial localization, the per-problem-domain summaries provide a piece suitable for comparing against similar products, to check whether common problems were missed; the game-type topics are suited to supplementing intuition during a design review, answering "where do products of this type usually compress their complexity." The way to use the appendices is to cross-check, not to learn from scratch.

## Common Pitfalls

- Taking the symptom as the problem domain; the more chapters you search, the more scattered it gets, until you pick a statement by feel.
- Reading only the chapter you hit, without tracing back to prerequisites; conclusions applied to your own project immediately distort.
- Treating the solution to one problem as closure of the problem domain, leaving no verification metrics or monitoring points.
- Spreading effort evenly across keywords, splitting a cross-layer problem among several people who do not talk to each other.
