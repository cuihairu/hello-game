# Monetization Numerical Example Models

## Category

Numerical tree. Node positioning: monetization numerical example models — four basic product types, value-for-money gradient projection, and pay depth versus experience boundaries. All numbers are example models, not industry standards.

## Definition


> All prices, quotas, and ratios on this page are **example models, not industry standards** — used to illustrate structure and projection method, not to constitute any pricing advice; real pricing is subject to multiple constraints including category, region, compliance and platform revenue share.

Monetization numbers answer three questions: what to sell (product structure), at what price (pricing and the value-for-money gradient), and how deep (the boundary between pay depth and experience). This page unfolds the projection method along the mainstream product structure; data conventions and the operations side are in [Chapter 14](/operation/bi/) and [Feature Note 12](/24-game-types-architecture/12-data-analytics).

## Problem

Without a projection method, product structure and value-for-money gradient are pulled out of thin air: gradient gaps (nobody buys the mid tier) and runaway depth (whales buy through a version in one day) are both structural problems, not numerical problems. Pay depth also has to stop at the experience boundary — cross it and the paying experience becomes a negative asset.


### Product Structure: Four Basic Types

- **Direct-purchase bundles** (one-time, clearly priced): the carrier of the pricing anchor — players learn from here the exchange rate of "1 yuan ≈ how much resource"
- **Monthly card / subscription** (periodic, small amount high frequency): trades time for value-for-money, the core role is **turning payment from a decision into a habit** (daily login to claim rewards)
- **Battle pass / pass** (seasonal, quest-driven): ties payment to activity, what is bought is "the growth permission for this season"
- **Gacha** (probabilistic): expectation is public, variance creates surprise, bound by disclosure and pity compliance (page 05 of this chapter)

Combinations of these four basic types form most monetization panels; the rest (direct cosmetic purchase, renaming, stamina purchase) are all variants or supplements of these four.

### Value-for-Money Gradient: Example Projection

Let the example exchange rate be 1 yuan = 10 gems (**example anchor**). The gem-per-yuan ratios of three products:

- Direct purchase: 6 yuan = 60 gems → **10 gems/yuan** (baseline)
- Monthly card: 30 yuan, claim 100 gems daily × 30 days = 3000 gems → **100 gems/yuan** (10× baseline, **example ratio**)
- Battle pass: 68 yuan unlocks the premium reward line, example total value converts to 3400 gems → **50 gems/yuan** (5× baseline, between direct purchase and monthly card)

Structure of the projection conclusion (the numbers are examples, the structure is universal): **value-for-money is proportional to "time / activity binding depth"** — direct purchase is instant and the most expensive, the monthly card is cheapest but takes 30 days of claiming, the battle pass sits in between and requires seasonal activity. This gradient is itself a design: it guides players from low binding (direct purchase) toward high binding (monthly card / battle pass), raising retention rather than purely harvesting one-time payments.

### Pay Depth and the Experience Boundary

Two measures of pay depth: **total pay depth** (the expected total investment to graduate one account) and **single-point pay depth** (the price band of a single product tier). Three empirical rules for the experience boundary (all design disciplines, not regulatory clauses): systems where **payment cannot buy exclusive strength** (cosmetics, convenience, collection) have the highest monetization ceiling and lowest risk; **strength payment** must have a free acquisition path (slow is fine, absent is not), and is protected by PVP isolation (tiered matchmaking, combat-power threshold zones); **pushing the first payment point forward has limits** — the experience before the first payment point must be complete (page 07 of this chapter on new-player pacing), and the longer the stretch of "can't keep playing without paying", the larger the loss in next-day retention and word of mouth.

### Projection and Acceptance

Monetization numbers follow the same data closed loop for acceptance: bundle purchase rate, revenue share by tier, first-purchase conversion rate, ARPPU/ARPU trends (conventions in Chapter 14 and Feature Note 12). Any price adjustment first verifies on a canary server the simultaneous change in ARPPU and retention — a price adjustment that raises revenue while retention falls is drinking poison to quench thirst. **Monetization numbers have no "industry standard answer", only "the verified answer under this project's data"**; this is also why every number on this page is only an example.

### Common Pitfalls

- **Inverted value-for-money gradient**: direct purchase is more cost-effective than the monthly card, and the periodic product loses its reason to exist
- **Watching only revenue, not retention**: a short-term ARPPU lift masks a next-day retention slide; two weeks later revenue falls back and the pie is smaller
- **No free path for strength payment**: the free player's catch-up channel is missing, and community hostility and churn happen in lockstep
- **Gacha expectation ungoverned**: no one is assigned to reconcile expectation and pity, the disclosed convention and the implementation disagree (a compliance red line, see page 05 of this chapter and Chapter 17)
- **Packing payment points into the first 30 minutes**: first-purchase popup density exceeds the experience's carrying capacity, and the tutorial period directly becomes the turn-off period
