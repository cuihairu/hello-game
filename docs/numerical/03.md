# Growth Curve Models

## Category

Numerical tree. Node positioning: three base curves, cost curves (growth feel = return/cost), combat-power quantification and segmented nodes — the skeleton of the input-to-capability exchange relationship.

## Definition


A growth curve answers one question: the exchange relationship between player input (time, skill, payment) and character capability (attributes, combat power). It is the skeleton of long-running games — growth too slow will not retain players, too fast and content rapidly devalues. This page gives three base curves, how to pair cost with return, and a quantitative convention for "growth feel".

## Problem

Growth curves too slow fail to retain, too fast devalue content, but without a convention for "fast vs. slow" there is nothing to discuss: it must land as a return/cost ratio and combat-power quantification before growth feel becomes computable, tunable, and acceptable.


### The Three Base Curves

Let n be the level, A(n) the capability value, and A₀ the base value:

- **Linear**: A(n) = A₀ + a×n. The absolute increment per level is constant, the relative increment diminishes (100+50n: level 10 gains 50, which is 8% of 600; level 100 also gains 50, only 1% of 5100 — the absolute value is stable, growth feel decays with level)
- **Power function**: A(n) = A₀×n^b. Between linear and exponential; larger b makes the late game steeper (b=1.5, A₀=100: level 10 is 3162, level 50 is 35355, level 100 is 100000). Attribute growth in many MMORPGs falls here
- **Exponential**: A(n) = A₀×r^(n−1). Each level compounds at a fixed rate (r=1.1, A₀=100: level 10 is 236, level 50 is about 10.7k, level 100 is about 1.25M). Late-game numbers explode; suited to "numerical carnival" idle gameplay, use with caution for long-balance PVP

The three curves differ dramatically in shape across the same level span — choosing a curve is choosing the face of the game: competitive confrontation wants it flat (balanceable), single-player growth thrill wants it steep (dramatizable), long-running operations usually start with a power function and use segmented fitting to control each segment's slope.

### Cost Curves: Growth Feel Is a "Return / Cost" Ratio

The growth speed a player perceives is not A(n) but **ΔA/ΔT**: attribute increment per unit time. And ΔT is determined by the level-up cost curve (cost C(n) = C₀×n^c or tuned by hand per segment). The design move is to pair two curves: the return curve sets the shape, the cost curve tunes the pacing — early on c must be small (a growth pulse of one level every few hours during the new-player phase), mid-game c rises (yielding ground to progression systems), and major version nodes use new systems (new equipment slot, new skill slot) to suddenly raise ΔA, manufacturing a "sense of stage".

**The quantitative convention for growth feel uses relative increment**: +50 attack on attack 100 is +50%, on attack 1000 it is +5% — the same absolute value, a tenfold difference in feel. When accepting growth pacing, look at percentage increment (combat-power gain percentage per hour / per day / per version), not absolute values.

### Combat-Power Quantification

A multi-attribute system needs a **combat-power equivalent** to fold different attributes into a single number (for leaderboards, combat-power thresholds, growth reports). Conversion coefficients come from combat simulation or formula derivation: for example, anchored on "equivalent DPS", the value of 1 point of crit rate = the expected damage increase it brings × base attack. Two disciplines of quantification: **weights are re-verified per version** (numerical inflation changes the marginal value of attributes; if weights are not updated, combat power is distorted) and **combat power is a reference only, never settlement** (PVP matchmaking and reward thresholds by combat-power tiers are fine; deciding win/loss directly by combat power is not — beyond the formula there is also skill and counters).

### Segments and Nodes

The real shape of long-running growth is almost always a **piecewise function**: each expansion/major version is one segment, smooth curves within a segment, jumps between segments via new systems. The management cost segmentation brings is "cross-segment reconciliation": at the start of a new segment, whether the relative position (percentage gap) between old and new players is within an acceptable range — this is the numerical foundation for server merges, return campaigns, season resets and other operational actions (see Chapter 14 on the operations side).

### Common Pitfalls

- **One exponential curve all the way**: after level 100 the numbers grow too large to mean anything, and both balance and surface area go out of control — long-running games use segmentation to control this
- **Tuning only return, not cost**: if leveling feels slow you raise attribute increments, when in fact tuning the cost curve is subtler and more controllable
- **Accepting on absolute increment**: "+50 per level" sounds neat, but perception is diminishing — growth-feel acceptance must use relative increment
- **Combat-power weights never updated long-term**: after version turnover, combat-power leaderboards decouple from real strength and players vote with their feet
- **Growth without pulses**: smooth all the way equals flat all the way — the sense of stages comes from designed jump nodes, not natural growth
