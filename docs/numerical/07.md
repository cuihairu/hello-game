# Level Difficulty and New-Player Pacing

## Category

Numerical tree. Node positioning: quantified difficulty and new-player pacing — the definition of "hard", the flow channel, failure penalties and retry pacing, and the numerical safety net for the first 30 minutes.

## Definition


The difficulty curve decides "which level players stop at", and new-player pacing decides "whether to give them a chance to stop". This page quantifies both: how to measure difficulty, how to manage the flow channel, how to design failure penalties, and the numerical safety checklist for the first 30 minutes. All example numbers are **design-convention examples, not industry standards**.

## Problem

Difficulty cannot be discussed as "which level players stop at" without quantification: the flow channel, failure penalties, and new-player guarantees must all land as numerical conventions, otherwise designers and programmers argue without a shared language, and new-player churn cannot be attributed either.


### Quantifying Difficulty: Define "Hard" First

"This level is hard" is not a metric; the following are four measurable conventions:

- **Clear rate**: the share of players who clear the level within a first-day / seven-day window — the hardest difficulty metric
- **Attempt count**: average attempts to clear — reflects the strength of a bottleneck, not its breadth
- **Churn-point overlap**: among players who did not clear, how many churn at this level — difficulty only becomes a problem once it converts into churn
- **TTK / clear-time distribution**: in-combat difficulty (see the TTK convention on [page 02 of this chapter](/numerical/02))

Acceptance goals for the difficulty curve (example convention): early clear rates approach universal (tutorial levels ~99%, first 10 levels 95%+), declining monotonically with progress but with no single-point cliff (a single level with an oversized drop is a "turn-off wall"), with the first designed bottleneck placed after the new-player phase and before the first deep monetization / progression point — letting players "stall on capability, not stall on payment".

### Flow Channel and Difficulty Tiering

The classic flow model (matching challenge to ability, from Csikszentmihalyi's flow theory) operationalized in level design: challenge is set slightly above current ability, and the rate of ability growth keeps pace with the rate of challenge escalation. The landing tool is **difficulty tiering**: within the same level, tune three classes of knobs separately — mechanical difficulty (hands), strategic difficulty (brain), grind difficulty (liver/grind) — players weak mechanically compensate with strategy and progression, and do not raise all three lines at once. Each major level gets a convention-ized label (what this level mainly tests), and difficulty reviews check the labels to confirm only one class of knob was moved.

### Failure Penalties and Retry Pacing

The numerical essence of a failure penalty is "retry cost": stamina deduction, equipment durability, distance rollback. Guideline: **retry cost is proportional to the level's learning value** — zero-cost failure in the tutorial phase (unlimited retries), low-cost fast restart in the growth phase, and high-cost graduation challenges are acceptable (failure has weight), but it must satisfy "failure is attributable" (the player can say what they did wrong, only then is the penalty seen as fair). Retry pacing must also manage "flow cooldown": a forced wait after consecutive failures (loading animation, stamina recovery) that is too long turns frustration into a quit point.

### Numerical Guarantees for the New Player's First 30 Minutes

The new-player phase's numerical work has a hard checklist (time thresholds are example conventions):

- **First victory front-loaded**: the first battle is a guaranteed or near-guaranteed win (tutorial-phase clear-rate target ~99%), with victory feedback appearing within the first minute
- **First growth pulse**: the first noticeable numerical gain (level-up / first piece of equipment) is completed within the first 10 minutes, letting players see "getting stronger" with their own eyes
- **First bottleneck back-loaded and solvable**: the first level requiring thought appears after 20 minutes, and is solvable with already-taught mechanics
- **Numerical fail-safes**: tutorial-phase numbers are locked (attribute overflow and extreme inputs unreachable), and no randomness may affect tutorial completion (see variance management on [page 05 of this chapter](/numerical/05))
- **Pacing reconciliation**: the experience / resource yield per hour in the tutorial phase is several times the income of the growth phase (example convention 5-10×), then monotonically falls back — the new-player economy is "generous mode"

### Common Pitfalls

- **Difficulty tuned only by feel**: without clear-rate and churn-point data, difficulty review becomes the lead designer's personal experience statement
- **Cliff-style difficulty walls**: a sudden single-level clear-rate drop with no early-warning mechanism, relying on emergency difficulty compensation after launch — the difficulty curve needs monitoring and hot-tuning capability
- **One-size-fits-all penalties**: zero-cost failure all the way (no sense of achievement) or high-cost failure all the way (no inclusivity), penalties should rise with the phase
- **New-player numbers decoupled from the long-run curve**: being too generous in the tutorial phase makes minute 31 feel like a "suddenly broke" collapse
- **Difficulty tiering fails**: twisting all three knobs — hands, brain, grind — at once, and players cannot say which class they lost on
