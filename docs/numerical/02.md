# Combat Numbers and Damage Formula Families

## Category

Numerical tree. Node positioning: the defense-return shape of damage formula families, crit expectation, TTK acceptance and the three selection questions — the design side checked against the same formulas as the implementation side in the Feature Note.

## Definition


The damage formula is the skeleton of combat numbers. Section 3 of [Feature Note 02](/24-game-types-architecture/02-game-design) covers the four mainstream models and implementation points from the server-side angle; this page answers the other half from the design angle: what is the defense-return shape of each formula family, how to choose one, and how to accept the result against combat pacing (TTK) after choosing. The formulas on both sides are identical, and every number on this page can be recomputed.

## Problem

Choosing the wrong formula family dooms everything downstream to rework: the defense-return shape determines the player's intuition of "is stacking defense worth it", and TTK determines the acceptance of combat pacing feel. Memorizing formulas without asking about the shape, and not accepting against TTK, dooms a numerical proposal to rework at the review stage.


### Formula Families and Defense-Return Shape

Three basic families; the shape of the damage-reduction rate and Effective HP (EHP) determines the feel:

- **Subtractive family**: damage = attack − defense×k (floor of 1). Defense return is linear, and there exist a "defense is useless" region (attack far exceeds defense) and a "defense is invincible" region (defense approaches attack×2k) — suited to casual gameplay where the numerical range is controllable
- **Divisive family**: damage = attack × K/(K+defense), damage-reduction rate = defense/(defense+K). Defense return diminishes but **Effective HP is strictly linear**: EHP = HP×(1+defense/K); every additional K of defense doubles Effective HP — the default choice for card games and most ARPGs
- **Multiplicative zone family**: multiple damage-reduction sources each compute a percentage and are then multiplied together (0.8×0.7=56% damage taken). Naturally prevents "damage reduction exceeding 100%", the standard practice for MMOs and multi-buff systems (merge rules in the additive/multiplicative zones are in Section 2 of Feature Note 02)

The linear EHP of the divisive family is the root cause of its popularity: **the value of every point of defense is constant and predictable**, so both gear builds and progression returns are easy to explain. Example: K=600, HP=1000 — at defense 0, EHP is 1000; at defense 600, EHP is 2000; at defense 1200, EHP is 3000.

### Crits and Expected Damage

Crit parameters (crit rate c, crit multiplier m) contribute linearly to expected damage: expected = attack × (1 − c + c×m). Example: attack 100, crit rate 20%, crit multiplier 150%: expected = 100 × (0.8 + 0.2×1.5) = 110. **A stable expectation with controllable variance** is its value for PVP — variance size determines the "luck content"; competitive gameplay should compress variance (low crit rate or pity-style randomness), progression gameplay can amplify variance to create surprises.

### Combat Pacing: TTK Is the First Acceptance Metric

After the formula is chosen, the first thing to compute is not damage but **TTK (Time to Kill, effective kill duration)**: target EHP ÷ actual DPS. It is the combat metric closest to the player's direct feel — elite monsters in action games typically fall between a few seconds and a dozen-plus, while card games' single-round burst and total match duration form their own system. TTK acceptance requires three curves: **starting gear** (the TTK upper bound when the player is weakest, to prevent turn-off), **graduation gear** (the TTK lower bound at peak strength, to prevent content being one-shot), and **version inflation** (the DPS growth rate between two versions, to control numerical inflation speed).

Two levers for inflation control: **horizontal inflation** (new content complexity and mechanic depth increase, numbers stay flat) and **vertical inflation** (numbers themselves rise, old content devalues into "daily grind"). Vertical inflation must have explicit version discipline (a cap on per-version growth), otherwise catch-up cost will kill new-player retention — this is the number one numerical risk for most long-running live games.

### The Three Selection Questions for Formula Families

1. **How wide is the numerical range**: controllable range choose the subtractive family (most intuitive), uncontrollable range choose the divisive family (constant return, no runaway)
2. **Do you need multi-source stacking**: with multi-source buff/equipment damage reduction, go straight to the multiplicative zone family, avoiding the saturation problem of additive merging
3. **PVP or PVE dominant**: PVP compresses variance and watches the TTK range; PVE leans more on variance and mechanics to create the memorable "legendary hit"

### Common Pitfalls

- **Setting damage before duration**: "how much damage does this skill do" is the result, "how long do I want this fight to take" is the design
- **Computing expectation but not distribution**: a skill with expectation 110 may actually hit 80 or 150 per strike — the distribution shape determines feel and fairness
- **Defense parameter K pulled out of thin air**: K determines the "defense saturation point"; it should be back-solved from the target gear's defense range and written into the formula document
- **Inflation without discipline**: every new version adds coefficients; a year later old content is effectively obsolete and new players cannot catch up
- **Ignoring pity interaction**: after crit, hit, block and other layered randomness stack, the worst-case experience (missing everything in a row) must be caught by pseudo-randomness or pity (see Section 4 of Feature Note 02 and page 05 of this chapter)
