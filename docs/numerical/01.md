# The Work and Conventions of a Numerical Designer

## Category

Numerical tree. Node positioning: the responsibility boundaries of the numerical designer, deliverables, the four must-answer convention points for collaboration with programming, and the verification loop — the translator from "game feel" to "computable".

## Definition


The numerical designer is not a "spreadsheet-filler" but the quantitative owner of gameplay experience: the "game feel", "thrill", and "suppression phase" in a designer's mouth must all land as computable numbers, and must be pre-verified before implementation and cross-checked after launch. This page defines the boundaries, deliverables, and collaboration conventions of this work.

## Problem

Treating the numerical designer as a "spreadsheet-filler" systematically misses two failure points: the "game feel / thrill / suppression phase" in designers' mouths is not translated into numbers by anyone, and the numbers computed by programmers are not verified by anyone. Without convention alignment and a closed loop, numbers are wrong before implementation and unknown after launch.


### Responsibility Boundaries

A numerical designer is typically responsible for four kinds of questions: **set standards** (how long a match lasts, how much growth per season, how many seconds to beat a boss — experience goals come before numbers), **build models** (attribute structure, formula families, curve families, the structure of economy and drops), **populate numbers** (expand the models into implementable tables and parameters), and **verify results** (cross-check experience goals against data before and after launch). What they are not responsible for matters equally: system rule structure belongs to the gameplay designer, and implementation correctness belongs to the server side (the "four technical questions for receiving a numerical requirement" in Section 1 of [Feature Note 02](/24-game-types-architecture/02-game-design) is the dialogue script at the boundary between the two sides).

### Deliverables

Three things make up the standard delivery of a numerical designer: a **formula document** (symbol definitions, formulas, parameter tables, boundary conditions — so a reader can recompute without looking at code), a **numerical table** (concrete values expanded by level/level tier/rarity, the shared data source for server and client), and a **balance report** (projection of key metrics: TTK, growth-curve comparison, economy faucet-and-sink reconciliation, drop expectation). The shared acceptance criterion for all three deliverables is **recomputability**: a third party can compute any intermediate value from the formulas and parameters without asking anyone.

### Convention Alignment: Four Must-Answer Items for Collaboration with Development

Before handing numerical tables to the server side, four things must be written clearly — these correspond one-to-one with the four implementation questions in Feature Note 02:

1. **Calculation timing**: are attributes recomputed in real time or snapshotted on entering combat (determines the trigger point of the attribute recomputation pipeline)
2. **Merge rules**: do multi-source bonuses enter the additive zone first or the multiplicative zone (the additive/multiplicative zone rule in Section 2 of Feature Note 02)
3. **Randomness convention**: independent per roll or a pseudo-random distribution, and who owns the seed (determines whether replay and audit are possible at all)
4. **Rounding and caps**: rounding direction for damage, overflow handling, and the hard caps of each number (determines whether behavior under extreme values is controllable)

All four of these should have a definite answer in any requirement review — a numerical requirement without answers will inevitably be reworked during implementation.

### Verification Loop

The closed loop of numerical work is "design → projection → implementation → canary → cross-check": before launch, verify the model with projection (tables, simulation scripts, a controlled-size closed beta); after launch, cross-check the same convention against real data (clear rate, TTK distribution, economy faucet-and-sink ledger). The easiest thing to trip over in cross-checking is convention drift — the "clear rate" meant at design time and the "clear rate" tallied by the analytics events are not the same denominator. The numerical designer should proactively cross-check the definition of conventions (the data-side conventions are in [Feature Note 12 "Game Data Analysis"]( /24-game-types-architecture/12-data-analytics) and [Chapter 14, Operations and Data Analytics](/operation/bi/)).

### Common Pitfalls

- **Populate numbers before setting standards**: answer "how many seconds should this boss take" before "how much HP should this boss have", otherwise there is nothing to evaluate any number against
- **Formulas without derivation**: giving only a result table and no formula makes re-derivation impossible when a designer leaves or requirements change
- **One-sidedly defined conventions**: if numerical conventions are not aligned with the server and data teams during review, everyone tells a different story after launch
- **Using feel to override data**: after launch, "it feels wrong" but no metric to back it up — experience goals must be converted into measurable indicators at design time
