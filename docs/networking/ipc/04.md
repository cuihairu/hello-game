# Low Latency vs. High Throughput Tradeoff

## Category

networking / IPC. Node position: the pull and the pricing among five goals — latency, throughput, ordering, reliability, and cost.

## Definition

The most common illusion in message systems is believing that low latency, high throughput, strong ordering, strong reliability, and low cost can all be obtained easily at the same time. Reality is usually not that one of them cannot be achieved, but that every time you strengthen one goal, the other goals start to get more expensive.

So when discussing a communication approach, you cannot look only at average throughput or peak QPS; you must first ask clearly: does this link fear being slow more, or fear backlog more; fear jitter more, or fear loss more; does it need to complete immediately, or does it need to hold steady at the peak.

### Why low latency and high throughput naturally pull against each other

Chasing extremely low latency usually means:

- Reducing queueing
- Reducing batch processing
- Reducing intermediate hops
- Staying as close as possible to the caller and the executor

Chasing extremely high throughput usually means:

- Accepting queueing and buffering
- Accepting batch processing
- Accepting deeper asynchronous links
- Accepting consumers pulling at their own pace

These two sets of strategies are naturally in tension. In other words, a message system can help raise total throughput, but it often makes the moment a single message finishes less certain.

## Problem

### Which links lean toward low-latency priority

Links that lean toward low-latency priority usually have these characteristics:

- Directly affecting the player's current interaction
- Extremely sensitive to tail latency
- A small context per processing unit, but a very tight time window
- Backlog itself equals business failure

For example:

- In-match real-time input and feedback
- Key state confirmation
- A player entering or leaving a real-time instance

These links do not so much pursue large throughput as fear any unpredictable queueing.

### Which links lean toward high-throughput priority

Links that lean toward high-throughput priority are usually:

- A single message does not have to finish immediately
- Batch processing is allowed
- Peak traffic is pronounced
- The business can tolerate second-level or even minute-level latency

For example:

- Event tracking reporting
- Log collection
- Reports and statistics
- Batch notifications
- Historical archival

What these links really need to solve is not crushing the system at the peak, but getting each single message processed as soon as possible.

### Why a message queue is good at peak shaving, and also good at creating backlog

One of the values of a queue is flattening instantaneous peaks and decoupling producers from consumers. But the same mechanism brings another reality: if consumers long cannot keep up, the system will not immediately error — it will hide the problem in the form of backlog.

Once backlog occurs, the consequence is usually not simply "a little slower," but:

- Event value decays over time
- The ordering window gets stretched
- Failed retries pile on more pressure
- The state the business sees lags further and further

So peak shaving is not a free capability; it must be designed together with capacity planning, backpressure strategy, and expiration strategy.

### What actually needs to be designed is backpressure, not just throughput

Once a link's traffic rises, sooner or later it faces one question: what to do when the downstream cannot keep up. The common options are only a few:

- Blocking the upstream
- Rate-limiting and dropping
- Backing up continuously
- Degraded handling
- Keeping only the latest value or key values

Different links have completely different answers. Event tracking and logs allow sampling and dropping; a player's key settlement cannot be lost and can only be retried or compensated; real-time state broadcast may rather overwrite an old message than keep all history.

If the backpressure strategy is not settled first, so-called high throughput is just trading the risk for a systemic backlog at some future moment.

### Average performance is meaningless; tail performance is what determines the experience

Many communication approaches look beautiful on throughput, but the moment you look at tail latency and backlog recovery time, they fall apart. For a game system this is especially critical:

- An average of 5 ms but an occasional 500 ms — what the player perceives is the 500 ms
- An average of a hundred thousand messages per second, but peak recovery takes ten minutes — what the business perceives is the ten minutes

So performance judgment cannot look only at the mean; it must also look at:

- P95 / P99 latency
- Backlog growth rate
- Peak recovery time
- Degraded behavior when the downstream fails

### A tradeoff method closer to engineering reality

The steadier approach is usually:

- The primary path prioritizes latency and determinism
- The peripheral path prioritizes throughput and decoupling
- High-frequency links minimize Broker and persistence hops
- Large-throughput links must design backlog governance and expiration strategy

This is not conservatism, but admitting that what different links truly fear is not the same problem.

### Common pitfalls

The most common error is designing a real-time link with the throughput thinking of a batch-processing link. The second error is looking only at production speed, not at the consumer's long-term processing capacity, so the queue backs up for the long run. The third error is having no backpressure or expiration strategy at all, assuming every message should just keep queuing, and dragging the problem from "slow right now" into "everything lagging later."

### Practical case: a missing-backpressure investigation where "the evening peak queue grows and grows, and ranking refresh drags to nine seconds"

> This case is a demo example (not drawn from a real project), with numbers rounded and simplified. It takes this page's "Why a message queue is good at peak shaving, and also good at creating backlog", "What actually needs to be designed is backpressure, not just throughput", and "Average performance is meaningless; tail performance is what determines the experience" into deep water: backlog does not error, so backlog is the most dangerous — this is the tail investigation into peak backlog, and the full unfolding of "if the backpressure strategy is not settled first, so-called high throughput is just trading the risk for a systemic backlog at some future moment."

**Background and symptoms**: A project mixed event tracking, logs, and settlement peripheral events (ranking refresh, achievement progress) into the same MQ pipeline, selected by throughput metrics, and the consumer group had no backpressure or expiration strategy. At the evening peak, entry traffic was **80,000** messages/second (nearly seven times the daily **12,000**/second), backlog peaked at **14,000,000** messages, and recovery took **22** minutes; during that time ranking refresh P99 rose from **180** ms to **9** seconds, while the mean only rose from **180** ms to **260** ms; the peak produced **60** business alerts.

### Step 1: Symptoms and definitions — three ledgers: backlog, tail, and alerts

Backlog definition: peak entry **80,000**/second, the consumer group cannot keep up, and every gap becomes backlog — peak **14,000,000** messages, recovery **22** minutes — the system does not error, it just lags further and further; event value decays over time, the ordering window gets stretched, failed retries pile on more pressure, and the business state lags further and further.

Tail definition: ranking refresh P99 rose from **180** ms to **9** seconds, while the mean only rose from **180** ms to **260** ms — what the player and the business perceive is the tail, not the mean.

Alert definition: **60** business alerts during the peak, all concentrated on ranking refresh and peripheral-event timeouts — these are downstream symptoms of backlog, not independent failures.

Item by item: this page's five backpressure options (block the upstream, rate-limit and drop, back up continuously, degraded handling, keep only the latest or key values) — the system silently chose "back up continuously" with no one making the decision; event tracking and logs allow sampling and dropping, yet they shared one pipeline with settlement peripherals; of the four tail metrics (P95/P99, backlog growth rate, peak recovery time, degraded behavior on downstream failure), only throughput was reported. The 60 alerts attributed one by one: 30 + 18 + 12 = 60, and the ledger closes.

### Step 3: Root cause — backpressure is the first question of link design, and it was skipped

This page says in its own words: if the backpressure strategy is not settled first, so-called high throughput is just trading the risk for a systemic backlog at some future moment; once backlog occurs, the system will not immediately error — it will hide the problem in the form of backlog. The team did not perform the pricing step of "does this link fear being slow more, or fear backlog more," and the high-throughput acceptance metric became the only metric.

### Step 4: Handling and backfill — settle backpressure first, then talk throughput

1. Link tiering: settlement peripheral events get an independent pipeline that prioritizes latency; event tracking and logs get an independent pipeline that prioritizes throughput; high-frequency links minimize Broker and persistence hops; large-throughput links get backlog governance and expiration strategy.
2. Backpressure and expiration settled first: each link first answers the five options — block, drop, back up, degrade, keep the latest — before going live; backlog over the threshold is cleared per the expiration strategy, and event tracking auto-degrades at the peak.
3. Tail metrics on the dashboard: P95/P99, backlog growth rate, peak recovery time, and degraded behavior on downstream failure are listed as link SLOs; the mean is only for reference.

Review ledger: backlog recovery **22** minutes → **90** seconds; ranking refresh P99 **9** seconds → **160** ms; peak alerts **60** → **5**.

### Three lessons from the case

1. **The average is for the report; the player perceives the tail** — an average of 5 ms with an occasional 500 ms means what the player perceives is the 500 ms.
2. **Backlog does not error, so backlog is the most dangerous** — the problem gets hidden until the business is lagging across the board, and only then does it surface.
3. **Backpressure is the first question of link design** — high throughput without settled backpressure is just trading the risk for a future systemic backlog.
