# Events, Leaderboards, Matchmaking and Social

## Category

system / general services subtree. Node position: the four platformization candidates of events, leaderboards, matchmaking and social — the real difficulty of each one (rule execution, caliber and update cost, not filling a room, relationships and governance) and how to judge it.

## Definition


These kinds of systems are often put in the same chapter, not because their implementation approaches are the same, but because they all carry a strong "operations amplifier" character. When traffic is normally steady, they look like nothing more than peripheral capabilities; but once a new season goes live, an event opens, a streamer brings volume, or a holiday return happens, these systems simultaneously become the traffic center, the dispute center and the failure center.

## Problem

These four are most easily built as "page + database table": events are hard in rule execution, not the page; leaderboards are hard in caliber and update cost; matchmaking is not queueing until a room is full; social is hard in relationships and governance. Get the difficulty wrong and what you build looks presentable but does not work.


### Why they become platformization candidates

Events, leaderboards, matchmaking and social all share a few common traits:

- Rules change frequently, strongly tied to version and operations cadence
- Lots of reads and lots of writes with obvious peaks, prone to bursting at fixed points in time
- Need to connect multiple business systems, such as combat results, asset rewards, friend relationships, chat, guilds, mail
- Hard to describe with database tables alone, often also needing scheduling, state machines, real-time computation and backend tooling

So teams often ask: should these capabilities be in-project modules or platform services? The answer is usually not uniform, because the "degree of commonality" of the four is actually different.

### The difficulty of an event system is not the page, but rule execution

Event systems are the most easily underestimated. On the surface it looks like a backend configuration problem, but in reality it involves:

- Start and end times and timezone handling
- Audience filtering and gray-release switches
- Condition accumulation, quest progress, reward claim state
- Resource drop and asset re-issuance
- Coupling with version updates, channel review and payment events

Many teams implement "config table + if else" quickly in the early days, which works well in the short term, but once event types multiply two problems appear:

- The configuration is not expressive enough, so you keep adding special fields and special logic
- The configuration is too expressive, so operations can assemble execution paths nobody actually understands

So an event system usually has to find a balance between "templating" and "scripting". Templating is safer, more reusable and easier to audit; scripting is more flexible, but testing and rollback costs are high. Long-running projects generally turn high-frequency events into standard templates, put a few complex events in controlled extension points, and do not let every event run on a custom script.

### The core problem of a leaderboard is caliber and update cost

A leaderboard is not finished with one Redis `zset`. What is really hard are three things:

- What is the ranking basis, and is the caliber stable
- Is the update real-time writes, near-real-time aggregation, or offline settlement
- How are reward issuance and appeal handling reconciled

Different leaderboards differ a lot in implementation:

- A power leaderboard emphasizes fast queries and stable display, but power itself may be an aggregated value with a very long update path
- A speedrun leaderboard emphasizes write real-time-ness and cheat verification, otherwise the top few entries keep being disputed
- Guild and cross-server leaderboards involve partitioning, grouping, season rollover and reward batch processing

Without a stable caliber, a leaderboard becomes a source of operations incidents. Typical problems include:

- After a power calculation version switch, old data is not recomputed and the leaderboard shifts overnight
- Cross-server leaderboard grouping logic changes and some players are put into the wrong pool
- Delayed battle results arrive after the leaderboard has settled, and there is no compensation rule when they are recomputed

### A matchmaking system is not "queue until a room is full"

What a matchmaking system really balances is wait time, fairness, perceived quality and system cost. Room-based light games can often accept a simple Elo or tier-range widening strategy, but hard-real-time competitive games face more constraints:

- Whether network quality is part of the matching conditions
- Whether party and solo queue share a pool
- Whether AI or bots participate as a fallback
- Whether cross-server matching amplifies latency and cheat risk

If matchmaking is made too independent it disconnects from the real game experience; if it is embedded entirely in the battle server it is hard to reuse across modes or do pool-wide scheduling. A common approach is:

- A unified matchmaking service maintains pools, buckets, strategies and queue state
- The business side supplies real capability tags, for example rank tier, hero pool, network quality, party composition
- The room or battle service is responsible for the final room creation and start validation

The easiest mistake here is to treat the matching result as final fact, and ignore the validation on "the last hop before entering the room". In reality, disconnects, backgrounding the app, party changes and service load changes can all make an apparently valid match result fail at room entry.

### The real difficulty of a social system is relationships and governance

Friends, guilds, chat, private messages, blacklist, mentor-disciple and party invites look like "community features", but they significantly change the data model and governance cost.

As long as a project does social heavily, it must answer the following questions:

- Is a friend relationship one-way or two-way, and how do deletions and blockings propagate
- Who maintains the authoritative state of guild members, roles, approvals, logs and contributions
- How long do chat messages need to be kept, and which channels need review or traceback
- How do minor protection, report handling and mute punishments land in concrete systems

Social systems are hard to fully close inside a project, because they naturally connect to risk control, support and audit systems. A mature approach is usually:

- Relationship state is maintained by the social service
- Business events are fed to the social presentation layer through standard interfaces
- Chat and reports go through an independent governance path
- Heavy gameplay logic such as guild wars and party dungeons stays in the project business services

### A more useful way to judge at design time

Rather than asking "can events, leaderboards, matchmaking and social be shared", ask separately:

- Which rules change most frequently, and therefore need console configurability
- Which state is most sensitive, and therefore needs strong audit and strong idempotency
- Which path is the peak-traffic entry, and therefore needs independent scaling and rate limiting
- Which capability is reused across multiple gameplay modes, and therefore worth extracting into a platform service

### Common mistakes

- Cramming every event into one over-complex event engine for reuse, so nobody dares to change it
- Only doing leaderboard display, not caliber management, season archival and reward reconciliation
- Matchmaking strategy looks only at numerical fairness, ignoring network quality, party composition and wait experience
- Treating social as a UI feature, with no reports, mutes, log retention or permission governance

The shared rule for these systems is: once they have real impact on revenue, retention and public sentiment, they can no longer be treated as "peripheral features". What they need is not more pages, but clearer state boundaries, more complete backend tooling and stronger online governance capability.
