# Transactions, Payments and the Economy System

## Category

system / general services subtree. Node position: the accounting essence of transactions, payments and the economy — the asynchronous uncertainty of the payment path, the economy is not inventory arithmetic, the key boundaries and the trade-offs between approaches.

## Definition


The biggest misconception about payment and economy systems is that many people split them into two problems: payment belongs to platform integration, economy belongs to game designers. In reality the two are highly coupled, because every top-up, fulfillment, consumption, exchange, auction, gacha, rebate and refund ultimately lands on the same core question: did the asset change correctly, and can we explain why each change happened.

## Problem

Treat transactions and assets first as an accounting problem: every change must have a transaction record and be reconcilable. The real difficulty of payment is asynchrony and uncertainty (lost callbacks, duplicate crediting); the economy system is not inventory arithmetic — a faucet-sink imbalance drags down the whole payment model.


### First treat transactions and assets as an accounting problem

As long as the system has paid currency, scarce items, player-to-player exchange or platform revenue share, the economy system is no longer just "numerical design". It is first an accounting system, and it must at least be able to answer:

- What is the current balance of a given asset
- Through which events did this balance accumulate
- Whether a given order is paid, fulfilled, refunded or re-issued
- When a complaint or anomaly occurs, can the full transaction record be restored

If these cannot be answered, any discussion of "economy balance" loses its foundation, because you are not even sure of the real asset state.

### The real difficulty of the payment path is asynchrony and uncertainty

A typical payment path contains at least these stages: client order placement, channel payment, server-side order verification, order crediting, fulfillment, receipt, make-up orders, refund. The trouble is that almost none of these stages completes atomically, and there are a large number of duplicate and out-of-order notifications.

So payment design must assume these facts by default:

- Client callbacks cannot be trusted; the server must actively verify the order
- Channel notifications may arrive more than once, or be delayed for a long time
- An order being created successfully does not mean payment succeeded, and payment succeeding does not mean fulfillment succeeded
- Even after fulfillment succeeds, refunds, chargebacks and disputed orders can still happen

Mature systems generally decouple the order state machine from asset fulfillment:

- The order service records the order's main state, channel transaction records, verification result and callback logs
- The asset service makes the actual currency and item changes
- The two collaborate through idempotent fulfillment keys, events or task compensation

The cost is a longer path, but the benefit is that payment and assets can each be audited on their own, and one duplicate notification will not fulfill the goods twice.

### The economy system is not "inventory arithmetic"

A game economy has at least three layers:

- Asset layer: concrete objects such as diamonds, gold, stamina, items, coupons and shards
- Rule layer: production, consumption, exchange, drops, pity ceiling, taxes, trade restrictions
- Market layer: auction house, player trading, gift packs, in-game shop, event rebates, ad monetization

Different projects emphasize different things. A more single-player-leaning product may only need to keep the shop and inventory correct; a heavily social or heavily trading product must additionally handle:

- Whether player-to-player transfer is allowed, and to what degree
- Whether bound and unbound currency both exist
- Whether scarce items may circulate on a secondary market
- How gacha pity ceiling and probability disclosure land in real transaction records

Once player-to-player trading is allowed, system complexity rises noticeably, because you must care not only about "what the system issued" but also "what players moved between each other" and "whether anyone is laundering money, reselling, farming accounts or cashing out".

### The most critical boundaries when designing

### Separate order authority from asset authority

Whether an order is paid successfully is judged by the order service; whether the asset really reached the player is judged by the asset service. Do not let any single service both "say the money arrived" and "directly change all assets", otherwise reconciliation and compensation become very painful.

### Asset changes must be recorded as a transaction stream

Whether you use database transactions, event logs or dual-write compensation: as long as assets change, there should be a queryable change stream, recording at least:

- Who the asset belongs to
- What was changed
- The value before and after the change
- Which source event or order number caused it
- Who initiated, who approved, who executed

This serves not only ledger tracing, but also risk control and support when handling disputes.

### High-value operations must support freeze and rollback strategies

For example auction settlement, cross-server trades, refund clawback, correcting an accidental grant: none of these should be finished with a single immediate debit-credit transaction record. Many scenarios need a three-phase flow of "pre-authorization freeze → settle when the condition is met → roll back on anomaly", otherwise anomalous states are hard to converge.

### Common approaches and their trade-offs

### Payment and fulfillment embedded in the game server

The benefit is fast implementation and a short path. The drawback is that order records, channel callbacks, asset fulfillment and make-up order logic are all mixed together, so when something goes wrong it is hard to tell whether it is a payment problem or a business problem. Only suitable for small projects with a simple payment shape.

### Unified order center plus project asset service

This is the balance point most mid-size teams can maintain long term. The unified order center handles channel adaptation, order verification, reconciliation and refund processing; the project asset service only handles "what to issue given a trusted order". This makes onboarding new channels and unified audit much easier, while the project still keeps its own economy model.

### Economy platformization

Going further, the shop, gift packs, price tables, fulfillment templates, promotional events and rebate rules are also platformized. The benefit is high operations efficiency and unified configuration; the cost is that business innovation is constrained, and once the platform rules are not expressive enough, the project will build its own side channel around the platform.

### Common mistakes

- Merging order state and fulfillment state into one field, making make-up orders and ledger tracing impossible to handle precisely
- Only recording the final balance, not the asset transaction stream, so when something goes wrong you can only guess
- The refund path only integrates with the channel, without handling in-game asset clawback or debt state
- Treating the economy system as pure designer configuration, without letting engineering take part in accounting and risk design

What truly marks a mature payment and economy system is not how fancy the shop page looks, but whether after one complaint, one refund, one accidental mass grant in an event, or one attack from the black market, you can still use a clear ledger to bring the problem back into shape.
