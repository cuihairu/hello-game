# Persistent Online World Architecture

## Category

design / problem-domains subtree. Node position: crosswise quick reference for the persistent online world problem domain — world always resident, interest management, boundary slicing, scaling with state.

## Definition


This page generalizes persistent online world projects from the problem-domain angle. It is the crosswise quick-reference edition of the main line's world model and capacity chapters. It does not re-derive how to split the world or how to divide the services; instead it puts the judgments that distinguish this kind of project from other types onto one page: why the world stays resident, how interests are managed, how boundaries are cut, how stateful services scale and migrate.

One-sentence portrait: world state exists long term, players only enter and leave sessions, and maps, guilds, social relations, and world progress all still hold after players go offline. MMORPG, open-world mobile games, SLG big maps, and survival-crafting online all fall into this portrait.

## Problem

The judgments for world-type projects concentrate on four resident facts: why the world cannot be emptied, how interests are managed (AOI and split frequency), how cross-server boundaries are cut, how stateful services scale and migrate. The answers to these four lock each other in place, and looking at them in isolation easily loses sight of one side.


### Characteristics of this problem domain

- World state is resident; restart and downtime both have explicit cost, and rolling maintenance is often the normal state.
- Space is the main organizing dimension; position decides who can see whom and who interacts with whom.
- Player sessions are short while world progress is long; a session interruption should not damage world state.
- Social structure accumulates on the server; guilds, friends, and trading relations span multiple online periods.
- The online count swings heavily by time of day, while the world cannot be born and die on demand the way a room can.

The first three characteristics mean the state of this kind of project cannot be snapshotted and rebuilt as a whole; the last two mean it cannot scale freely at session granularity. This forms the most essential dividing line with the room-based type.

### AOI and split frequency: the two rulers of interest management

A spatially continuous world cannot let every client see all state, so interest management is usually the first architectural constraint.

- AOI decides who is visible: it bounds the sync range by line of sight, screen, or region, and the range strategy directly affects the bandwidth curve and the ceiling of the gameplay.
- Split frequency decides how fast it is visible: moving objects update at high frequency, stationary ones at low frequency, and distant players often get only summary information.
- The two rulers have to be tuned together: if the AOI is drawn wide and the split frequency is not lowered, bandwidth runs away fast; if the split frequency is cut too hard, melee judgment and interaction start to show visible misalignment.
- The common practice is fine split frequency within line of sight, degradation to events outside it, and a dedicated channel for cross-region interaction rather than one uniform frequency globally.

Interest-management parameters are usually treated as a tuning target and iterated for a long time. A single mis-tuning typically shows up as bandwidth exploding at peak, or players reporting that they are clearly standing together but cannot see each other.

### Trade-offs on cross-server boundaries

The bigger the world, the more necessary it is to cut boundaries in the right place, and where the cut lands often decides the ceiling of the gameplay.

- Splitting by map and by server is the most direct cut: boundaries are clear and implementation is simple, at the cost of players being hard-separated.
- Line splitting allows multiple instances of the same map, with better capacity elasticity, at the cost of social being sliced apart by lines and requiring cross-line visibility and teleportation mechanisms.
- A cross-server gameplay layer moves strong-interaction gameplay such as dungeons, competitive, and trading into independent clusters, leaving only world and social accumulation on the home server, at the cost of data ownership and settlement chains being adjudicated across servers.
- A seamless big world does dynamic load balancing by coordinates, with the most continuous experience and the highest implementation and operations cost.

The fewer the boundaries, the more continuous the experience; the more boundaries, the easier the scaling. The common practice is to decide based on each gameplay's real need for continuity, rather than cutting uniformly.

### Scaling and migration of stateful services

Most services in this kind of project carry state, so scaling cannot be as simple as adding and removing instances the way it is for stateless services, which makes migration the core problem.

- Downtime migration is the most common and the safest, relocating by whole map or by whole server, at the cost of a maintenance window.
- Dynamic migration moves online players from high-load nodes to low-load nodes with a smooth experience, but it requires serializable state, an atomic migration process, and rollback on failure.
- Scaling by line or by map is the compromise: open lines at peak and merge them at valley, with the boundary still a clear map or line.
- Any migration has to answer state ownership: world references, queues, and trading contexts attached to a player — how they follow or are suspended during migration.

Projects whose migration is done poorly usually fail not at the migration itself but because state ownership was never defined, and only at migration time is it discovered that half the state lives in process memory.

### Common solution shapes

The typical shape is three layers stacked: a world layer with resident maps and NPCs, a session layer managing player connections and line of sight, and a cross-server layer taking up strong-interaction gameplay.

- The world layer is sharded by map or by coordinates, with each shard usually advanced authoritatively in a single process.
- The session layer is stateless or lightly stateful, handling access, routing, and AOI filtering.
- The cross-server layer is an independent cluster per gameplay, interacting with the home server over an explicit protocol, with settlement sheets written back to the home server.
- Global services such as rankings, mail, and friends are deployed independently and shared across maps.

The elasticity of this shape comes mainly from the session layer and the cross-server layer; the world layer is often hard to make elastic, so capacity planning is usually computed from peak map pressure rather than average online count.

### Pitfalls specific to this type

The world process is treated as a single point: the core map or a global service cannot be sharded, so once peak crosses the threshold the only option is downtime, with no place to apply scaling.

AOI parameters hardcoded: designers change a gameplay range, art changes the field-of-view distance, and nobody coordinates the sync parameters, so the problem surfaces only after launch, as bandwidth or stutter.

Cross-server boundaries moved back and forth: trading moves into cross-server today, competitive moves back to the home server tomorrow, and every move is another round of data-ownership rework.

Migration tested only on the happy path: dynamic migration was verified only at low load, so migrating at peak loses contexts such as queues, trading, and guild applications, with failures concentrated at exactly the moment they should not happen.

### Common pitfalls

- Building the world type as a "super room," with the whole world in one process; early development is fast, but the capacity ceiling arrives quickly.
- Treating AOI and split frequency as pure performance parameters, ignoring that they also define gameplay boundaries, so later changes drag the gameplay along.
- Assuming that moving to the cloud gives automatic scaling, ignoring the migration cost of stateful services; elasticity only holds at the session layer.

The architectural direction of this kind of project is usually summarized as: a resident world sharded for governance, interest management paired with split frequency, boundaries cut by gameplay, and state ownership defined before migration.
