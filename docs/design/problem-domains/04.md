# Long-Cycle Progression Architecture

## Category

design / problem-domains subtree. Node position: crosswise quick reference for the long-cycle progression problem domain — state accumulation, asynchronous settlement, rolling and merging servers, economy governance, long-run data migration.

## Definition


This page generalizes long-cycle progression projects from the problem-domain angle. It is the crosswise quick-reference edition of the problem-domain appendix. It does not re-derive why the asset model and the operations chains take this shape; instead it puts the key judgments that distinguish this kind of project from other types onto one page: how state accumulates, how asynchronous settlement works, how servers roll and merge, how the economy is governed, and how data survives three years.

One-sentence portrait: the account is the unit that carries state, a session is just an access window, value accumulates on a scale of months and years, and the operations cadence touches the data more frequently than gameplay iteration does. Idle and AFK games, card cultivation, SLG progression lines, MMO progression lines, and simulation management all fall into this portrait.

## Problem

The judgments for progression-type projects concentrate on the time scale of the data: how state accumulates, how asynchronous settlement works, how servers roll and merge, how the economy is governed, and how data survives three years. All five of these grow with operations time, and the cost of fixing them after the fact is a bookkeeping incident.


### Characteristics of this problem domain

- State lifetimes are measured in accounts and count in months and years; any single write error means facing the "roll back to three months ago" problem.
- Interaction is heavily asynchronous — offline earnings, mail distribution, AFK settlement, scheduled rewards — where the moment the computation happens and the moment the player perceives it are separated.
- Numbers are the gameplay themselves; progression curves, currency production and sink, and stacked events directly decide retention and revenue.
- Operations intensity drives the shape of the systems; events, versions, compensation, and rollback touch core data more often than new gameplay does.
- Once data has accumulated it is hard to rebuild; deletion and modification both carry audit and compliance cost.

The first three characteristics push the complexity of this kind of project onto data lifetime, and the last two push it onto the operations chains; these two main lines make up its basic profile and what sets it apart from the room-based and world types.

### Boundaries with adjacent types

- Compared with the room-based session type, its in-session process is usually very light; what truly needs long-run governance is the out-of-session assets, progress, and relationships — the two are effectively mirror images.
- Compared with the persistent online world type, what is resident is the account rather than the world, and the difficulty lies not in space and concurrent interaction but in data versions, migration, and audit.
- Compared with the economy/platform type, its economy is a designed production-and-sink curve where currency mostly flows between players and the system, rather than being freely priced and circulated between players.

To judge which type a project belongs to, a common approach is to look at the dominant contradiction: if word-of-mouth depends mainly on "are the numbers right, was the wrong thing granted, is the data still there after downtime," it is progression-type led, even if it also hangs combat and social systems off it.

### Architectural constraints derived from the characteristics

Long-cycle state constrains the data model: object structures have to evolve with versions, snapshots and ledgers usually coexist, and rollback and audit capability usually has to be built in the first year rather than bolted on after an incident.

Asynchronous interaction constrains the write path: crediting usually funnels into a unified asset-change interface, where each change has an idempotent marker, a ledger entry, is retryable, and never double-issues.

Numbers-as-gameplay constrains the config chain: progression curves, currency rules, and event orchestration are usually fully configured, and config changes often need canary, validation, and rollback, with a risk magnitude no smaller than a code release.

Operations intensity constrains audit and compensation: reward entry points usually converge to a small number, each traceable and reconcilable, and compensation flows through the same chain as normal issuance so no second path bypasses audit.

### Asynchronous interaction and offline settlement

Asynchrony is the most everyday and most error-prone part of this kind of project, because it splits "computed" and "issued" into two separate moments.

- Offline earnings are usually computed at login or in a scheduled task; the common practice is to set a duration cap and an earnings cap so a single return settlement does not tear through the numeric curve.
- Mail often takes the role of the general-purpose asynchronous issuance channel, with attachments, validity periods, and claim state; claiming an attachment itself has to be idempotent.
- Scheduled settlement and player actions may reach the same asset concurrently; the common protection is a version number or a conditional write rather than read-then-write.
- The settlement boundary across days and time zones must be defined in exactly one place in the system; multiple places each judging "today" is usually the source of double rewards.

The core of the settlement trade-off is better late than wrong: issuing late gets player complaints, issuing wrong loses player trust, and the two recovery costs differ by an order of magnitude.

### Rolling servers and server merge

Rolling servers is the most common capacity and monetization shape for this kind of product: each server is an independent data domain, capacity rolls out horizontally on the server-open schedule, per-server scale stays controllable, and the blast radius of a failure is isolated per server.

- The cost of rolling servers accumulates at the end of the lifecycle: at merge time, name conflicts, character ID conflicts, ranking rebuilds, social relation merging, and trading-record ownership all have to be handled.
- A server merge is essentially one long-run data migration, where years of accumulated zombie accounts, dirty data, and cross-server assets all surface at once.
- The common practice is to reserve globally unique IDs and namespaces from day one of opening a server, downgrading a merge from "data stitching" to "namespace consolidation."
- Rankings and cross-server gameplay are usually rebuilt or migrated to the cross-server cluster at merge time, with the home server keeping only basic assets.
- Rolling-server products often have several data-structure versions in existence at once, with a generation gap between old and new servers; cross-version migration at merge time is the hardest tier of migration problems.

Projects that plan capacity only on single-server peak and never rehearse a merge are often forced to take down and merge mid-product life, which is exactly the moment revenue cannot stop.

### Economy accumulation and inflation governance

The total currency supply in long-run operations usually only grows, never shrinks, so inflation often does not appear in the first month but shows up a year later in the form of prices and churn.

- Production outlets and sink outlets have to be designed as pairs; sink outlets are usually continuous-consumption gameplay such as repair, consumption, crafting, and gacha, rather than one-off large sinks.
- A common currency practice is classified management, with bound currency and circulating currency accounted separately, and the fewer outlets that feed circulation the easier it is to govern.
- Inflation governance depends on ledger-level data: only by seeing where every production instance went can you judge which gameplay, which event, and which channel the leak is in.
- Common governance tools are new sink gameplay plus targeted sinks; rolling back directly or cutting assets usually triggers public reaction, at a cost far higher than everyday governance.

The first round of inflation in many projects comes from a wrong reward config rather than a design flaw, which means the first step of governance is often tightening reward entry points and validation rather than tuning the curve.

The problem of metric definitions in economy data is especially worth emphasizing on this page: in projects where the three definitions of production, sink, and accumulation do not line up, operations and engineering often talk past each other, and by the time consensus is reached inflation is already formed.

### Long-run data migration

For this kind of project, table structures and storage shapes after three years are often completely different from year one, so migration is routine engineering rather than a one-off task.

- Schema evolution is usually handled by versioned fields and compatibility reads: old data has defaults filled in on read and is upgraded to the new version on write.
- Hot/cold tiering is a common shape: active accounts keep hot data, dormant accounts sink to low-cost storage and are woken up at login.
- Rollback capability is jointly determined by snapshots and ledgers: snapshots decide which moment you can return to, ledgers decide what can still be recomputed after returning there.
- Migration scripts have to be able to relay across multiple versions; a project that can only migrate one step from the latest version is usually at a loss facing years-old servers.

Migration and rollback capability rarely appear on the requirements list, yet they often decide the ceiling of loss containment in an incident; the common practice is to schedule recovery drills into the regular cadence rather than wait for an incident to test them.

### Common solution shapes

The typical shape is a service group centered on the account domain: character, inventory, mail, quest, and shop are split by domain, converged by a unified asset-change interface, with a config center, event orchestration, and settlement tasks driving the outside.

- The asset service maintains balances and ledgers; gameplay services do not modify assets directly, they only submit change requests carrying an idempotent key.
- The event system is usually config-driven, so bringing an event up or down, its reward content, and its participation conditions all need no release.
- Scheduled tasks and the offline settler are deployed independently, advancing in batches by player or by partition, with retry on failure.
- The data side commonly runs dual tracks: snapshots serve queries and rollback, ledgers serve reconciliation and audit.

The elasticity of this shape comes mainly from rolling servers and asynchrony; inside a single server there is usually no pursuit of high concurrency — the pursuit is data correctness and recoverability.

### Pitfalls specific to this type

Reward entry points out of control: one reward function per event, each writing assets on its own, so idempotency and audit both fail, and a double-issuance incident cannot be traced back to which outlet leaked.

Offline settlement by feel: earnings with no cap, a returning player settles dozens of days at once, the numeric curve is torn through, and every subsequent event pays for that one.

Server merge treated as a stitching job: only data is merged, with ID and naming conflicts unhandled, so after merging there are two "yous," and social relations and trading records are mismapped.

Economy data watched only in totals: with no ledger-level detail, when inflation comes nobody knows which gameplay the leak is in, and governance can only be a global one-size-fits-all cut that harms normal players.

Rollback never rehearsed: snapshots are always being taken, the recovery chain has never been walked, and only when something really breaks is it discovered that snapshots are incomplete or that recovery takes far longer than expected.

### Common pitfalls

- Treating this kind of project as "no real-time requirements, so the backend is simple," when in fact the real-time requirement drops but long-term state and operations complexity rise to fill the gap.
- Measuring architectural quality by gameplay feature development speed, ignoring that the asset model, reconciliation, and rollback capability are the real foundation of this kind of project.
- Treating the crosswise quick-reference conclusion as a complete derivation, seeing the three words "progression type" and applying the solution, without first confirming that the dominant contradiction really is data lifetime and the operations chains.

The architectural direction of this kind of project is usually summarized as: accounts resident and sessions short, asynchronous crediting with idempotent convergence, rolling servers at launch with the merge path kept open, economy governance by ledger, and rollback capability by rehearsal.
