# Economy/Platform Architecture

## Category

design / problem-domains subtree. Node position: crosswise quick reference for the economy/platform problem domain — hot-spot governance, trade idempotency, derived-data convergence, and the risk-control surface.

## Definition


This page generalizes economy/platform projects from the problem-domain angle. It is the crosswise quick-reference edition of the problem-domain appendix. It does not expand the full design of the trading system and the risk-control framework; instead it puts the key judgments that distinguish this kind of project from other types onto one page: how hot spots are treated, how trades are made idempotent, how derived data converges, and where the risk-control surface sits.

One-sentence portrait: a few objects carry extremely high read and write frequency, high-frequency trading between players makes the system look more like an economy platform than a gameplay server, and the cost of a single error is real asset loss. Auction houses, exchanges, card trading markets, hot-spot entities in a shared world, and UGC asset markets all fall into this portrait.

## Problem

The judgments for platform-type projects concentrate on four things of trustworthy cooperation: how hot-spot objects are treated, how trades are made idempotent and reconciled, how derived data converges, and where the risk-control surface sits. These four decide "whether money and accounts can be trusted," and are entirely decoupled from gameplay implementation.


### Characteristics of this problem domain

- Object access heat is extremely uneven; a single popular item or a single shared entity takes on disproportionate read and write pressure.
- Players trade directly with each other, so the system moves from issuer to matching party and guarantor, with pricing power in players' hands.
- Single transactions are high-value and irreversible, so an error becomes an asset incident rather than an experience flaw.
- The rhythm is driven by players, peaks cannot be scheduled, and operations events only amplify unpredictability further.
- There is a lot of derived data: rankings, deal prices, indices, valuations, and notifications all derive from the primary data.

The result of these characteristics stacking up is that capacity problems concentrate on individual objects and correctness problems concentrate on individual trades, and neither can be solved by "adding machines overall."

### Boundaries with adjacent types

- Compared with the long-cycle progression type, its assets flow bidirectionally between players rather than accumulating one-way, and the economy is not a designed production-and-sink curve but formed by players pricing freely.
- Compared with the persistent online world type, its bottleneck lies not in the spatial dimension but the object dimension, so what needs scaling is not the map but the way hot-spot objects are hosted.
- Compared with the room-based session type, both its write frequency and its per-transaction value are much higher, and settlement is not once per session but happening all the time.

To judge which type a project belongs to, a common approach is to look at the direction money flows: assets mainly moving between players and the system is progression-type led; assets being transferred heavily among players is usually economy/platform-type led.

### Architectural constraints derived from the characteristics

Hot-spot concentration constrains object hosting: hot objects usually cannot be scaled by adding replicas, so they usually need their own queue, coalescing, and caching strategy.

Trading directly constrains the write path: asset changes usually funnel into a unified interface, where every trade carries an idempotent marker, is retryable, and is reconcilable, with the ledgers on both sides of a trade serving as each other's evidence.

Widespread derivation constrains data lineage: derived data such as rankings, market prices, and valuations must be rebuildable from the primary data rather than each being edited directly with no way to trace back.

Front-loaded risk control constrains event tracking: key nodes in the trading chain usually carry behavioral records from launch, because black-market entry often happens before risk-control strategy is in place.

### Hot-spot objects and single-point bottlenecks

Hot spots are the most typical capacity shape of this kind of project: one server-wide flash sale, one scarce piece of equipment, one busy stall can push a single process to its limit.

- A common practice is to isolate and host hot-spot objects, in a separate process or a separate queue, so their congestion does not drag down adjacent systems.
- Request coalescing and peak-shaving queues are almost standard equipment, turning an instantaneous click burst into a controllable processing rate, with results backfilled through notifications.
- Queue strategy is a product decision rather than a technical detail; first-come-first-served versus lottery directly affects the sense of fairness and the direction of public reaction.
- Hot spots migrate with gameplay and time, so static sharding often fails after a hot spot drifts; a common practice is to keep the ability to identify and migrate hot spots.
- Caching can hold back read pressure but not write contention; deduction of a hot item still has to be serialized or bucketed on the authoritative side.

The goal of hot-spot governance is usually not to eliminate waiting but to make waiting controllable, explainable, and fairly allocated — players can accept queuing, but have a hard time accepting mysterious failure.

### Trade idempotency and reconciliation

A common shape of the trade chain is stepwise advancement: lock, deduct, credit, confirm — every step can fail, and every step can be retried.

- The idempotent key is usually the first line of defense: the same request arriving repeatedly takes effect only once, so network retries do not turn into double deduction.
- Intermediate states must be visible and able to time out; orders locked past the deadline and never confirmed are usually rolled back by a timeout task rather than hung forever.
- Reconciliation is a routine mechanism, not an incident tool: ledgers and balances are checked periodically, the ledgers on both sides of a trade cross-verify, and differences go into a to-do list rather than being found by people scrolling logs.
- Disputes and compensation use a dedicated channel; cancellation, rollback, and support tickets reuse the same asset-change interface so there is no manual data editing that bypasses audit.

In projects that fail to reconcile, the problem is often not in reconciliation itself but in a trade chain with several entry points each writing on its own, where the ledgers are not even complete.

### Derived data consistency

Rankings, market-price curves, asset valuations, and notifications mostly derive from primary data, and their shared requirement is that they eventually converge.

- Derived data is usually allowed to be briefly inconsistent, but must have an explicit convergence deadline; a derived service past its deadline would rather degrade its display than show a wrong value.
- A common practice is ledger-driven rebuild: derived services can replay the current state from the ledger at any time, and the rebuild capability is itself evidence of data lineage.
- A derived service editing the primary store directly is a common anti-pattern; once it happens, rankings no longer match real assets, and the difference cannot be explained.
- Definitions must be unified: the same "deal price" being different on the market page, the valuation page, and the reports usually turns into a trust problem between players and the platform.

The cost of derived data is often underestimated: it is not a few leaderboards but a set of secondary systems requiring continuous maintenance of lineage and definitions.

### Risk-control surface

Wherever real value flows, studios, order brushing, account theft with laundering, and price manipulation tend to gather, so the risk-control surface is a permanent part of this kind of project rather than a post-launch patch.

- Behavioral records grow inside the trade chain: frequency, amounts, counterparty relationships, device and account associations — all are the basis for later judgment and appeals.
- Governance tools are often built into the economic rules themselves: fees, trading taxes, binding thresholds, trading cooldowns, which change behavior more than post-hoc bans do.
- The cost of false positives is real too; support complaints and public reaction from freezing normal players' assets are common, so a clear appeal and unfreeze path is a common practice.
- Risk-control strategy has to iterate with black markets; a project that runs one rule set for a year usually already has its defenses mapped out.

The risk-control surface and economy design are hard to separate: trading friction designed too low puts risk-control under pressure, designed too high chills the ecosystem. A common practice is to treat it as a product parameter and tune it over the long run.

### Common solution shapes

The typical shape is separation of matching and assets: order placement, matching, and deal confirmation form the trading cluster; asset balances and ledgers converge in the asset service; and derived services such as market prices and rankings consume the ledger asynchronously.

- The order book is stored independently, with active orders in low-latency storage and historical deals sunk to archive.
- Matching is usually sharded in parallel by item or by market, keeping order within a single market without pursuing global consistency across markets.
- Notifications, market prices, and valuations derive from the ledger through asynchronous pipelines and can be restarted and rebuilt independently.
- Support and governance back ends reuse the same asset interface, with every manual intervention leaving a trace.

The elasticity of this shape comes mainly from per-market parallelism and asynchronous derivation; the authoritative asset side usually stays simple and serial, trading simplicity for correctness.

### Pitfalls specific to this type

Treating a hot spot as an ordinary object: one server-wide flash sale directly takes down the process hosting the item, dragging unrelated gameplay in the same process down with it.

No idempotent key on trades: the client times out and retries once, deducting twice, with complaints and compensation following in quick succession.

Derived data edited directly: when the leaderboard goes wrong, numbers are fixed by hand, and after fixing they no longer match the ledger, so by the next fix nobody can say which version is real.

Risk control bolted on later: risk control is only built half a year after launch, by which point black markets have already mastered pricing and farming routes, and the governance cost is far higher than tracking at launch.

Friction tuned by feel: fees and trading restrictions changed on a whim, players pre-stock and dump in advance, and one adjustment turns into a run on the economy.

### Common pitfalls

- Assuming that adding machines solves hot spots, when the hot-spot bottleneck is often write contention on a single object; before horizontal scaling you have to answer how state is partitioned.
- Building matching as global strong-consistent ordering, paying a whole-system consistency cost for the order of a single market, with throughput and latency both out of control.
- Verifying only normal trades, not timeouts, retries, concurrent conflicts, and rollback paths, so incidents concentrate on the day with the largest trade volume.

The architectural direction of this kind of project is usually summarized as: hot spots governed separately, trades idempotent and reconcilable, derived data rebuildable, and risk control growing inside the chain.
