# Economy and Platform Game Problem Model

## Category

industry / models subtree. Node positioning: economy and platform problem model—trustworthy coordination with the external world becomes the primary path, and the failure shape is entirely different.

## Definition


For some projects, the core difficulty is not in combat itself, but in `trading, channels, payment, advertising, social virality, and external platform ecosystems`. For this kind of product, the first pressure on the technical system comes from "trustworthy coordination with the external world." Once this path becomes the growth core, platform and economy capabilities are no longer peripheral integration, but part of the primary path.

This model is most easily disrespected, because it looks like "connecting a few SDKs" or "connecting a payment." In reality, as long as capabilities like payment, orders, trading markets, ad monetization, UGC distribution, platform moderation, and channel differences enter core business, the system boundary has already been reshaped.

## Problem

Once trading, channels, payment, advertising, and social virality become the growth core, platform capability is no longer peripheral integration but the primary path; the failure shape shifts from "the experience broke" to "money and accounts are not trustworthy." Channel reconciliation, payment state machines, and risk-control compliance rise from integration items to the design core.


### Why do the failure shapes of this kind of project look entirely different

Different from hard-real-time projects, the most expensive failures of economy and platform projects are usually not in-session experience, but rather:

- Lost orders
- Duplicate fulfillment
- Callback order errors
- Wrong reward issuance
- Reconciliation mismatches
- Failed risk control
- Black-market arbitrage
- Stepping on moderation or platform rules

Once this kind of failure appears, what is affected is not just experience, but may directly trigger financial, compliance, channel relationship, and operations incidents.

### What is the real primary path of this model

For economy and platform projects, the common primary path is usually like this:

1. Users enter the product through a channel, platform, or external entry point.
2. Account, login, authentication, and identity mapping establish context.
3. Payment, advertising, trading, sharing, and social virality drive growth or revenue.
4. External platform callbacks enter the system, triggering order state transitions and fulfillment.
5. Audit, reconciliation, compensation, risk control, and compliance provide a backstop for the entire path.

In other words, the system is not just responsible to players, but also to platforms, finance, risk control, and audit.

### Where does technical pressure land first

### 1. Accounts and identity

Platform-type projects rarely have only one "local account." Common realities include:

- Channel accounts
- Guest accounts
- Internal accounts
- Third-party login identity
- Device identity and risk-control markers

If identity mapping, binding, migration, recovery, and ban paths are not designed clearly from the start, there will almost certainly be hard-to-manage historical baggage later.

### 2. Order state machine

All payment, fulfillment, and compensation problems eventually come back to whether the order state machine is clear.  
A mature order system must at minimum answer:

- When is an order created
- Who is authoritative for payment success
- How are callback failures retried
- How is fulfillment failure compensated
- How are duplicate callbacks handled idempotently
- How is the final order state reconciled

If it is just "receive the callback and then fulfill," that is basically waiting for a live incident.

### 3. Audit and reconciliation

As long as real money, ad revenue, trading markets, or channel revenue sharing enter the system, reconciliation and audit must become formal capabilities, rather than exporting logs and checking after the fact.  
The technical system must be able to answer:

- Why did a certain order succeed
- On what basis did a certain fulfillment happen
- Why did a certain player receive an extra reward
- Why do a certain channel's report and the internal report mismatch

If these capabilities are not done well, the system will later be dragged down by a large number of manual processes.

### 4. SDK adaptation and isolation

Many teams treat SDK integration as engineering leftover scraps, when in reality this step decides whether the system will later be tied up by platform differences.  
A more reliable approach is usually:

- Use a unified adaptation layer to isolate channel and platform differences
- Do not let business logic directly depend on SDK details
- Make clear which capabilities are platform capabilities and which are internal unified capabilities

Otherwise, when a new channel comes in, the entire client and server boundary may be dragged along.

### 5. Risk control and compliance

When the economy path enters the mainline, risk control and compliance naturally come forward. Including but not limited to:

- Black-market registration and click fraud
- Refunds and chargebacks
- Virtual trading arbitrage
- Gift pack and redemption code abuse
- Ad fraud
- Platform moderation and regional compliance differences

If these problems are only patched after the product is running, the cost is usually very high.

### When does this model dominate gameplay problems

If the product has the following features, economy and platform problems often become one of the dominant models:

- Heavily dependent on channels, platforms, or ad monetization
- Payment, trading, social virality, and creator ecosystems are themselves the growth core
- UGC content distribution and moderation are part of the primary path
- Co-operations, SDKs, and platform rules directly constrain versions and features

In this case, even if the gameplay itself is not complex, the technical boundary will not be simple.

### Common system organization approaches

The more reliable approach for this kind of project is usually:

- Accounts and identity layer governed independently
- Payment and orders managed by a unified state machine
- Fulfillment and rewards go through an idempotent path
- External platform differences isolated by an adaptation layer
- Reconciliation, audit, risk control, and compensation have a clear closed loop

The key is not how many platform SDKs are used, but whether internally there is a stable set of abstractions to catch them all.

### Typical failure modes

### Treating platform problems as pre-launch cleanup

This is the most typical error. The result is usually:

- Account model overturned repeatedly
- Channel logic scattered across business code
- Callback failures can only be manually re-issued
- Reconciliation relies on operations manually exporting tables

### Only integration, no state machine

The payment interface is connected but there is no complete order state transition; ads are connected but there is no revenue audit; trading is connected but there is no risk control and compensation. Such a system runs short-term but is extremely fragile long-term.

### SDK directly intruding on business

Large amounts of client and server business logic directly depend on a certain channel or platform interface, so whenever the platform changes, a new channel is added, or rules change, the entire system has to shake along.

### What questions to ask first when judging this model

- Without channels, payment, advertising, or platform social, can this product still stand normally.
- Are the most expensive failures lost orders, wrong issuance, arbitrage, and risk-control failure.
- Do external platform rules directly affect version cadence and feature form.
- Must reconciliation, audit, and compensation be formally systematized, rather than handled manually.

If most answers to these are "yes," then platform and economy are no longer peripheral constraints, but the architecture main problem.

### Conclusions closer to best practice

The most reliable design for economy and platform projects is not "finish connecting the SDKs," but first making the external coordination path into an internally controllable system:

- Account identity is clear
- Order state machine is clear
- Fulfillment path is idempotent and traceable
- SDK adaptation isolation is clean
- Reconciliation, compensation, risk control, and compliance are formal capabilities

The truly hard part of this kind of project is not the integration action itself, but "the external world is unreliable, and whether the system can still pull the results back."

### Common pitfalls

The most common error is understanding platform problems as pre-launch SDK-connecting cleanup work. In reality, platform capabilities will in reverse shape the account model, version cadence, and monetization implementation. If these capabilities are not designed in advance, later you will constantly patch system boundaries with temporary fixes. The second pitfall is having only interface integration, without order state machine, audit, and compensation closed loops. The third pitfall is scattering all channel differences directly into business logic, so any platform adjustment triggers system-level rework.
