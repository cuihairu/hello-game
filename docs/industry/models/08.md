# How Models Combine

## Category

industry / models subtree. Node positioning: the core of composition is priority, not addition—identify the dominant model, common combination scenarios, staged construction, and a directly reusable judgment template.

## Definition


Real projects almost never are a single model. One product may be hard-real-time combat in-session, long-cycle progression out-of-session, and also stacked with payment and platform constraints; another product may have a persistent online world underneath, but some gameplay is instanced rooms. Understanding this is important, because the most common architecture failure is not insufficient complexity, but complexity without priority.

The problem this page solves is not "does a project belong to multiple models at the same time," but rather: when multiple models are hit at once, who decides the primary path, and who only constitutes a secondary constraint.

## Problem

Real projects are almost always multi-model compositions (hard-real-time in-session + long growth out-of-session + payment/platform), and the most common failure is not insufficient complexity but lack of priority: every subsystem is built around its own dominant contradiction, with mismatched boundaries and stages dragging each other down.


### The core of composition is not addition, but priority

The two most dangerous approaches when judging a composite model are:

- Only labeling the project with one tag, ignoring that other models will sooner or later bite back at the primary path.
- Seeing that the project is complex and building all models to the highest standard at once.

The former misses key constraints; the latter turns the system into an over-heavy all-purpose architecture. A more realistic approach is first to distinguish three things:

- Which model decides the core experience players perceive first.
- Which model decides the most expensive, hardest-to-rollback failure.
- Which model only continues to apply pressure long-term but should not dominate phase-one cost for now.

### How to identify the dominant model

When judging the dominant model, the most effective thing is to look at the following three dimensions.

### 1. What makes players churn first

If players churn first because of desync, misadjudication, or input latency, the dominant model is closer to hard-real-time; if they churn first because games cannot start, they cannot return after disconnect, or settlement is abnormal, the dominant model is closer to room-based; if they churn first because of event cadence, nurturing accumulation, or resource allocation imbalance, the dominant model is closer to long-cycle progression.

The problem players perceive first is usually the problem that should least be hijacked by peripheral systems.

### 2. Where does the most expensive failure happen

The thing technically most worth preventing first is the failure with the highest cost after occurrence and the hardest recovery.

- A single adjudication dispute being expensive, the dominant model is closer to hard-real-time.
- A single scene migration or world state corruption being expensive, the dominant model is closer to persistent online world.
- A single asset misissuance, event incident, or rollback being expensive, the dominant model is closer to long-cycle progression.
- A single lost order, duplicate fulfillment, or risk-control failure being expensive, the dominant model is closer to platform and economy.

### 3. Where does the team spend most of its R&D time

This is the most honest metric. If most time is spent on synchronization, combat, skills, and replay, the dominant model is often hard-real-time; if mainly on events, configuration, asset chains, and compensation repair, the dominant model is often long-cycle progression; if mainly on zoning, migration, cross-server, and operations orchestration, the dominant model is often persistent online world.

### Common combination scenarios

### Room-based combat + long-horizon progression

This combination is very common. The usual approach is:

- Organize in-session around room-based or hard-real-time, first stabilizing start, reconnection, custody, and settlement.
- Let out-of-session progression systems separately carry long-term assets and events.
- Do not let progression systems directly tie up the room lifecycle.

If you first make the progression mid-platform extremely heavy but fail to stabilize the room primary path, players usually will not give the system a second chance.

### Hard-real-time combat + high-frequency object and heavy presentation

MOBA, action combat, some shooters, and ARPG easily hit this combination. Here you cannot only talk about synchronization, nor only talk about client optimization, because:

- Synchronization, adjudication, and replay decide fairness.
- Object budget, asset budget, and presentation budget decide whether the system can continue to live as content grows.

This kind of project usually needs to acknowledge that two mainlines hold at the same time, but still must distinguish dominant experience from secondary constraint.

### Persistent online world + long-cycle progression

This is the common combination for MMO, SLG, and large open-world online projects. They are often heavy on both, but still must be ordered:

- If migration, zoning, cross-server, server merge, and operations orchestration are more dangerous, the world model takes priority.
- If events, seasons, assets, and monetization paths are more prone to incidents, the progression model takes priority.

The key is not to choose one and discard the other, but to decide who the phase-one primary path first stabilizes around.

### Platform type + any gameplay type

As long as the project heavily depends on channels, payment, advertising, social virality, or a UGC ecosystem, the platform model stacks in as a primary or secondary model. The biggest error is treating it as a cleanup task of "just connect the SDK at the end."

### How to draw boundaries under a composite model

Once models are composed, boundaries must be harder. A more stable approach is usually:

- The dominant model decides the primary path and core service organization.
- Secondary models enter the system through data contracts, state boundaries, and extension interfaces.
- Different models interact through explicit interaction as much as possible, rather than intruding on each other.

This means:

- Room or combat processes should not directly bear long-term asset modification.
- Long-term asset systems should not directly depend on in-session temporary in-memory state.
- Payment and platform paths should not intrude into the real-time combat advance path.
- Pure presentation objects should not by default enter the most expensive authoritative synchronization path.

The more complex the composition, the more you cannot rely on "mix it for now, split it later."

### Staged construction is more realistic than one-step completion

Composite projects especially need to control phase-one complexity. A more realistic order is usually:

1. First stabilize the most expensive primary path around the dominant model.
2. Then fill in the parts of secondary models most likely to bite back at the primary path.
3. Only at the end build platformized and generalized capabilities for long-dated scenarios.

The purpose of doing this is not to cut corners, but to avoid fully loading system complexity before the product direction is stable.

### A directly reusable judgment template

When encountering a composite project, you can require the team to state it clearly in one complete sentence:

`The dominant model is X, because the core experience and the most expensive failures mainly land on A, B, C; the secondary models are Y and Z, because they will continuously constrain D, E, F; so phase one first does G, H, I, and does not pay the highest cost in advance for J, K, L.`

If this sentence cannot be said, it usually means the model composition relationship is not yet clarified.

### Common pitfalls

- Simply adding multiple models together into an all-purpose architecture where nothing is strong enough.
- Misjudging the dominant model and dumping large amounts of cost onto systems that are not the primary path.
- Only acknowledging the dominant model and completely ignoring that secondary models will sooner or later bite back at the primary path.
- Unclear stage division, paying the highest complexity for all long-dated capabilities from the start.

The meaning of model composition is not to prove the project is "very complex," but to help the team distinguish order and boundary responsibilities within complexity.
