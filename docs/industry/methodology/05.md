# Boundaries among Client, Server, Platform, and Operations

## Category

industry / methodology subtree. Node positioning: the four boundaries of client / server / platform / operations — blurry boundaries turn into finger-pointing, duplicated implementation, and nobody owning the result; drawing the boundaries is a design action.

## Definition


The problems that cost game teams the most effort are often not ones they cannot solve technically, but ones whose boundaries have been blurry for a long time. Whether a feature belongs to the client or the server, whether a restriction is absorbed by the platform or by the business side, whether an incident counts as a development incident or an operations incident — if these are not clear at design time, they continuously turn into finger-pointing, duplicated implementation, and unowned results after launch.

## Problem

What costs the team the most effort is often not a technical gap but long-term blurry boundaries: who owns the feature, who absorbs the restriction, whose incident it is — not settled clearly at design time, all of it becomes organizational friction after launch. Boundaries do not grow naturally out of an organization; they must be designed explicitly.


### The client's boundary

The client's job is to hand the experience to the player, so it naturally carries these responsibilities:

- input capture, rendering, animation, audio, UI, and interaction feedback
- prediction, interpolation, tweening, and presentation correction over local state
- managing asset loading, device adaptation, crash capture, and on-device performance control

But the client is not suited to holding an unauditable final authority over assets, and it is not suited to monopolizing the critical logic that directly affects fairness and revenue settlement. The client may respond first; it cannot be "the final word" without limit.

### The server's boundary

The server's core responsibility is not "doing everything" but carrying authoritative state and shared rules:

- managing accounts, characters, assets, combat results, matchmaking relationships, and core world state
- validating, adjudicating, compensating, and auditing critical operations
- maintaining consistency and recoverability in a multi-user shared environment

Once the server takes on an authoritative role, it must take on the recovery role at the same time. A server designed only to adjudicate, never to recover, is extremely fragile under disconnect, restart, retry, and version switches.

### The platform layer's boundary

The platform layer includes channels, operating system capabilities, third-party SDKs, review mechanisms, real-name and addiction prevention, payment gateways, ads, and social capabilities. The character of the platform layer is this: it is not something you can fully control, but it continuously constrains your implementation.

So the platform layer is more like an external source of constraints than an ordinary dependency library. The team needs to make explicit:

- which capabilities must depend on the platform directly
- which platform differences should be absorbed by an adaptation layer
- which failure modes need a business-side fallback prepared

Scattering platform differences through business code means the maintenance cost rises exponentially with every additional channel or region.

### The operations side's boundary

Operations is not a non-technical department that "posts announcements and configures campaigns." As long as the project runs long term, operations requirements directly shape the system:

- campaign switches, reward distribution, gray release experiments, and user reach all need a controllable backend
- risk control actions, customer support compensation, bans and unbans, announcement mail all require auditability
- data analysis and conversion experiments feed back into event tracking, configuration, and version cadence

If development treats the operations system as a peripheral tool, the main business often ends up relying on manual data backfilling, temporary database edits, and emergency releases to keep up with operations actions — extremely high risk.

### Principles for dividing boundaries

A fairly stable division of boundaries usually follows three principles:

- whoever holds the final say must also bear validation and audit responsibility
- whoever is closest to the experience takes priority on immediate feedback and local presentation
- whoever cannot be fully controlled by the team must have its impact isolated through an adaptation layer and failure fallbacks

These three principles help a team avoid the old rut of "let's blur them together for now to save effort, and patch in boundaries when something breaks."

### Common Mistakes

- both client and server keeping a copy of a critical rule, with no clarity on which is the final source
- operations wants flexibility, and development just hands out a database write entry with no audit path
- each business module handling platform integration on its own, so callbacks, login state, and payment state cannot be governed uniformly
- treating "either side is fine for now" as a small matter, and watching it grow into a large-scale refactor

Drawing boundaries is not an organizational management topic; it is system design itself. The clearer the boundaries, the more easily technical decisions stabilize, and the more easily online incidents are located and contained.
