# Game Types, Platforms, and Business Models

## Category

industry / methodology subtree. Node positioning: gameplay type decides the dominant contradiction, platform decides the technical boundary, and the business model shapes the system in reverse — the three inputs together constitute the engineering reality of the project.

## Definition


A game project does not start from a general-purpose technical solution and then look for gameplay to fill in. The real order is usually the reverse: gameplay type decides the shape of state, platform decides the runtime constraints, and the business model decides the version and operations cadence. Only when the three are stacked does the basic engineering reality of the project form.

## Problem

Starting from a general technical solution and then filling in the gameplay has the order backwards: type decides the shape of state, platform decides the runtime constraints, and the business model decides the version and operations cadence. If any one input is undetermined, the architecture discussion has no foundation; looking at any one alone (treating the platform as a release problem, or the business side as peripheral) produces a systematic misjudgment.


### Game type decides the dominant contradiction

Different game types have very different core difficulties:

- a room-based session product cares most about matchmaking efficiency, room lifecycle, settlement correctness, and short-path recovery
- a hard-real-time competitive product cares most about latency, sync model, determinism, anti-cheat, and adjudication authority
- a persistent online world cares most about partitioning, map load, state migration, cross-server collaboration, and long-term operations
- a long-cycle progression product cares most about the economy system, config-driven design, content consumption pace, and data consistency

If the type is judged inaccurately, the system optimizes in the wrong direction. Building a lightweight room game as a heavy world server makes development cost and deployment complexity far exceed the return; treating hard-real-time combat as an ordinary request/response system soon hits a wall on experience.

### Platform is not a release problem, it is a technical boundary

Many teams postpone platform constraints until close to launch, only to find that a lot of earlier implementation simply does not hold. Platform directly affects these designs:

- whether login and the account system can be self-built, or must hang under a channel identity
- whether client package size, asset download, hot update, and the review window are restricted
- whether networking capability is stable, and whether long connections, background survival, or specific script runtimes are allowed
- whether payment, ads, social sharing, real-name verification, and addiction prevention need platform capabilities

Mobile, console, PC, mini-game platforms, and overseas multi-channel release differ by more than a swapped SDK: they change client organization, version strategy, gray release approach, and operations actions altogether.

### Business model shapes the system in reverse

The monetization approach decides where long-term system pressure lands:

- a buyout product cares most about content delivery quality, save-data security, and cross-platform experience consistency
- an F2P numerical progression product cares most about campaign operations, economy balance, payment conversion, and risk control
- a strongly social or platform-style product cares most about retention, relationship chains, UGC review, and ecosystem governance

The same "in-game shop" means completely different things under different business models. One-time DLC sales care about version dependencies and content delivery; a continuous payment system cares about price experiments, inventory control, payment callbacks, backfilled orders, refunds, audit, and fraudulent behavior.

### The three have to be read together

What is really hard is not understanding gameplay, platform, and monetization separately, but understanding their combination. For example:

- hard-real-time competitive play plus a mobile weak-network environment pulls sync, prediction, disconnect recovery, and device performance into the core
- long-cycle progression plus a mini-game platform binds package size, hot update, login dependency, version review, and campaign pace together
- a persistent online world plus global release brings multi-region deployment, timezone operations, compliance differences, and network path problems at the same time

This is why technology selection cannot be detached from project context. Any "every game does it this way" experience must first be restored to its type, platform, and business background, or its reuse value is very low.

### Common Mistakes

- making technical judgments by gameplay type only, without looking at the platform's review, hot update, and networking limits
- implementing by platform integration only, without looking at the long-term data, operations, and risk control requirements of the business model
- using "we are doing a demo first" as a reason to postpone platform and business constraints that are bound to become main problems later

The most important thing to do early in a project is not to solve all problems at once, but to identify early which constraints will eventually dominate the architecture. Type, platform, and business model are the starting point of that set of constraints.
