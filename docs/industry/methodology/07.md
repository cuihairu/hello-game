# How to Systematically Accumulate Game Development Experience

## Category

industry / methodology subtree. Node positioning: organize experience by problem domain, write the constraints in retrospectives rather than the plot, and get experience into daily flow — the complete path from "we hit this pit" to "it is reusable."

## Definition


What is most scarce in game development is not "people who have hit pits," but people who can explain the pits clearly and reuse them. Many teams have clearly done several projects, yet when they meet a new problem they still act as if it were the first time. The reason is usually not a lack of experience, but that the experience has stayed inside personal memory and verbal impressions.

## Problem

Experience that stays in personal memory leaves new projects hitting pits as if for the first time: it cannot be explained clearly (not reusable), it never enters the flow (not executable), and it is organized by technical nouns (not searchable). The output of experience management is organizational capability, not a personal growth diary.


### Why experience goes stale

Experience goes stale for three common reasons:

- only the result is remembered, not the preconditions. Remembering "frame sync is good" without remembering that it depends on strict determinism and a fairly stable network.
- only the solution is remembered, not the cost. Remembering "splitting services scales" without remembering that the same move also paid a price in path troubleshooting and consistency.
- only the incident is remembered, not the trigger conditions. Knowing an incident happened, but not being able to say why it broke out at that phase and at that scale.

Experience without context easily degenerates into slogans.

### What should be sedimented

Rather than writing a "technology introduction," what has more value is decomposing one judgment into a fixed structure:

- what was the core problem faced at the time
- what were the project's gameplay type, platform, phase, and team conditions
- what the options were, and why the other options were ruled out
- what gains the final solution brought, and what new costs it introduced
- which post-launch phenomena proved the judgment right, and which show the original judgment needs revising

The value of writing it this way is that later people can judge whether they are in similar conditions, instead of mechanically copying the conclusion.

### Experience should be organized by problem domain

If experience documents are scattered by person, by time, and by project, reuse efficiency is very low. A better organizing approach is usually to sediment by problem domain:

- keep networking and sync problems together
- keep room and combat state problems together
- keep configuration, data, version, and operations problems together
- keep security, risk control, audit, and compliance problems together

The benefit is that a new project can search directly by problem, instead of guessing "did some old project happen to hit this at some point."

### Retrospectives should not only narrate the incident

A retrospective that is actually useful must at least answer four things:

- why it was not caught beforehand
- why the existing mechanisms did not stop it
- if it happened again, which line of defense should have moved earlier
- whether this problem is a one-off or a symptom of a systemic class of problems

If a retrospective stays at the timeline and the ownership of blame, it helps future projects only a little. Retrospectives should sediment judgment frameworks and fail-safes, not just record one incident.

### Get experience into daily flow

An experience library has value only once it enters daily flow. Effective practices include:

- requiring solution reviews to cite existing cases and boundary conditions
- requiring incident retrospectives to be added into the corresponding problem domain, rather than left as a standalone loose document
- training newcomers not only on technical nouns, but on typical problems and trade-off cases
- running a pre-release check against a problem list, instead of relying entirely on personal memory

Only then does experience turn from a "document asset" into "decision input."

### Common Mistakes

- sedimenting large amounts of conceptual summaries without real project conditions or cost descriptions
- documents that only talk about success, and never about failure or the rollback process
- experience libraries that are not maintained for a long time, until old conclusions have drifted from the current tech stack and organization
- treating experience accumulation as one architect's personal job rather than part of a team collaboration mechanism

The goal of systematically accumulating experience is not to turn the team into a document factory, but to make sure every cost paid once reduces the chance of repeating the same mistake in later projects.
