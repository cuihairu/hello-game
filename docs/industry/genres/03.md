# Persistent Online World

## Category

industry / genres subtree. Node positioning: persistent online world topic — a genre quick overview of the coordination difficulties that come from a world that keeps existing.

## Definition


The key to a persistent online world is not just "many people," but that the world must keep existing while players are offline, state must keep accumulating, and multiple systems must work together around the same long-term world state. Typical examples include MMO, open-world online products, large survival sandboxes, and strongly guild-social games.

## Problem

Not just many people: the world keeps existing while players are offline, and multiple systems must coordinate around the same long-term world state — this is the shared difficulty of MMO, open-world online, survival sandbox, and strongly guild-based games.


### Where These Games Are Actually Hard

Once the world persists, the technical problem escalates from "single-match correctness" to "long-term stability." You are no longer handling a single battle, but maintaining a living system that keeps expanding:

- Player state must be saved long term
- Scenes and objects must keep running
- Different players may modify the same world simultaneously from different entry points
- Events, versions, and operations keep piling onto production

So what these games fear most is not a single crash, but chronic rot: state gets dirty, cross-server coupling, scripts and hotfixes getting heavier and heavier, operations becoming increasingly uncontrollable.

### Where Architecture Focus Usually Goes

- How the world is split decides the ceiling: by server, by line, by scene, by shard, by dynamic instance — each limits social range and content expression.
- State ownership must be very clear: for characters, scenes, guilds, trading, mail, events, and leaderboards, who has final say must not be vague.
- Cross-scene and cross-server links need caution: migration, cross-server combat, and cross-server social play all significantly raise the difficulty of consistency and failure handling.
- Operations capability is product capability: scaling, server migration, rollback, banning, event switches, and gray release must all be institutionalized.

### Common Approaches and Tradeoffs

Most persistent online worlds choose a "local authority, partition governance" architecture, rather than maintaining a truly single global world. The reason is simple: a unified world is too expensive and too fragile.

Common tradeoffs concentrate in three places:

- Large-server social feel vs. controllable operations: the more players feel they live in the same world, the harder migration and scaling become.
- Strong interaction depth vs. system stability: free trading, free building, and strong guild warfare all amplify object-relationship and dirty-data risk.
- Hot-update flexibility vs. behavior verifiability: the more production scripts and the faster iteration, the larger the failure surface.

### Common Pitfalls

- Reading "persistent online" as "all state must be shared in real time." Many pieces of state should actually be made asynchronous and tiered, otherwise the system gets out of hand quickly.
- Committing to "large seamless world" too early. If gameplay and business model do not depend on it, the cost often far exceeds the payoff.
- Underestimating how intrusively operations touches the system. Persistent online projects are often not killed by combat but by events, compensation, cross-server, and accumulated baggage.
