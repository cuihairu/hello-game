# Login, Payment, Social, and Ad SDKs

## Category

industry / platforms subtree. Node positioning: the four SDK categories differ in nature — failure costs, error locations, and architecture requirements all vary; the isolation layer is the first principle.

## Definition


Platform SDKs are usually managed as one kind of thing, but login, payment, social, and ads differ greatly in nature: login concerns identity and compliance, payment concerns money and reconciliation, social concerns distribution and relationship chains, ads concern monetization and risk control. They differ in failure cost, in where problems surface, and in what they demand of the architecture. Scheduling them together as "integrate a few SDKs" usually underestimates the rigor the payment path demands and overestimates the technical difficulty of the social path.

## Problem

Scheduling by "how many SDKs to integrate" underestimates the rigor the payment path demands and overestimates the technical difficulty of the social path: login concerns identity compliance, payment concerns money and reconciliation, social concerns distribution, ads concern monetization and risk control. Without an isolation layer, any vendor's upgrade or deprecation becomes a whole-project event.


### The Four SDK Categories Have Different Failure Costs

Only by first separating what each category costs when it fails can the integration strategy have a basis.

- The cost of login failure is being unable to enter the game; it can usually be retried or downgraded to guest, so the business loss is limited but it happens most often.
- The cost of payment failure is real money — lost orders, duplicate delivery, wrong amounts are each asset loss and complaints — though the frequency is relatively low.
- The cost of social share failure is blunted distribution; players barely notice. Worth optimizing, but not worth sacrificing main-path stability for.
- The cost of ad failure is lost monetization revenue; a failed rewarded-video fetch also carries a strong complaint risk of "I watched the ad and got no reward."

The combination of cost and frequency decides where investment goes: the payment path is built to the most rigorous standard, the login path is degraded to the highest-frequency standard, and social and ads are handled for the experience of "fails but does not hurt the main path." Many projects' integration chaos has its root in applying one loose standard to four completely different things.

### Why the Isolation Layer Is the First Principle

The most common structural problem in SDK integration is scattered calls: interface code directly calling platform login, business logic directly assembling payment parameters, references to a specific SDK's global objects everywhere. It saves effort short-term; long-term, every SDK upgrade, every channel difference, and every platform-specific issue penetrates into core code.

A common practice is to define a set of stable platform-capability interfaces inside the project — one each for login, payment, sharing, and ads — and funnel all concrete SDK calls into adapter implementations. Core logic depends only on the interfaces you define; platform differences, SDK version differences, and channel differences are all compressed into the adapter layer. The extra benefits are real too:

- The adapter layer can provide empty or mock implementations, letting the client develop and test in an SDK-less environment.
- Every call passes through the funnel layer, where success rate, latency, and error codes can be recorded uniformly — SDK health becomes observable.
- Platform A's specific pitfalls are isolated inside A's adapter and never pollute other platforms.

The isolation layer's interfaces are designed around your project's own semantics, not copied from some SDK's interface shape, otherwise you have merely relocated the dependency.

### Login SDK: Identity Anchor and Session Boundary

The core of login integration is not which API to call but thinking through the identity model. The platform identity is the entry point; in-game accounts and asset ownership are your authoritative record, and the two need an explicit binding table, not a default one-to-one assumption. This design is especially critical for multi-platform distribution: the identity tokens the same player gets on different platforms do not interoperate, so the cross-platform anchor must be confirmed as usable at kickoff — after-the-fact remedies like binding by phone number cost an entire operational cycle of account fragmentation.

On session design, the common points are:

- The game's own session and the platform session are two separate lifecycles; expiry, renewal, and kick-out are handled independently.
- Guest identity must support later upgrade binding, with a contingency plan for account-merge conflicts at binding time — letting the player choose usually generates fewer complaints than automatic backend merging.
- The exchange step in the login path that depends on the platform's server is an external call and needs timeout, retry, and a clear failure message.
- The login API is designed idempotently; concurrent requests from repeated taps and reconnection must not create duplicate accounts.

### Payment SDK: Order State Machine and Reconciliation

Payment is the category with the highest rigor requirement of the four. A reliable payment integration usually revolves around an order state machine: created, paying, paid-awaiting-delivery, delivered, closed, with each transition having a clear trigger and proof. The client's payment-success receipt only optimizes the experience; the basis for delivery is always the platform notification the server receives or the result of an active order query.

Practices validated repeatedly:

- Platform notifications are handled idempotently; duplicate and out-of-order notifications produce no duplicate delivery and no state rollback.
- Active order queries as a fallback: notifications can be lost, so unsettled orders need a scheduled job to check status with the platform.
- Periodic reconciliation: compare your order table against the platform's bill, with divergent orders entering a manual or automatic compensation flow.
- Amount and item info are determined by the server at order creation; never trust the price and quantity reported by the client.

Lost orders will happen; the point of the design is to make them discoverable, locatable, and compensable. When a player complains "I paid but it never arrived," customer support must be able to follow the order number to see which step it stopped at and which proof is missing, rather than searching a haystack of logs.

### Social SDK: Borrowed Relationship Chains

Social SDKs provide sharing, invites, friend relationships, and similar capabilities; the easiest engineering mistake is binding the main path to unreliable callbacks. Whether a share succeeded, whether the player actually completed an invite — the trustworthiness of these signals is far lower than it appears: callbacks can be lost, can be forged, and platform definitions can change.

The safe design principle is to treat social capabilities as a distribution channel, not as a settlement basis:

- Share rewards are granted based on server-verifiable events, with frequency control and risk control — never unconditional trust in client callbacks.
- Invite attribution is bound on the server, preventing scripts from farming invite rewards at scale.
- Friend relationships are separated by source: platform friend relationships are borrowed, with rules set by the platform; in-game social relationships must be self-built and are the basis of assets and gameplay.
- Share content and landing pages fall within platform review; changing copy may require re-review, so leave that margin in the schedule.

### Ad SDK: Monetization Path and Reward Trustworthiness

Rewarded video is a major revenue source for mini-games and casual products, and its chain is longer than it looks: fetch, show, close, settle — any step can fail. From the player's perspective there is only one thing: watch the ad, get the reward. So the core tension of ad integration is the balance between monetization experience and reward trustworthiness.

A common practice is to grant rewards based on the ad platform's server callback, with the client callback only advancing the UI flow; the server callback must be signature-verified to prevent forged callbacks farming rewards. Fetch failures need an experience fallback: a "try again later" prompt, an alternative reward, or a reworked gameplay entry, so the player is not stuck on an ad slot that spins forever. The ad SDK's own size and initialization timing also deserve management — crude synchronous initialization slows startup, and startup speed directly affects user-acquisition conversion. Metrics such as ad fill rate, fetch latency, and callback arrival rate should be in monitoring, so that when monetization revenue swings abnormally there is evidence to trace.

### Degradation Paths and Failure Experience

SDKs all fail; the difference is only what the player sees when they do. The goal of degradation design is to localize failures: one SDK's fault should not drag down the whole game.

- Login failure degrades to retry or guest mode, while preserving the upgrade-binding entry.
- On payment failure the order is retained and the UI gives a clear status and a follow-up path, not leaving the player wondering where the money went.
- Ad failure takes an alternative reward or a delayed entry; main gameplay never depends on the ad succeeding.
- Share failure degrades silently and does not interrupt the operation flow.

Another dimension of degradation is launch isolation. SDK initialization failure used to be a frequent cause of whole-package launch failure; making each SDK's initialization asynchronous and independent means a single SDK's exception no longer blocks entering the game. SDK crash and error rates are monitored by version, so that an abnormally bad SDK version is caught and rolled back in time.

### SDK Version Upgrades and Deprecation Cycles

Platform SDKs evolve continuously: interfaces get deprecated, minimum versions get forced, and behavior changes quietly. Long-lived projects will almost certainly go through several forced upgrades, and the upgrade window is often bound to the review cycle, making the schedule reactive.

Three things are worth preparing in advance. First, record SDK versions and adapter versions, so you know what each platform at each version uses and what has been changed. Second, run a full regression on an isolation branch before upgrading — behavior changes introduced by an SDK upgrade often appear nowhere in the release notes. Third, watch the platform's deprecation notices and leave at least one version cycle of margin for replacing old interfaces, instead of waiting for the forced-shutdown date. SDK governance is not hard; the hard part is that it is never urgent — until it suddenly becomes very urgent.

### SDK Compliance and Privacy Disclosure

Integrating third-party SDKs carries another easily ignored obligation: what data an SDK collects and for what purpose must be disclosed in the privacy policy, and some platforms also require submitting a third-party SDK list. Ad and attribution SDKs are usually the most sensitive collectors, and the compliance risk of under-reporting is bigger than the technical risk.

A common practice is to maintain an SDK ledger: each SDK's provider, version, purpose, data categories collected, and initialization timing. New SDKs go through a ledger assessment before adoption; when retiring an SDK, confirm data collection stops at the same time. This shares the same infrastructure as technical governance, so the incremental investment is small.

### Common Pitfalls

- Applying one loose standard to four SDK categories: the payment path not rigorous enough, the social path over-engineered.
- SDK calls scattered through business code, so one SDK upgrade becomes a project-wide regression.
- Treating the client payment receipt as the delivery basis, lacking server notification, idempotency, and reconciliation, so lost orders become asset loss.
- Binding reward settlement to client callbacks for shares or ads, and being farmed at scale by forged callbacks.
- A single SDK's initialization failure dragging down the whole launch, so the blast radius far exceeds the fault point.
- Only caring about SDK versions when something breaks: nobody reads deprecation notices, and the forced upgrade blindsides the schedule.

The maturity of SDK integration is measured not by how many vendors you integrated but by what happens on failure: does login failure have an exit, does payment failure have an order to inspect, does ad failure have an alternative path. Design these failure paths well, and the SDK truly turns from a risk into a capability.
