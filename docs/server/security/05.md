# Licensing risk and dependency governance

## Category

server / security subtree. Node positioning: third-party dependencies and licenses are a long-term governance target -- if you do not take inventory during development, you cannot say what you used at funding, acquisition, going overseas, or audit time.

## Definition


Many projects can accept technical debt in code, but rarely truly treat third-party dependencies and licensing risk as a long-term governance target. The result is often: during development, to go fast, a lot of SDKs, libraries, assets, and tools are integrated first; then at launch, funding, acquisition, going overseas, or compliance audit, the team discovers they can no longer say what was actually used, what can be used commercially, what must be open-sourced back, and what suppliers, once gone dark, will block a release.

## Problem

Technical debt can be accepted, but not being able to say what's in the dependency list cannot: only at launch, funding, acquisition, going overseas, or compliance audit do you find out what can be used commercially, what must be open-sourced back, and what suppliers, once gone dark, will block a release -- dependency governance must be done early, because inventorying it afterwards is extremely costly.


### Why dependency risk is not just a legal problem

Dependency governance matters not only because of license obligations but also because it directly affects technical stability and business continuity:

- Third-party SDKs may crash, lag, leak data, or change interfaces
- Open-source components may stop being maintained, disclose vulnerabilities, or break compatibility on upgrade
- Commercial licenses may restrict platform, region, concurrency scale, or redistribution method
- If a critical toolchain has no one maintaining it, it will directly stall builds, releases, and operations

In other words, dependency risk is at once a legal problem, a supply chain problem, and an engineering governance problem.

### Why game projects are more prone to dependency runaway

Game projects usually integrate a large number of external capabilities, and these capabilities are scattered across client, server, operations backend, and toolchains:

- Login, payment, ads, push, customer support, analytics SDKs
- Engine plugins, audio/video libraries, anti-cheat components, crash reporting components
- Image, font, audio, motion, map, and other asset resources
- Build scripts, hot update tools, packaging plugins, and automation platforms

Once these dependencies have no unified registration and version strategy, several common problems appear:

- The same capability is integrated from different suppliers in different projects, and maintenance cost keeps rising
- Upgrading one SDK drags out multiple incompatibilities, but no one knows the blast radius
- When a license dispute arises, the team cannot prove the source and license scope of assets or libraries

### Where license problems most easily trip you up

- Copying open-source code into a project without preserving the license notice
- Using components with strong copyleft obligations without evaluating the distribution method
- Commercial assets, fonts, and audio whose license scope does not match the actual publishing platform
- Resources obtained from outsourcing, plugin markets, or forums without a clear source record

Many pitfalls are not because the team intentionally violated rules, but because in early development dependencies were not managed as assets.

### A more practical dependency governance approach

For most teams, dependency governance should at least achieve:

- Maintain a unified dependency list, including version, source, purpose, owner, and license type
- Establish an intake process for high-risk dependencies, such as payment, account, ads, data SDKs, and core open-source libraries
- Manage the lifecycle of client, server, toolchain, and asset resources separately
- Know which projects and modules will be affected during version upgrades and vulnerability fixes

If the team is larger, you can go further:

- Dependency tiering and approval
- License scanning and supply chain security scanning
- Contingency plans for alternatives, to avoid single-point failure of a critical supplier

### Why dependency governance should be done early

Because once dependencies enter multiple projects, multiple release paths, and multiple outsourcing processes, trying to unify them afterwards becomes very painful. By contrast, spending a bit more effort early to record source, version, license, and owner clearly is far cheaper.

### Common mistakes

- Only caring whether code runs, not where dependencies come from or whether they can be used long-term
- Third-party SDK integration decided by a single developer, with no unified registration or upgrade strategy
- Only managing code repositories, not asset, font, audio, and external tool licenses
- Only sorting out license problems when audited, complained about, or going overseas

The truly mature mark of licensing risk and dependency governance is not how pretty the list looks, but, when some library discloses a vulnerability, some SDK is taken down, or some asset license is questioned, the team can quickly know the blast radius, alternative path, and handling owner.

### Practical case: triaging a "going overseas audit, three days without being able to say which SDKs were used" dependency inventory breakdown

> This case is a demonstration example (not drawn from a real project), with numbers rounded and simplified. It pushes the page's "Why game projects are more prone to dependency runaway," "Where license problems most easily trip you up," and "A more practical dependency governance approach" into deep water: the audit arrives and only then do you find you cannot say what you used -- this is the triage of dependencies not treated as assets, and also a complete unfolding of "how to keep blast radius and owner always knowable."

**Background and symptoms**: A product's going-overseas compliance audit, and the team took three days without being able to say which SDKs were integrated or which could be used commercially: inventory found **2** strong-copyleft components with no distribution method evaluated, **1** asset license scope not matching the publishing platform, and the audit was delayed **3** weeks.

### Step 1: symptoms and caliber -- first count which categories you cannot say

Inventory caliber: dependencies have no unified registration; client, server, operations backend, and toolchains each integrate on their own, and the same capability is integrated from different suppliers in different projects.

License caliber: **2** components with strong copyleft obligations have no distribution method evaluated, and the license scope of commercial assets, fonts, and audio does not match the actual publishing platform.

Authorization caliber: resources obtained from outsourcing and plugin markets have no clear source record, so when a license dispute arises, source and license scope cannot be proven.

### Step 3: root cause -- dependencies were not managed as assets

Many pitfalls are not because the team intentionally violated rules, but because in early development dependencies were not managed as assets. Once dependencies enter multiple projects, multiple release paths, and multiple outsourcing processes, trying to unify them afterwards becomes very painful -- not inventorying during development means paying triple time at audit time to settle the debt.

### Step 4: handling and backfill -- list, intake, lifecycle

1. Unified dependency list: version, source, purpose, owner, and license type registered item by item, with client, server, toolchains, and asset resources each getting their own ledger.
2. High-risk dependency intake: payment, account, ads, data SDKs, and core open-source libraries go through an intake process, with strong-copyleft components having their distribution method evaluated before introduction.
3. Lifecycle management: define upgrade and retirement strategies separately for the four dependency types, and locate affected projects and modules by the list during version upgrades and vulnerability fixes.
4. Contingency plans for alternatives: a critical supplier single-point failure has a backup, so one SDK being taken down does not stall a release.

Review ledger: audit delay **3** weeks -> a complete list in **2** days; **2** strong-copyleft components evaluated and license notices added; asset license alignment coverage **0**% -> **100**%.

### Three lessons from the case

1. **If you cannot say what's in the dependency list, the audit phase must settle the debt**. Not inventorying during development means three days at going-overseas audit without being able to say what was used.
2. **License obligations must be evaluated at introduction time**. Introducing strong-copyleft components without evaluating the distribution method means the cost of catching up later is the entire compliance bill.
3. **Assets and tools are dependencies too**. Managing only code repositories and not assets, fonts, or audio still gets stuck on publishing when a license dispute arises.
