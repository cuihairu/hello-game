# Engine Selection and Business Models

## Category

client / engines subtree. Node positioning: read the license terms as a cost structure — where revenue share, buyout, subscription, and open source each put the cost, and where the exit is when terms change.

## Definition


Engine selection discussions tend to concentrate on rendering quality, performance, and community reputation, while the business-model thread often gets waved off in a single sentence. In reality, license terms are written directly into the project's cost structure: they decide how much revenue gets taken, whether costs are front-loaded or back-loaded, and whether there is an exit when terms change. An engine is a long-term business; choosing an engine equals choosing a long-term commercial partner. Add this perspective, and many selection arguments become far clearer.

## Problem

Selection arguments concentrate on rendering and performance, and the business model is usually waved off; yet license terms write directly into the cost structure: how much revenue gets taken, whether costs are front- or back-loaded, whether there is a way out when terms change. An engine is a long-term business — lock-in and migration costs were priced in the day you signed.


### Licensing Models Are a Spectrum

Engine licensing is not a binary "paid or free" but a spectrum: revenue share by income, subscription by seat, one-time buyout, open source under permissive licenses, paid source-code partnerships, and fully in-house development. Each model maps to a different cost structure and risk distribution, and none of them wins under all conditions.

- Revenue share is friendly to early-stage projects — if it doesn't sell, you pay almost nothing — at the price that once you succeed, the share taken is the most expensive slice of revenue.
- Subscription and buyout front-load costs and make them budgetable, fitting teams whose revenue is uncertain but who are sensitive to labor costs.
- Open-source licensing drops the licensing cost to zero while transferring the costs of maintenance, upgrades, and pitfalls onto the team itself.
- Source-code partnerships sit between commercial and open source, trading negotiation for control, with price and terms usually settled case by case.

When evaluating, it is worth converting each model into a total-cost curve over the project's lifecycle instead of only looking at what you pay today. Revenue-share costs explode when revenue explodes; buyout carries the highest sunk cost when the project fails. The shape of the curve says more about which model fits your revenue expectations than the size of the numbers.

Seeing the full spectrum has one more use: it gives discussions like "should we switch engines" an intermediate state. The licensing arrangement can change while the engine stays — from per-seat to revenue share, from closed source up to a source-code partnership — and many arguments that looked black-and-white dissolve by moving one notch along the spectrum.

### Revenue Share Stacked on the Revenue Structure

What most deserves careful arithmetic in a revenue-share model is the stacking effect. A mobile game's money first passes the platform channel cut, then the engine share, and only what remains is the team's gross profit. Each layer looks small on its own; stacked together, thin-margin categories become highly sensitive to every single layer.

That is why revenue-share negotiation and selection evaluation usually need finance in the room: against the distribution of expected revenue, compute the team's actual retention under different share schemes. One easily overlooked detail is which basis the share is computed on — gross revenue, net revenue after the platform cut, or the figure after deducting channel and advertising costs; under different bases, the same percentage produces very different amounts. Revenue share carries another implicit constraint: revenue reporting and audit clauses. The terms often include verification and filing obligations, and the team needs to keep its revenue books auditable from the very start — the later this is done, the more trouble it causes.

### Buyout, Subscription, and Front-Loaded Cost

Buyout and subscription models move cost out of revenue and into the budget. The upside is financial predictability — no renegotiation when revenue spikes; the downside is that the money is spent regardless of the product's fate. For projects with highly uncertain revenue, that is real risk; for projects with predictable revenue, it is instead a chance to lock in cost.

When evaluating such models, the common mistake is looking only at the license price and not the accompanying costs: whether version upgrades cost extra, the response tier of technical support, offline activation and license management as the team scales. For subscriptions, also check what the terms say about "stopping the subscription" — whether already-published works may continue in commercial use after renewal stops. These clauses differ widely between vendors and directly determine whether one subscription lapse turns into a business incident.

Matching a budget-based model has a team dimension as well: funding cadence and cash flow determine which kind of cost the team can afford. In a cash-tight, revenue-uncertain phase, a large license fee is a luxury; for a stable-revenue, long-running product, converting licensing into fixed spend is actually more worry-free. Financial state is not the backdrop of technical selection — it is a selection condition in itself.

### Open Source Is Not Zero Cost

An open-source engine's licensing cost is indeed near zero, but its total cost is not. Whatever the community edition lacks — dedicated platform support, commercial-grade technical support, a mature education and outsourcing ecosystem — gets paid back in labor cost. When you hit an engine defect there is no vendor backstop: fix it yourself or wait for the community; tracking mainline upgrades and maintaining an internal branch are ongoing investments.

The real value of open source is control: code you can read, modify, and hold long-term, with no fear of the vendor changing terms or dropping maintenance. For teams with engineering capability and long product cycles, that control is often worth more than the saved license fee. For teams short on labor that need to validate a market quickly, the completeness of a commercial engine's bundle actually costs less in total. So the choice between open source and commercial is in essence a choice between "spend on license fees or spend on labor" — whichever resource the team is short of, avoid paying in that currency.

### License Terms Can Change

The industry has seen a terms upheaval with huge impact: a major commercial engine announced per-device-install fees, adjusted the scheme after massive developer backlash, and then went through management changes and policy reversals. The lesson of that episode is not in the clause details but in one basic fact: there is no such thing as frozen license terms for a commercial engine — today's policy is not a promise about tomorrow.

This yields one pragmatic piece of advice for selection: plan for terms changes as a scenario that is bound to happen. The contingency plan covers several levels:

- Export capability for key assets and code — confirm how much you can take with you when you leave this engine.
- Sensitivity testing of the revenue structure against share changes — how far terms must deteriorate before hitting the team's bottom line.
- Version strategy for long-running projects: lock onto the old version and maintain it, or keep following new versions — estimate the cost of both paths.

The longer the terms stay stable, the more easily the team forgets this risk — and long-cycle projects are precisely the ones exposed to terms changes the longest. Spending one extra day reading the terms before signing beats spending a year migrating after they change.

### Hidden Clauses and Compliance Obligations

Beyond money, license terms hide a class of easily missed obligations. Some engines require the engine logo on the splash screen — especially common in free or low-price tiers — and removing it usually means upgrading to a pricier tier; some clauses include revenue disclosure obligations and transition arrangements after license termination; the use of the engine's trademarks and names has boundaries too, and how the engine is named in marketing materials is usually not a matter of writing whatever you like.

Each obligation on its own is light; the trouble is that they are scattered across the terms and nobody aggregates them at signing. A one-time task worth doing is distilling the license terms into an internal summary: how money is paid, what must be disclosed, what must be displayed, how termination transitions, how trademarks may be used. Shared across business, legal, and R&D, this summary lets any later commercial move involving the engine — financing disclosures, overseas publishing, onboarding a new channel — check its boundaries against it first.

### Engine Version Strategy for Long-Running Projects

Sooner or later a long-cycle project must answer one question: do we follow engine upgrades? Following costs adaptation and regression on every major-version upgrade, in exchange for new features, platform support, and security fixes; locking the version costs patching new platform OS support yourself, with defect fixes depending on whether the vendor still looks after the old version. Teams have made both paths work, and the precondition is thinking it through rather than defaulting to the latest version.

The common phased approach: follow the stable version during development, freeze the version during testing, and during operations absorb only the necessary security and platform-compatibility patches. Schedule upgrade windows in the gaps between releases rather than during content sprints, and assess the blast radius on a branch before upgrading. The engine's long-term support versions and upgrade decisions deserve dedicated archiving — three years later, when someone asks "why are we stuck on this version," the archive is more reliable than memory.

### Engine Choice Affects More Than the Client

The engine's tentacles reach far beyond "rendering and physics." In hiring, the engine determines the size and price of the talent pool — a niche engine means slow recruiting and having to grow your own. In outsourcing, art and audio vendors deliver assets per engine, and the maturity of the outsourcing ecosystem directly affects throughput. In platform coverage, console certification and per-platform feature support depend on the engine vendor's follow-through, and choosing an engine with weak platform support means leaving all the grind of platform adaptation to yourself. In tooling, the engine determines how the editor, profiling, and asset pipeline work, and the tools and processes the team accumulated over time may be invalidated wholesale.

These influences share one thing: none of them appears in the engine comparison table, yet they consume the team day after day. At selection time it is worth running a "beyond engine capability" checklist item by item: official support for target platforms, local talent supply, the outsourcing ecosystem, and the integration cost with the existing server and operations setup. This checklist often carries weight no lower than the engine's own feature comparison.

### Source Access and the Ability to Rescue Yourself

Closed-source engines run on trusting the vendor: file a ticket when something breaks, wait for the official patch. Most of the time this is more efficient — the vendor's fix quality and regression testing are usually better than the team's own hands. But a long-cycle project will almost certainly hit a moment when it cannot wait: a severe production defect, a new platform OS needing adaptation, or the vendor dropping support for an old version.

At that moment, whether the team has source access decides whether it rescues itself or waits. Buying source is not cheap, and getting the source is only the beginning — a team that can read it, modify it, and maintain an internal branch needs to afford several people who genuinely know the engine core. So source access is a "capability-for-cost" choice: without the matching engineering capability, source in hand is only a placebo. When evaluating, honestly asking "do we actually have anyone who can modify this engine" decides this option's fate more than asking how much the source costs.

### Lock-In and Migration Cost

Engine lock-in is among the deepest of all commercial dependencies. Asset formats, code structure, staff skills, toolchains, team habits — everything grows on the engine. Switching engines is not switching tools; it approaches a rewrite: assets must be re-imported and adjusted, code rewritten in another language or framework, the team retrained, every stage of the pipeline rebuilt. Teams that have completed engine migrations commonly estimate the migration cost as a considerable percentage of the original development effort, and new content nearly stalls during the migration.

Lock-in cannot be avoided, but it can be managed. Things worth doing: keep a clear boundary around the parts of core gameplay logic that are tightly coupled to the engine, for future portability; keep asset and config formats under your control, and verify export channels early; stay restrained with engine-proprietary features — the deeper you go, the narrower the exit. This is not being afraid to use engine capability; it is knowing clearly what you are borrowing and what you will pay when it comes due.

### Team Growth Changes the Negotiating Position

License terms are not fixed once signed; changes in team size and product count change the negotiating position. A small team with a single product pays standard terms, while a mid-size team with multiple product lines can often negotiate revenue thresholds, seat discounts, or source partnerships; conversely, the engine vendor's sales strategy shifts with the market, and the same clause has different negotiating room in different periods.

The mechanism worth establishing is periodic re-evaluation: at every new product line kickoff, every step-up in team size, and every contract renewal point, re-compare the current terms against the alternatives on the market. Re-evaluation does not mean migration — most of the time the conclusion is to keep using it — but that conclusion should be backed by data and alternatives. Whether you hold a seriously evaluated alternative is itself a bargaining chip, and this holds equally for commercial engines and for commercial licenses of every kind.

### Decision Focus for Teams of Different Sizes

The same licensing model means different things to teams of different sizes. For small teams and independent developers, labor is more expensive than license fees; an engine with a mature ecosystem, rich learning material, and easy hiring is usually the lowest total cost, and sensitivity to the license fee is actually low. Mid-to-large teams face a different set of problems: multi-project shared pipelines, integration with a self-built server stack, long-term license negotiation, and influence over the engine's direction of evolution. Teams at this scale often begin evaluating source partnerships or even in-house development, because every upgrade cadence and every policy change gets amplified across multiple product lines.

To judge where you stand, one simple question: are we shorter on people or on money, more afraid of license fees rising or of being held up by the vendor? The former leans toward commercial completeness, the latter toward control. Both answers are valid; what is bad is having no answer and deciding on reputation and demos.

### Common Pitfalls

- Choosing an engine by demo effect — demos are made by the vendor's most practiced teams and do not represent your own team's productivity.
- Counting only the license fee and not total cost — share stacking, the outsourcing ecosystem, and talent cultivation, the big-ticket items, never enter the model.
- Assuming commercial terms stay unchanged long-term, making no contingency for worsening terms, and falling into passivity the moment they change.
- Treating open source as zero cost, underestimating the labor of self-fixing defects and tracking upgrades.
- Overestimating your own ability to digest source — you buy it, no one can maintain it, the money is spent and the control never arrives.
- Archiving the license terms right after signing — hidden obligations stay scattered in the original text, and you discover you crossed the line long ago only when you need them.
- Deeply binding to engine-proprietary features and leaving no export channel, until migration cost balloons and staying put forever is the only option.

The business-model lens on engine selection has one core: treat "how does this deal split the money, who carries the risk, and what happens when terms change" as an input on par with technical metrics. Think these through, and the engine turns from a reputation topic into a computable business decision.

### Case Study: A Share-Basis Investigation After "5% Revenue Share, Yet 5.4 Million Extra Paid at Year End"

> The case is a demonstration example (not drawn from a real project), with numbers rounded for simplicity. It takes this page's "Licensing Models Are a Spectrum", "Revenue Share Stacked on the Revenue Structure", and "Hidden Clauses and Compliance Obligations" into deep water: the signed percentage was not high, yet the annual ledger does not add up — this is attribution for the revenue-share basis, and a complete unfolding of "stacking effects must be read as curves."

**Background and Symptoms**: The project grossed **360** million yuan annually, with a **30**% channel cut and a **5**% engine share; finance computed the team's retention as **65**% via "100 − 30 − 5." At year-end settlement, the engine vendor invoiced **18** million per the contract, while the team had reserved only **12.6** million under its own reading — a **5.4** million gap appearing out of nowhere; it also emerged that revenue reporting was **14** months overdue — the reporting basis and the contract basis had never been aligned since signing.

### Step One: Symptoms and Baselines — Lay the Two Share Ledgers Side by Side

Basis baseline: for the billing basis of the engine share, the contract says "revenue," without pinning down whether that means gross revenue or net revenue after the channel cut. On gross: 360 million × 5% = **18** million; on net: 360 million × 70% = 252 million, × 5% = **12.6** million. The same **5**%, and the two ledgers differ by **5.4** million.

Stacking baseline: the channel first takes 360 million × 30% = **108** million, leaving net revenue of 252 million; the team's retention on the gross basis is 252 − 18 = **234** million (**65**% of gross), and on the net basis 252 − 12.6 = **239.4** million (**66.5**% of gross) — one layer of basis difference, a **1.5** percentage-point gap in retention.

Compliance baseline: the revenue reporting and audit clauses require filing on the contract basis, while the team's statements were produced on the net-revenue basis — reporting was **14** months overdue, not from forgetting to file, but because the two bases did not match and filing was impossible.

### Step Three: Root Cause — The Percentage Was Negotiated Down, Nobody Watched the Basis

Negotiation attention went entirely to "5% or 3%," while the word "basis" passed through the contract as the single word "revenue." This page puts it plainly: under different bases, the same percentage produces very different amounts — the percentage is the explicit clause, the basis is the implicit clause; win the explicit negotiation and lose 5.4 million on the implicit one. The stacking effect was likewise viewed only as a single layer: a 30% channel and a 5% engine share each look small alone, but stacked on a thin-margin category every layer is a sensitivity item — the shape of the curve says more about which model fits your revenue expectations than the size of the numbers, and this curve was never drawn from start to finish.

### Step Four: Remediation and Backfill — Pin Down the Basis, Turn Obligations Into a Ledger

1. Pin the basis: sign a supplementary agreement with the engine vendor defining the share basis explicitly as "net revenue after the platform channel cut"; the annual share comes back from 18 million to **12.6** million.
2. Align reporting: finance, business, and legal share one basis summary, aligning the contract basis, filing basis, and statement basis across all three; overdue **14** months → **0**.
3. Terms summary: distill the license terms into an internal summary — how money is paid, what must be disclosed, what must be displayed, how termination transitions, how trademarks are used — five columns on one page, shared with business, legal, and R&D.
4. Contingencies on file: verify key-asset export capability once; test one bottom line for the revenue structure's sensitivity to share changes; estimate the cost of both version-strategy paths (lock the old version or follow the new).

Recheck ledger: annual share **18** million (gross basis) → **12.6** million (net basis); team retention **234** million → **239.4** million; reporting overdue **14** months → **0**; before the next renewal negotiation, the sensitivity bottom line and the alternatives list are in place.

### The Three Lessons of the Case

1. **The same percentage on a different basis is a different bill** — the percentage is the explicit clause, the basis is the implicit clause; win the explicit negotiation and still lose 5.4 million on the implicit one.
2. **Stacking effects must be read as curves, not single layers** — the channel cut and the engine share are each small alone, but stacked on a thin-margin category every layer is a sensitivity item.
3. **One extra day reading terms before signing beats one year migrating after they change** — financial state is not the backdrop of technical selection; it is a selection condition itself.
